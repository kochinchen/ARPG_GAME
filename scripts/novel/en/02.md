# Book Three: Fire

Floors 11 to 15

## Floor Eleven

You asked me today whether the old priest on floor ten was a bad man. As it happens, today we read floor eleven.

After the bells stopped, the sanctum felt very big. I walked past row after row of pews and came to a wall set with small wooden boxes, each with a kneeling cushion in front of it. These were confession boxes. The faithful dropped slips of paper in, and the priests took them out at night and read them. The locks were all broken, and the boxes were so stuffed that the slips had spilled out and covered the floor.

I squatted down and started picking them up.

Most of them were small things. I stole my neighbor's eggs. I lied to my husband. There were a lot of words on them, written with great care. I read them one at a time for nearly two hours. Villagers, miners, knights, whole lifetimes of small things were all here.

The further down the pile, the newer the slips, and what they said changed.

::lore.f11

I looked at that slip for a long time.

You've heard that voice too, so you know what this slip is talking about. What was the last thing your grandmother said to you? Only you and she know that, don't you? But it knows too.

You must remember that the voice is not your grandmother. It has only heard her. The people in this sanctum sang every day to remind it that it had walls, and it was down there listening. They sang for many years and prayed for many years, and they said their most private words to that crack, and it took in every one.

So was the old priest a bad man? I don't know. All I know is that his people came running to him in tears, saying they had heard a dead son calling, and he told them: this is the god. He believed it himself, because he preferred to.

This wasn't the first I'd heard of people who took that voice for a god. When I was seventeen I went down the mountain with Old Bow to collect the tax, and we passed through a village where they said they wouldn't pay the Well tax anymore. There was a god in the Well, they said, and paying the church would do more good. Old Bow didn't argue with them. On the way back I asked her what that meant. She said, "They don't want to be afraid anymore. What do you want from them?"

We were on the road for more than twenty days that trip, and we went through every village in the valley. It was the first time I learned what the world looked like.

The villages in the east were poor, with fields that were all stones. They couldn't pay in wheat, so they paid three skinny chickens. The ones in the west along the river were better off. They had a mill and a little tavern run by a widow, and someone asked Old Bow if she wanted to buy cloth. The crown's tax collectors had got there a month ahead of us and taken all the good things. They had horses, soldiers and printed forms. The village elder showed us a form. It said royal road tax, royal guard tax, royal birthday tax. The road had never been mended.

"So why do you still pay?" I asked.

The elder looked at the crest on my chest, then at the old sword at my waist, and didn't answer. Old Bow kicked me lightly.

That night we slept in a barn. I asked her why the Order didn't go and tell the crown that we were the ones guarding the Well and the money should come to us. She lay on the straw with one hand on her bow, and it was a long time before she spoke.

"When I was young, the crown sent five hundred sacks of wheat a year," she said. "Every few decades the seal comes loose and they remember us. The rest of the time we're just a lot of people eating. When enough people die, they send a little. When no one dies, they forget."

"And now?"

"Now they think the Well won't do anything," she said. "It hasn't done anything for a thousand years."

She rolled over. I thought she had gone to sleep. A while later she said, "That sanctum underground, no one's come up from it in a hundred years. And those people are still collecting tithes. Guess where the tithes go."

I thought she was joking at the time. Only when I got here did I see what was piled under the pews: copper coins, rings, children's silver bracelets, layer on layer, all sealed in wax.

It wasn't only money the wax had sealed in. Passing the next row of pews, I kicked a foot.

Not all of those people in long robes had died on floor ten. There were still dozens of them here, sitting in the pews with their heads bowed, as if waiting for the service to begin. The bells had stopped and they were still sitting there waiting for the service to begin. As I walked by, the whole front row stood up, and their hoods all turned toward me.

No one was singing anymore. That was worse than the singing.

They climbed over the pews toward me from every side, very fast, with their nails scraping on the wood. I got up on the pew backs and jumped from row to row, cutting as I ran. One grabbed my boot and pulled me down. I fell between two rows and couldn't move, and above me face after face came pressing down. The smell of wax made me want to vomit. I put fire on my sword and swung it around me. Their robes were all wax, and one touch set them alight.

The fire went up all at once and took the pews with it. I crawled out of it with a patch of my hair burned off. The whole row behind me was burning, and those people were still crawling toward me, with the fire on them.

I ran for the wall with the confession boxes and drank my last potion. The fire burned that row down quickly, and the copper coins and the rings all melted together. The slips of paper didn't burn at all.

That night I tied my hand to my sword and slept on a pew. When I woke I didn't get far before I found another chest.

::note.p01

Only those two sentences. The writing was very calm, almost as if the writer was smiling.

The Order lied. Lied about what? Was this what the old priest meant by "offerings"? Whoever wrote this knew, and still said: I would have come anyway.

I didn't really understand that at the time. If I found out I'd been deceived, I'd be furious. I'd drag those nineteen people out of the great hall and question them one by one.

I understand it now. In that person's place, I would have come too. I've since met plenty who came because they were deceived, and some who knew and came anyway. There are far fewer of the second kind, but they're the ones I'd rather sit down with over a drink.

Don't copy them. I'm only saying I'd like to drink with them.

## Floor Twelve

You got halfway through reading today and asked me what the old priest's diary said. It's further on, but first I'll tell you something. He was a man who worried about the price of candles. Remember that. Someday when you meet someone who frightens you, think about whether they ever worried about candles too. It won't make them any less frightening. You just won't go thinking they're a different kind of thing from you.

What comes next I have never told anyone.

Floor twelve was where the priests lived, a long row of small rooms. The one at the far end was bigger, with a slanted writing desk and a diary on it. I had already killed the man who wrote it, so I felt no great scruple about leafing through it. I wanted to know when he went mad.

I couldn't tell from which page he went mad. For the first few years he wrote about repairs to the sanctum and the new novices, and he worried a lot about the price of candles. His writing was small and honest. You could see he was the sort who'd get up at night to pull the blanket over a novice.

He wrote about a novice who loved to sing but was always off the note, and how he practised with the child for an extra quarter of an hour before morning prayers every day. He wrote that the child had learned it and that he was very happy, and had eaten an extra slice of bread that day. Further down he confessed that he shouldn't have been so greedy.

He wrote that people used to come to him every day. A child with a fever, wheat that wouldn't come in, a husband who hadn't come up from the mine, where people really go when they die. He answered them one by one, and when he had no answer, he knelt and prayed with them. He wrote: there are more and more I cannot answer. After that, the faithful got fewer. One year he wrote that a rope had snapped and the biggest bell had fallen, wrecking half the altar and cracking the flagstones. He sent for men to repair it. Three workmen came down. Two went back up.

That was the crack.

He wrote that it couldn't be mended, so they'd cover it with a cloth to keep the children from seeing it. A few pages later he wrote that at night, beside the crack, he had heard singing off the note, very like that novice, but the child had died of an illness two years before. He wrote: I knelt and listened for a while, and I was much comforted.

He wrote that one day a crowd of villagers burst into the sanctuary saying he was a liar, that prayer did no good, the wheat still wouldn't come in and children still died. He stood before the altar and reasoned with them for a long time. They wouldn't listen. They dragged the deacon who kept the door out into the yard and beat him, and broke one of his legs. That night he wrote: I shouted at them, go down the Well, all of you. I have never cursed anyone in my life. He wrote: my hands are still shaking. I know I cannot hold on much longer.

After that his handwriting got bigger. He wrote that down below there was a kingdom of heaven with no walls, where every one of us would meet again and no one would ever die. He wrote: don't let them ask anymore. If they ask, I can't answer. Let them sing. When they sing they aren't afraid, and they don't have to think. They only have to follow. He wrote: all I want is for everyone to be saved.

::lore.f12

That was it. There was no day he suddenly went mad. There was just a day he stopped asking, and stopped letting anyone else ask.

The corridor beside the diary room led to the novices' dormitory. I hadn't meant to go in. I went because I heard something.

It was a long room with two rows of very small beds. A dozen or so children in white robes sat on the beds, all of them nothing but bones. The robes had gone yellow, and they still wore them properly. They were singing. I stood in the doorway. It was the first song I'd heard in that sanctum with a beginning and an end, only there was nothing in their mouths but wind. The one nearest the door was half a beat behind the rest, and not quite on the note.

That must have been the child in the diary.

I backed away slowly. My heel caught a candle stand and it went over on the floor with a clang. The singing stopped. A dozen small heads turned toward me at once.

They jumped down from the beds. Their bare feet made no sound on the floor, and they ran very fast. They had no weapons. They just threw themselves at me and grabbed my arms and legs, and their teeth bit at the leather. They couldn't get through and they kept biting. They were too light. One shake sent two of them flying into the wall, where they came apart all over the floor, and a few tiny hand bones were still hanging on to my sleeve.

I didn't use fire. I knocked them apart with the flat of my sword, one at a time. I can't really tell you what difference the flat of the sword made.

When it was over the room was very quiet. The skull of the child who sang off the note rolled to my feet, its jaw still opening and closing. I squatted down and pressed it shut with my hand, and it opened again. I stood up and left. When I got to the door there was a very faint sound behind me. That child was still singing, one single note, and not on it.

I didn't look back.

I went back to the diary room, closed the diary and sat there a while, because the room had a chair. There was a crack in the floor here too, very thin, with red light much dimmer than the one in the main hall. I didn't pay it much attention.

Someone spoke.

"You've grown."

I jumped up with my sword already in my hand.

There was no one in the room. The voice was very low and a little nasal, because a horse had broken his nose once. I heard that voice every day from five to twelve. I couldn't have mistaken it.

"Master?"

I shouldn't have answered. You did the same thing at the Well. It called you, and you answered, and you leaned over.

"Then don't come back."

This time it was a little girl's voice.

I'll tell you who said that.

He began to change the year I was eleven.

Until then he came to the yard every day, one move after another, as if he wanted to cram them all into me. That spring he spent an afternoon behind the closed door of the Grand Master's room. When he came out, he started taking me down the mountain all the time.

He took me into a smithy. He bought nothing. He sat there watching the man work for a whole afternoon, and had me work the bellows for him. He took me to a farm, where he mended a fence for the people and told me to go and feed the chickens. He took me to a market. It was the first time I had seen people selling sweets and selling cloth, and people selling nothing at all, just sitting by the river fishing. He bought me a small piece of sugar. I didn't know how you ate it, so I held it in my hand until it melted.

I was bored to death, and I kept asking when we were going back to practise. He didn't answer. I thought he couldn't be bothered to teach me any more, so every time we got back I trained harder. I got up at night to steal his moves, beat the instructors one by one and ran to show him. He looked, and he wasn't pleased at all. He only looked at me. I couldn't read that look at the time.

The year I was twelve, he suddenly disappeared. I asked all over the mountain and no one knew where he had gone. Even the cook only swore once. On the morning of the fifth day he came back, without the stick, in his armor. He walked up to the third post and crouched down.

"I'm going down the Well today," he said.

I was twelve, and I wasn't stupid. I had seen those bare nails on the wall of the hall.

"That smithy down the mountain. I've settled it with him. Go down today and he'll take you."

"You taught me for seven years so I could go and shoe horses?"

"Yes."

"I can beat Big Lad. I blocked you twelve times. Take me with you."

"No."

"Why?"

He didn't answer. He looked down at my hand, gripping the wooden sword until the knuckles went white.

"Hand," he said.

I didn't let go.

"Leave this place," he said. "Don't wait for me. Forget me."

You have to understand, he wasn't my father. I had forgotten even the face of the man who was. But everything I had owned since I was five came from him: the wooden sword with the grip cut too thick, that small space inside the cloak, the half loaf on the post. Now he was telling me to forget all of it.

I wanted to say don't go. I wanted to say I'm scared. But he had never taught me any of that, and neither of us was the kind who says such things.

I said, "Then don't come back."

He looked at me for a while, patted my head, stood up and left. It was the first time he ever touched my head, and the last while he was alive. His hand was very big and smelled of rust.

That was the last thing I ever said to him. For ten years I told no one. I didn't even dare say it aloud when I was alone.

That year, on the day I got on the horse, I went to the third post very early. There was nothing on it. I stood there until noon.

I didn't go down the mountain. The smith sent someone up to ask, once. I told him to go home.

That thin little crack had just said it back, in my twelve-year-old voice, word for word.

I brought my sword down on the flagstone so hard my arm went numb. Sparks flew. The crack stayed just as thin. It didn't say anything else. It didn't need to.

Now I understood the old priest. If a voice can say the deepest thing in your heart back to you word for word, and you don't know what it is, you'll certainly take it for a god.

I walked out and didn't look back. I fought harder that day than on any other, and I took wounds without feeling them. I found out later that this was just what it wanted. It had been watching how I fought, all along. The harder I hit, the better it could see.

I remember two armoured skeletons and a crowd of skeleton mages in the corridor that day. I ought to have gone around them. I walked straight in. An ice spike hit my shoulder and I didn't even try to dodge. I crushed the first mage's head in my hand and rammed the second into the wall. An armoured one's hammer hit me in the back and I heard the bone go. I turned and cut it in half at the waist, then stamped on the head while it was still moving. I kept cutting until there was nothing left in the corridor to cut, and then I went on cutting the wall.

My hands were dripping blood. It wasn't anyone else's. The webs of my thumbs had split.

That night I sat in the stone ring with the old peddler beside me. It had been a long time since I'd asked him about anything but potions. I said, can you hear that voice too?

He looked up. I couldn't tell where he was looking.

"Twelve a bottle," he said.

His voice was nothing at all like the voice in that crack. But that night I didn't dare sleep with my back to him.

## Floor Thirteen

I spent a long time on this floor. A stretch under the sanctum was all burial chambers for the priests of past generations, and every stone coffin had a figure lying on it, carved in stone. As I walked by, the lids began to move.

The fanatics hid in the coffins. I don't know how long they had been hiding. The first lid came off and a ball of blood-red fire hit me square in the chest and burned a hole in my leather. The second and the third stood up in their coffins throwing fire at me, and the coffin stone stopped my arrows for them. I ran forward, stepped on something soft, and another one reached up from a coffin in the floor and grabbed me.

I jumped onto the stone coffins and went from one to the next, cutting downward with every jump. Their heads rolled out of their hoods, and some of the heads were still young. Fire bolts burst at my feet and the soles of my boots got hot. As I jumped for the last one its lid was pushed open from inside. I missed my footing and fell into the coffin, on top of a dried-up corpse in priest's robes. The corpse opened its eyes and wrapped its arms around me, with a grip like tongs, and the coffin lid thumped shut again.

Total dark. I was held in a dead man's arms with my nose against his face, and there was no air in the coffin. Outside, someone was laughing, or singing.

My arms were pinned and I couldn't raise my sword, so all I could do was use fire. Fire in a space that small burned him and it burned me, and the coffin filled up with smoke and the stink of burning. His arms finally let go. I pushed the lid up with my back and scrambled out, gulping air, with my hair and eyelashes gone. Two more believers were standing outside holding up candles, looking at me.

I stood up and cut them down. I don't remember what I was thinking.

After I drank a potion I sat on a bench for a while. The next day I noticed that my eyelashes had already grown back. It had only been a day.

The sanctum's library was on floor thirteen. Half the shelves had fallen, but the books were all still there.

I stayed in the library two or three days, I think. By then I couldn't really tell the days apart. I wanted to know what exactly these people had known. In the most prominent place on the shelves was a very thick leather-bound book of scripture, and it was very good, all about light and humility and how to save on candle oil.

But the binding was loose in one place. I counted the page numbers, and one page was missing.

It took me a long time to find it under a bookshelf, torn out, crumpled into a ball and stuffed in a crack between the boards. Whoever tore it out probably didn't dare burn it or keep it, so they stuffed it in there.

::lore.f13

**The Eye.**

I smoothed the page flat, weighed it down with a book, and sat on the floor thinking.

It hath no body. It only learneth.

So people had known long ago. The elders knew that voice was no god. They gave it a name, wrote it into the scripture, and told those who came after that it was only an Eye that learns. And then someone tore that page out.

Why?

I didn't understand then. I can guess now. It was probably when the faithful began to hear their dead relatives talking. Someone thought it would hurt less to let them believe it was a god than to tell them it was only an Eye.

That's the sort of person who tore out this page. Very kind-hearted, and very worried about the price of candles.

Now you know why I'm showing you all sixty-nine pages.

There was something else in the library that kept following me. It was a lump of flesh floating in midair, with a wrinkled surface like a brain. It wasn't much for hitting people. It just came close. When it came close your head started to hurt, and then you'd suddenly lose track of what you had been about to do. Once I was squatting in front of a bookshelf with a book in my hand and couldn't remember why I had picked it up. I stood to go and didn't know which way to go. I couldn't even remember how many steps I had walked. I went cold all over with fear. That number was the only thing I had, and now even that had been taken.

I looked down at my sleeve. There in charcoal was a number: one thousand three hundred and six.

I stared at the sleeve for a good while before I remembered what it was. Then I turned around and split the lump of flesh with one stroke.

When you need to remember something, write it down. Anyone can get into a head.

That lump wasn't the only one. In the corridor on the far side of the library there were five or six of them, floating between the shelves. My head started to hurt, as if someone were knocking on it from inside. I cut down the first one, turned around, and forgot where the second one was. I stood there holding a sword without knowing why I was holding a sword. Something rammed into me from behind. It was half a skeleton with a broken shelf board. It took me a dozen blows or more to smash it. When I finished I forgot again what I'd been doing, looked down, and there on my sleeve was that number.

One thousand three hundred and six.

I took the charcoal and wrote three words on the back of my hand: KILL THE LUMP. Then I'd look up, see a lump and cut it, look down, see the words and carry on. That's how I cleared the corridor. What are you laughing at? It worked.

I got that trick from Two-Fingers.

His Art-hall was in the most out-of-the-way room on the mountain, with walls burned black. When he taught us to throw fire he always made us write down first what we meant to do, because once the fire's in your hand, you go stupid. I asked him once how he lost his fingers. He said that when he was young he was an apprentice in a forge in the capital, and he handled a kind of warm stone. He got careless grinding it to powder, and the fingers weren't his anymore. He held up what was left of the hand to show me. There was a patch of dark hard skin on the stump.

"What kind of stone was it?"

He put the hand down and asked if I had finished my exercises.

It only occurred to me later that he was afraid of the fire he taught us, too. Every time he finished throwing it, he spent a long time rubbing lard into his hand.

I also found the song in the library. Not the floor eight kind. This was a true chant, written in a thin little songbook, with a beginning and an end. I can't really read music. I sat on the floor and hummed along with the marks once, and the second time I sang it out loud. I sang it very badly. My voice has never been any good.

When I reached the last note, the thing that had been sounding under my feet all this time stopped for a moment. Just for a moment.

I kept the songbook too.

I worked out something else that day. It knew what I had said because it was listening when I said it. I was twelve, standing in a yard up on the mountain, two days' ride from here. Even then it had reached that far.

Since that day I have rarely talked to myself. I still don't.

## Floor Fourteen

At the very bottom of the sanctum was a great door with seven crossbars, each as thick as my waist. The door stood wide open and the bars lay on the ground.

The small room beside it was the doorkeeper's. On the table was a door log, very clearly kept: what hour, who went in, who went out. I turned to the last entry.

::lore.f14

Just that one line, with nothing under it.

I looked at the bars. They had been opened from the inside, and none were broken. Someone got an order, wrote up his log, picked up his keys, and lifted them one at a time, very dutifully. He probably stood right here afterward, waiting to see the things the priests had spoken of come up and kneel.

I saw him too. He was in the corridor outside the room, in a doorkeeper's uniform with the keys still hanging at his waist, and there was only the top half of him. Where the bottom half had gone I don't know. There was a long drag mark on the floor, heading for the door. He had crawled there on his hands. He had reached the door and stretched out a hand toward one of the fallen bars, and the hand had stopped there.

As I went through the great door, I felt air blowing up from below for the first time.

Beyond the door was a very wide slope, and something was waiting in the dark. The things that came up had not knelt. They were a kind I had never seen. Some were like hairless wolves covered in bone spikes, on all fours. Some were like lizards, with blood-red scales that opened up one by one. They had probably been waiting here a long time for someone to come down from above.

I had barely set foot on the slope when the first bone-spike hound jumped off the side wall and knocked me flat. The spikes went through my leather into my shoulder and ribs, and every spike on it was barbed. It lay on top of me as we slid down the slope, all the way, with the rest of them chasing behind. I grabbed its neck bone, shoved its head down, and rode it like a sled. By the bottom of the slope half its face had been ground off. I rolled clear, and the barbs tore out several pieces of me.

Blood-scale lizards closed in from all around. They didn't hurry to spring. They made a ring and slowly circled. I turned slowly too, with my sword point out. The meat of my belly was laid open and the blood kept running. I raised a potion bottle, just lifting my hand, and one of them jumped and bit my wrist. The bottle flew off and smashed on the rocks.

I stared at that red puddle. That was twelve gold coins.

I suddenly got very angry. I let go of my sword, took hold of the one biting my wrist with both hands, and forced its jaws apart until they went crack. Then I picked up my sword, turned the fire up as far as it would go, and walked at them, cursing as I went. I used every filthy word I knew. When it was over I sat among the bodies, drank another bottle, counted my gold, and it came to me that every one of those words had come from Mavis, and that she still owed me twelve.

Sometimes I think what got me that deep wasn't the Ward-hall. It was Mavis.

From here on down, the monsters weren't just dead people. Some were covered in shell, some dragged egg sacs, and some floated in the air and were nothing but one eye. They weren't made from people. There were a lot of them, they were hungry, and they weren't afraid of me. Later I found out whose children they were. At the time all I knew was that the road kept getting harder and each fight took longer than the last.

One side chapel had been taken over by something. It was the only way down, and there was no going around it.

It was bigger than a cow. It had a spider's legs, and behind it dragged a huge grey-white belly bulging with egg sacs, and it gave birth as it crawled. As soon as I went in it turned toward me, with its whole body writhing. I slashed the first sac open and the little things inside jumped out and bit my legs. If they couldn't bite they climbed, and got inside my armor. I cut the second one and slime sprayed in my eyes, stinging so badly I couldn't open them. I backed up, stepped on an egg that hadn't burst yet, and slipped.

It came down on top of me.

That belly was wet and heavy, hundreds of pounds, and it covered me completely. My face was pressed against its skin. Through the skin I could feel things moving inside, butting at my nose. I couldn't breathe and I couldn't raise my sword. I felt for the dagger at my waist with my left hand, found it, and stabbed upward. The belly split, and something hot came down like a bucket being emptied and poured into my mouth and nose. I choked until I nearly died, stabbing and climbing at the same time, and I crawled out through the hole, wrapped in all those things that hadn't hatched.

I stood up. It wasn't dead yet. It turned and spat slime at me and glued my feet down. I used fire. All the eggs in the chapel burned at once, with a sound like hundreds of little stones bursting together. I burned the slime off my boots, burned a layer of skin off my calves too, and walked out through the fire.

Outside I knelt and threw up for a long time. What I threw up was still moving. I stamped on it.

When I came out I was covered in slime and my nose was full of that smell. It didn't go away for days.

Worse were the ones with one eye. They didn't do much fighting. They just floated along behind me at a distance and kept looking. When I turned around, they stopped. When I walked, they walked. I shot one down with an arrow and went over. It really was just an eyeball, its pupil still shrinking, and in the pupil was a tiny person with red hair.

I thought of that torn page of scripture and stamped the eyeball to pulp. The next day three of them were following me.

Later I opened a chest, the best one on these floors: a good leather jerkin and a pair of boots. I finally had boots to change into. At the bottom of the chest was a sheet of paper, very good paper, the kind the Order uses.

::note.p09

The handwriting was beautiful. Whoever wrote this must have grown up in the great hall on the mountain too, had his hand rapped by the instructors too, and had a nameplate on that wall.

"I should have liked to be a person."

I walked a long way in those new boots with that line going around in my head. I asked myself what I would have liked to be, and I thought for a long time and came up with nothing. From the age of five, people had told me what I was going to do when I grew up, and I had never thought of anything else. You can herd sheep. You know which hillside's grass they like, and you say you're going to keep a hundred someday. At twenty-two I couldn't have told you even that much.

The only time I ever danced was when I was eighteen.

That year at harvest, Mavis dragged me down the mountain, saying there was a festival in the village. The rules forbade it, so we went over the wall. It was my first time going over a wall. I didn't know where to put my hands or feet, and she sat on top laughing at me. The village below had lit torches. Someone was playing a fiddle, there was roast mutton, and everyone was dancing in a ring. I stood off to the side in my old clothes. My hand kept wanting to rest on my sword hilt, but I hadn't brought my sword, so the hand just hung there, feeling foolish.

Mavis jumped into the ring. She danced terribly, even worse than she laughed. The miller's son took her hands and spun her around. She laughed until she couldn't breathe and stepped on his feet several times.

A tall boy came over and asked if I wanted to dance. I said I didn't know how. He said it was easy, give me your hand. I looked at his hand. It was clean, with no calluses. I held mine out and he took it and stopped for a moment. He had probably never felt a girl with a hand like that. But he pulled me in anyway.

I couldn't find the beat. I stood too straight and my elbow kept going tight to my ribs. He laughed and pulled my arm away, and I drew it back again. On the third time around, I suddenly noticed how loud it was and how hot. Everyone was laughing, someone was shouting something at me, and I was laughing too.

Just for that little while.

Then the village church struck four. If we didn't go we wouldn't make it by dawn, so we ran all the way back up the mountain. Mavis talked about the miller's son the whole way, and I didn't really listen. I stood at the third post just as it got light, and the instructor already had his stick. Left foot forward, elbow tight to my ribs, legs still shaking. I told myself it was because I had walked too far.

I saw the miller's son again four years later, on the night at the mill. He wasn't a person anymore. I cut his knees.

The year I was sixteen, the recruiting officer came up the mountain.

He wore new clothes in the crown's colours and brought two clerks, and he stood in the yard with a handkerchief over his nose. He watched us stand at the posts for a while, and then he told the Grand Master that His Majesty was at war in the east and needed men, and since the Order was sitting idle, it might as well do its share. The Grand Master said we guarded the Well. The officer said, what is there to guard about a well?

He picked three. Big Lad and two others older than me. He looked at me too. By then I could already outfight a lot of men, and I stood very straight. He asked the Grand Master, "What about this one?"

The Grand Master glanced at me and said, "This one stays."

That afternoon Big Lad came to see me. His nose was still crooked, my doing. He had packed a small bundle, and he stood by the third post looking a bit embarrassed.

"I'm off," he said.

I said I knew.

"Actually," he said, rubbing his nose, "I'm glad."

I glared at him.

"At least some people come back from a war," he said.

He left. I heard he died in the east. I also heard he became a squad leader and got married. I don't know which is true. I thought he was a deserter at the time, and I looked down on him for it.

Why the Grand Master kept me back, I didn't understand until floor twenty-two.

I was sent to be a wall too, and at the time I was very pleased about it.

Further down, the road started to get hot.

## Floor Fifteen

The ground was scorching, and the air had a burnt smell of iron and sulfur. At the end of the sanctum's last stairway, the stone turned to raw rock. Wooden scaffolds were braced against it, level on level, with cages and pulleys and long rusted rails. This place was older than the sanctum.

The first body I saw was a miner with a pick still in his hand. So was the second. All along the way the bodies held picks, and they had all fallen in the same direction. They had all been running for the way out.

There were footprints in the ash.

I squatted down to look. The miners' boot prints were all old, covered with a layer of ash. But one line of prints was new. They were bare feet with very even strides, walking very straight, in no hurry at all. The toes were long, and the feet were a little bigger than mine.

The prints went on down the mine tunnel. He had got there before me.

I thought of that fork on floor eight. I got up, ran along the prints for a while and shouted a couple of times. The rock walls swallowed the echo right away. The prints came to the edge of some lava and kept going, right along the side of it, so close that the ash next to them had baked into hard lumps. The Southerner's soles must have been burned through by then. He hadn't stopped, and he hadn't gone around.

I saw them a few more times afterward, on floors sixteen and seventeen. I kept thinking I would catch up with him.

An ore cart lay overturned by the road, its load spilled everywhere.

Guess what it was.

Warm stones, the colour of coals about to go out, some as big as a fist and some as small as a fingernail. The same kind that was on my sword. To be honest, my first thought wasn't fear. It was "Look how many." I squatted down and picked out the three biggest.

A ledger was nailed up in a wooden shed.

Six men. What a tidy entry. I thought of the village elder's ledger on floor two, the sacks of wheat, the sheep.

Then the ground shook. It wasn't the knocking I had heard on floor four. This one threw me flat. All three stones rolled away and I crawled after them and got them back. The cages on the scaffolds began to sway.

I crawled to the edge of the cliff and looked down.

Below was a sea of lava, and in the middle of it was a mountain.

The mountain stood up.

I drew him very big in that picture on floor eleven, and he was bigger than that. He was a heap of rock with fire running through the cracks. A great hammer was chained to one arm. That hammer had been made for a man's hand, and on him it looked ridiculously small, like a nail. Every step he took left a pool of lava behind it that took a long time to go dark.

He didn't speak either. Not one of these guardians ever spoke to me while they were alive.

The first time, I never even touched his knee.

I ran down the scaffolds, planning to jump onto him from above and put my sword in the top of his head. The instructors had taught us how to fight big things. I was halfway down when, without even looking, he brought both fists down on the ground. The whole scaffold whipped like a shaken rope. I went up in the air with the timber and saw that red below me rushing up.

I fell in the lava.

It didn't hurt for long. All I remember is the leather catching fire first, and then I had no legs.

I woke up screaming in the stone ring. My legs were there, but I felt them and felt them, for a long time. The third stroke went on my sleeve. When the old peddler held out a bottle, my hand shook too much to take it.

The second time, I went along the ground. Under the cliff was a trodden path, all ash. I kept to the edge and worked around to his side. Everywhere he had walked there was lava, pool after pool, red going to white, each taking a long time to go dark. I cut at his ankle. Sparks flew, it was like hitting an anvil, and my arm went numb to the shoulder. Only then did he look down at me.

The vents on his body opened. On his shoulders and back, a row of holes spat molten rock all at once. It flew very high, trailing smoke across the air, and slowly came down toward me. I ran left and it came down on the left. I ran right, and a crack suddenly shot along the ground and a whole wall of fire stood up at my feet and swallowed my left leg. Trouser, boot and flesh all caught at once. I threw myself out and rolled in the ash. The fire went out, but the skin on that leg wasn't skin anymore.

I drank a potion, got up and cut again. Seven or eight strokes opened a little crack. The light from it shone on my face, very hot. He raised his fist. I rolled aside, the ground jumped, and I went dizzy for a moment, only for a moment. When the next fist came down I couldn't even see it clearly.

Fourth stroke.

Before the third time, I sat in the stone ring for a long while, thinking about what this mountain was actually doing. He wasn't fast and he wasn't clever. He just walked and hit and burned. The places he had walked kept getting more, and the places I could stand kept getting fewer. Time was on his side. He didn't hate me. He hardly noticed me. It didn't feel like being attacked. It felt like rain.

But rain doesn't pick where it falls. I remembered the moment he stood up. When the ground shook, I had those three stones in my hand. And every time since, wherever I was, he had always ended up walking toward that overturned cart.

He wasn't chasing me. He was chasing the stones.

I thought again about the line in the ledger: keep digging. The miners had dug for who knows how many years, and the rails ran down the cliff face one below the other. The deepest one crossed a chasm on a wooden bridge to the mouth of a shaft on the far side. The bridge was built for carts, with the rails nailed to the timbers. The second time, going the long way round, I had dropped a pebble off it and counted to seven before I heard it land.

The third time I bought six bottles and spent all my money. I didn't go looking for him. I went to the bridge first. Using my sword as a pry bar, I worked the posts in the middle span loose one by one and pulled half the iron spikes. It took a long time, and I wore through my gloves. Far off in the lava he walked back and forth and paid me no attention.

Then I went back to the cart and put every stone scattered on the ground into my pack, dozens of them, big and small. It was so heavy I couldn't straighten up, and it was hot on my back like a stove full of coals.

The ground shook.

I turned and ran. It was the longest I have ever run. Down along the rails, through scaffold after scaffold, and him behind me, not fast, but one of his steps was twenty of mine. The scaffolds he passed went down like paper, and pulleys and baskets crashed down around me. His vents opened again and molten rock fell out of the sky. I didn't dodge anymore. I just ran, and if it fell on my left I veered a little right. A gob of it landed on my pack and burned a hole through with a hiss. Stones dropped out of the hole, one after another all along the way, glowing in the ash. He followed the stones and didn't miss a single one.

I ran onto the bridge. The timbers groaned under my feet. I ran over the loosened span without daring to look down, and didn't turn round until I reached the mouth of the shaft on the other side.

He came to the end of the bridge and stopped for a moment.

That bridge could hold a cart. It could hold miners for hundreds of years. It couldn't hold a mountain. He lifted his foot and stepped on. At the first step the bridge groaned. At the second the rails bent. The third came down on the span I had loosened, and the posts snapped one after another with a sound like someone breaking a bundle of firewood.

The bridge fell, and he fell with it.

But the chasm wasn't as wide as I'd thought. He was too big. Halfway down he stuck, his shoulders jammed against the rock on either side and his arms pinned to his body, only his chest and head showing above, like a man standing in a well too small for him. The fire on him ran down the rock walls and into the dark, and I never heard it hit bottom.

I jumped onto his shoulder. He couldn't pull his arms free. All he could do was twist, grinding his whole body against the chasm walls while rubble poured down, and every lurch nearly threw me off. I drove my sword into a crack in the rock and climbed down one hold at a time. The rock burned through my gloves and I didn't notice. I kept climbing toward his chest, where the cracks were brightest. I had seen that kind of brightness before, on my own sword. I stood on his ribs with the heat so strong I could hardly open my eyes. I had one potion left and I didn't drink it. I poured all the heat of all my stones into the blade, took the hilt in both hands, and drove it in with my whole weight.

When it went in, I actually felt good. It was as if the two fires knew each other.

The mountain slowly went dark. It broke apart piece by piece, starting at the chest, and fell away into the chasm. I slid down with it holding my sword, caught a length of broken rail, climbed back to the mouth of the shaft and collapsed in the ash. The hammer hung on the rock wall with its head half melted. A thread of white smoke rose out of the chasm. I lay face down without the strength to lift my head.
