# Epilogue: The Way Above

## The Bottom

::panel.f35-1

This is the best picture I ever drew.

They're clearer in the picture than they were to me. All I saw then was two very faint lights, and I was crying the whole time, so I couldn't see much of anything. But they were holding hands. Of that I'm certain.

When they began to rise, light came down from above. Of that I'm certain too.

I looked up. That arm had withered, the temple floor had cracked, the fog in the fortress had cleared, and all thirty-five floors had opened up, one after another, all the way to a tiny white dot at the top. That was the sky. For the first time in a thousand years, light had reached this place, one thin line of it, with dust floating inside. I reached out and touched it. It had no warmth.

Since I came down the Well, it was the first thing I had touched that wasn't warm. I kept my hand there. I didn't want to take it away.

They rose very slowly. After a while I couldn't tell which was the light and which was them.

I knelt there. I didn't want to do anything except go on kneeling. There was no one waiting for me anyway.

A paper in my coat jabbed me in the ribs.

**Go home, if it let thee. Someone ought to.**

On the back was one very ugly word: Yes.

I stood up.

I picked up my own sword and went over to the shield. It was heavier than I expected. I tied it on my back with rope. The great sword was stuck in the ground. The hard crust had dropped off it too, and what was left was a real sword, still as tall as my shoulder. My left arm was still tied to my belt, so I pulled with my right, and the first time it wouldn't budge. I rested and tried again. When it came out, it made that same clear ring.

She didn't need these two things any more, so why take them? Because if I left them there, someday someone would come down, find them, hang them in a great hall and build a new Order.

I tied the great sword to my belt and started back, dragging it along the ground.

The stairs were right there.

Right where I had come down, step after step, not one of them fallen. **The way above is given back to you.** I stepped onto the first one and began to count.

One.

When I reached the first stone ring, I stopped.

The carved lines were dark. I stepped in and they didn't light. My wounds didn't close. I stood there a moment, walked out and walked back in. Nothing changed.

The old peddler's cloth was still spread beside it, with the bottles in their row. The red in the bottles had all turned grey. That ragged robe lay across a stone, empty. I lifted it on the point of my sword. There was nothing in it. A few gold coins fell out.

That was my money, I thought later, twelve a bottle. I bought a great many bottles.

Every ring on the way up was the same, an empty robe and dark lines. On one floor the coins under the robe were piled higher than my head. Some were so old I didn't know the stamp, with the faces of kings from hundreds of years back. What did the Well want with gold? I thought about that for a long time. Probably nothing. It just handed you something and took what you had on you, and that was all.

I didn't take a single coin.

Then something occurred to me, and I froze.

The rings had gone dark. So now, if I missed a stair, if a stone came down, if my leg gave out, I would die. Really die, and never draw another breath. In three years down the Well I had died thirteen times, and that was the first time I had been afraid of dying.

I gripped the wall and walked very carefully. You might find that funny. I killed the thing at the bottom of the Well, and then I went up the stairs holding on to the wall like an old lady.

When I was only a few dozen steps up, I had looked back once. That thing had begun to harden, like cooling fat. The eyes were all shut, some not quite tightly, showing a grey slit. I looked one last time and then I stopped looking. It had looked at me long enough.

That walk took a long time. I won't take you through it floor by floor. Every floor was quiet. The monsters had dropped where they stood. Bones were only bones, and shells were only shells. There was a cold draught in the air, the first in a very long time.

The thirtieth floor. The people on the stairs were still lying there. I went very slowly, finding the gaps, and didn't step on any of them. The throne lay on its side. The wounds on those wings had grown nothing more.

The twenty-ninth floor. The little things in the hall had all stopped moving. I looked around for the small one that had followed me. I didn't find it.

The twenty-eighth floor. Nothing had grown in the burned flower patch. On the chapel wall, the prayer the Queen had cut was still there. I added a few words beneath it with the point of my knife: She has gone, with her. I had meant to cut "Someone remembers" and got halfway, and then I felt that wasn't a thing to say to a wall.

The twenty-sixth floor. I went back to the small room with the low door. The two beds were still pushed together. I took the wooden sword from against the wall and laid it on the beds, in the middle, across the two of them.

The twenty-fifth floor, the Queen's room. I hung the half-burned tapestry back on the wall, thought about it, and took it down again. You've seen it. It hangs behind my door now.

The twenty-fourth floor, the gatehouse. I climbed up and stood in front of that wall. Under my master's line, that space of exactly the right size was still there. I took out the little knife from the third floor. My hand was shaking badly, so I cut slowly. What I cut was:

The gate can stand empty.

On all that wall, that's the only line that's different. When I had finished I looked at it. The writing was ugly, even uglier than his.

The twenty-third floor. I took the short wooden sword down from my master's room. I still don't know whose it was. It's in my cupboard. You've played with it.

The twenty-first floor, that corner in the inner city. Four suits of armour lay on the ground in single file, all fallen the same way. The leader's hand was still raised.

I said the password to them. No one answered. I stood there a while and walked through.

The twentieth floor, outside the gate. My master's sword was still standing in the crack between the flagstones, very straight. I sat beside it all night. I didn't say anything to him. Everything had been said. When the light came into the fog, I stood and gave him the salute of the Ward-hall. Elbow very low.

The nineteenth floor. All the hands on that rock face had let go, fingers open, like a crowd of people asleep.

I found the hand with the copper ring. It had let go too, and the drawing of the house by the sea had fallen to the ground. I picked up the drawing and pulled off the ring. It came off very easily.

Later I took that ring to the sea. It was the only long trip I ever made after I stayed at the Well, sixteen days on foot.

I had kept those three papers all along. The inn printed on the handbill was still there, smaller than in the drawing, with half its sign gone and a dog in the yard that barked and barked at me. A woman was washing dishes out the back. She was older than I had expected, with very red hands and hair only to her shoulders.

I asked if she had a husband, with part of his little finger missing, a great talker.

She paused, then went on washing. "Dead," she said. "The second year after he left, I started telling people he was dead. My husband now is on the boats."

I put the ring down beside the sink. It was copper, and it had gone green.

She looked at it for a long time, with water dripping off her hands onto the floor. Then she asked me, "Did he owe you money?"

I thought about it. I took that gold coin out of my boot, the one I had won from him, and put that beside the sink too.

"He paid," I said.

She picked up the ring and the coin, dried them, put them in her apron pocket and went back to the dishes. I stood there a moment, not knowing what else to say. She didn't look up. When I reached the gate of the yard, she said behind me, "He told you I was beautiful, didn't he?"

I said yes. The most beautiful in town.

She laughed a little, an ugly laugh, and turned away.

I walked sixteen days back. That dog in the yard barked until I was out of sight.

The kind of dog he wanted, the kind that doesn't bark, probably doesn't exist.

The eighteenth floor, the corridor outside the forgemaster's workroom. There had been nothing there when I came down. Now there was a body, sitting against the wall.

I knew that armour. Our crest, two sizes too big for her. She had altered it from someone else's.

I stopped. It was a long time before I walked over.

She was very short. She never did grow. One leg was gone, and there was still a rope tied at the knee. She must have tied it herself. She had got this far, further than plenty of people with proper training. I don't know how she did it. She couldn't even stand at a post properly. The things on that road had left me covered in wounds. She must have snuck past them. That was what she was best at.

Her hands lay on her belly, with her fingers curled around a small cloth bag. I crouched and opened it.

Twelve gold coins.

I crouched there, I don't know how long. She really had gone and stolen them, or else she had gone through the dead on the way down. Only one person in the whole world would carry twelve gold coins down the Well to the eighteenth floor just to pay me back. She must have come down a year or more after me.

She could have gone over the wall at fifteen.

A die lay on the ground by the pouch, its corners worn round, and I picked it up without thinking. At the bottom of the pouch was a piece of paper, folded very small. I knew it. It was the IOU from when we were fourteen. I had made her write it when she lost the twelfth coin. On the front it said:

"Owed to the red one, twelve. Mavis."

The writing looked like a chicken had walked across it. I turned it over. The back was full, written in charcoal, one line to a floor, getting smaller and smaller until it was squeezed along the edge. She never wrote anything. She gave her Way-hall reports out loud, and got them wrong.

"Floor one. The Grand Master asked how many floors I could last. I said I'm only going to pay a debt. He cried. You don't know how old he is now."

"Floor two. All the dead things have the same slanted wound, low left to high right. I'd know it with my eyes shut. I'm following the wounds."

"Floor three. A dead man in a good pair of boots. You didn't take them. Neither did I. Too big."

"Floor five. A big one kneeling there. Your doing, wasn't it. I laughed for a long time, and then for some reason I wasn't laughing."

"Floor eight. Someone's been throwing dice on the floor. Two people. You've made a friend. I sulked for half a day."

"Floor eleven. Died. Woke up in the ring and threw up everywhere. There's an old peddler next to it. Twelve a bottle, he says. I said these twelve are spoken for. He said it again. It's all he says."

"Floor fourteen. Died again. This time I woke up standing very straight with my elbow against my ribs. I've never stood right in my life. It scared me to death. Also, it took me a long while to remember what I came down here for."

"Floor sixteen. The track's broken. You jumped it, didn't you, you idiot. The third hole on the right goes through. I tried it. It's hot. I've marked it. You'll be very tired when you come back, so go this way."

"Floor eighteen. Leg's gone. I know what to do. Die once and it grows back. But next time I wake up I'll probably stand straighter, and remember less."

"I won't. These twelve are the one thing I've owed you all my life, and I mean to remember them."

"So this far is fine. I haven't sat this comfortably in years."

"The money's here. We're square. Don't hang a plaque for me. Don't carve my name either. Remember it or don't, it makes no difference, you never listen to anyone anyway."

"That night I said let's go, and you told me I was mad. Go and see the sea by yourself, and tell me about it after. You can lie."

"The Well will go quiet. You're that good at standing. When it does, you can go downstairs. You know the rest of that line."

The writing stopped there.

This paper isn't one of the sixty-nine pages. It was written to me, and I have never shown it to anyone.

Ten floors before, on the wall of a small chapel, I had read something else. **Let me not end.** The Queen was afraid paper would rot, so she cut every stroke over and over. This paper was in charcoal. My finger touched it and a patch of it smeared away.

I couldn't cry this time. I just sat down beside her against the wall, my shoulder touching her shoulder, the way we did when we were worn out from standing at the posts. She had once asked me what I said to my master the day he left, and I never answered. Now I did.

"I told him, then don't come back."

No one said anything.

I put the coins back in her hand. She owed me, and she had paid, but I didn't want them. I covered her with rubble from the corridor. It took a long time. My left hand had only just started to move, and there was no strength in it. The paper I kept. Last of all I took off my boots, their soles nearly burned through, put on the socks she had given me and pulled the boots back on. I had never worn them. I had been saving them till now.

Those socks were too small, and there was a hole in the heel.

I took floor seventeen slowly. On floor sixteen I came to that broken track.

The lava had gone dark. A black crust lay over it, with red showing underneath. Twenty paces across. There was no cart this time, there was no strength in my left hand, and I had a shield on my back. The stone rings were dark too, so if I fell, I fell. I stood at the break for a long time, until my legs started shaking.

Then I saw a line of charcoal on the rock wall to my right, a crooked arrow.

The third hole.

It was very low, and I had to crawl. The shield stuck twice, so I untied it and dragged it behind me on the rope, and the great sword too. She was very short. That hole fit her and it didn't fit me. In the second half I could walk bent over, with the lava right underneath and the rock so hot it smoked. My boots had burned through altogether, and there was nothing between me and the rock but those socks. She had darned them badly, patch on patch, thick as a sole. Except for the hole in the heel, which burned at every step. The scar is still there, the exact size of that hole.

On the wall at the far end was a line of charcoal.

"Three corners, four hundred and twenty paces, no water. Report ends."

On a stone under that report sat a die, its corners worn round. It showed a six.

She never once beat me at dice. I don't know whether she threw it or set it that way.

There had been another by the pouch, and I had picked that one up on floor eighteen. Her dice were a pair. I put this one away too, with the other.

I have counted paces all my life. I had counted these. Four hundred and twenty. It was the first time she ever got it right.

I sat down at the mouth of that hole and cried very loudly, crying and swearing together. I called her an idiot, I called her short, I swore until I ran out of words. She had taught me all of them.

From there on up, every corner had her mark. Which stone was loose, where a stair was missing, where to keep right. The higher I went, the bigger the writing got, and the harder she had pressed, because that was from when she still had her strength. It was the first time since I came down that I had been afraid to die, and I walked home along her writing. I never once missed my step.

The fifteenth floor. I stood in front of the little hill of ash. The forgemaster had said: Tell them to stop digging. I said I would tell them. They will not. I know that. But I would tell them.

The thirteenth floor, the library. I took down the leather-bound scripture, copied the torn page and put the copy back in its place, and took the original with me. I also added one small line to that page: This page was once torn out. The one who tore it had a good heart.

The tenth floor. The candles were all out and the crack had closed, leaving only a black line. I crouched down and said very quietly, "Then don't come back."

This time there was no voice to copy me.

The ninth floor, the captain's room. The sheet was still pulled very tight. I sat on it for a bit and creased it. I felt that was better.

The eighth floor, the Southerner's niche. His boots and pack were still there, and I left them there again. I felt in my coat for a stub of candle I had picked up in the chapel, lit it with my flint and stood it in his niche. He was afraid of the dark. That candle probably wouldn't last until I was off the floor.

I set it down and went.

The seventh floor. That wall with the white crust was gone, and the stairs were right there, as if nothing had ever happened. The one who wrote "There were stairs," I hope he saw that too.

The sixth floor, the guardroom. I took out the five letters that man had written to his mother and laid them on top of the log. Whether that cow ever calved was three hundred years ago now. The man with the crooked helmet in the next niche was still lying there. This time I didn't touch his helmet.

The fifth floor.

I pushed open that door. The captain was still there, his hammer set down very straight, his armour kneeling on the ground. Only now did it occur to me to wonder how long he had been kneeling like that. I found out later I had been down there for nearly three years.

I took out that writ of the Order, found the last line and read it aloud.

**"Let this stand until the Well be quiet."**

My voice carried a long way through the crypt. The niches rose tier on tier on either side, and they all lay very neatly.

"I am the last guardian of the Order," I said. "By order of—"

By whose order? Whoever sent me was probably gone too. But no one other than me could say this.

"I declare," I said, "that this Well is quiet. The order is lifted."

I waited a moment. Nothing happened.

"Sixth Watch, you may stand down."

I felt like a fool. I put my hand on his shoulder plate.

"You asked me to see for you," I said. "I've seen. There's nothing down there. Only a girl, who held on for a thousand years."

As I walked out, there was a very small sound behind me. I looked back. In one niche a helmet had slid off and rolled to the side, and the skull had tipped over. They weren't lying quite so neatly any more.

They had rolled over.

I know that doesn't mean anything. Stones shift and things fall. But I saw it.

Then I came to that staircase on the fourth floor.

I climbed very slowly, afraid of miscounting. My left hand still couldn't grip, so I pressed my right thumb into my forefinger, one press for each step. At the top, in front of that wall, I stopped, turned back both my sleeves and found the last number from the way down.

Eleven thousand four hundred and seven.

The number I had counted coming up was also eleven thousand four hundred and seven.

I worked it out again.

I sat down. This time my legs really did give way. Count going down, count coming up, and in a thousand years they had never once been the same.

They matched.

The Well was dead. It was only then that I truly believed it.

The chalk writing was still on the wall. I never did find out who wrote it. I felt around on the ground and found a stub of chalk someone had dropped, and I added a line below his. My writing was ugly, but we had one habit in common. We both wrote only the facts.

"Today they match."

The third floor, that body sitting against the wall, that good pair of boots. I hadn't taken them on the way down. I took them now. I stood there and put them on, and they fit exactly. I said to him, "Sorry. I regretted it by the tenth floor." Then I walked on up in his boots.

The first floor. I found the cellar where I had picked up that letter. I copied it and put the original back on that chest. If the writer's brother came back, that was where he would look first.

On the wall at the cellar mouth was the last mark. For Mavis it had been the first, so it was the biggest. The arrow pointed up, and next to it she had written:

"Dinner."

## The Surface

::panel.f35-2

The first breath of air was sweet.

It was evening. I climbed up from the cellar of a house I didn't know and pushed open the trapdoor. The boards nailed across it had rotted. I lay on the ground with my face in the dirt, smelling grass and cow dung, and it was a long time before I got up.

There were people in the street.

They were all lying down. Some were face up, some face down, some in burial clothes, some in rotted work clothes. They had all walked a long way. One had got as far as the Well. One had dropped in front of his own door, with his hand still on the latch. They must have fallen at the same moment, the moment my sword came down. They had come all that way and were still just short.

No one had come to gather the bodies.

Half the village was burned or collapsed. A few chimneys were still smoking. An old man watched me through a crack in his door for a long time, then shut it. I was carrying a shield on my back, dragging a great sword, my right arm black to the shoulder. I don't suppose I looked very reassuring.

The Well was still on its mound, and the chains were still locked. I went and looked in. There was still nothing to see, only a cool breeze coming up from inside.

I went to the old woman's house.

The door was open. The low stool was still there, and the winnowing tray lay upside down on the ground. I don't know where the chickens had gone. I expect they had gone wild. A length of rotted rein was still tied to the post by the door. The beans spilled on the ground had sprouted and grown for three years, out past the threshold and over half the wall. I searched the house inside and out and didn't find her. I don't know if someone took her or she walked away on her own. She had said her legs wouldn't go that far.

I picked a handful of beans, lit a fire in her stove and cooked them. They didn't get soft. I've never been much of a cook. I sat on her stool and ate them all.

She had said she would cook for me. There's no paper to record that, so I keep it in my head.

I walked for three days to get back to the mountain.

The doors of the great hall stood open. The yard had grown over with grass, there were mushrooms on the posts, the kitchen pots had rusted, and there wasn't a soul. On the table was a note from the Grand Master, which said, more or less: The dead have reached the foot of the mountain. All who could be sent have been sent down. The rest are to go their own ways. Two old people who couldn't walk had stayed. I found their bodies in the back yard.

One was Floorboards, lying on the ground in the vegetable patch, face down. I think he was probably looking at something.

The other I don't want to call old. It was the cook. She was sitting on the bench by the kitchen's back door, with her apron still tied on, and beside her in a little pot was something black. I picked it up. It was burnt crust, kept for a very long time, as hard as stone. She was probably saving it for someone.

Pebble's bed was empty and very neatly made. I taught him that. He wasn't in the yard, and he wasn't on the road. Since then I've asked a lot of people if they've seen a boy in his teens who always stands with his elbow against his ribs. To this day no one has.

I hope he went over the wall.

The ones he had sent down, I had seen on my way back. From the eighteenth floor on there were a few fresher bodies by the path, wearing our crest. They had been sent down after me, and not one had caught up. Going down, I had thought it was only me again. There had been people behind me all along.

The Grand Master's roll lay open on his desk. I turned to the last page. It was in his hand, getting more and more crooked, one name to a line and a date after each, all dates after I went down the Well. The people I had called instructor when I was small, one after another. I saw Two-Fingers' real name for the first time. After it was written: "Went on his own. Would not take potions."

The second line from the end was Mavis. I knew it at once. He could still spell that much.

The date was the fourteenth month after I left. After the name was one word, in the Grand Master's badly shaking hand:

Volunteered.

I closed the roll.

No one sent her. She was never the kind who got sent. She couldn't even stand at a post properly, and the Grand Master would never have put someone like that on the roll. She walked into this room herself and told him she was going. I can imagine how she said it. She must have been laughing as she said it. She knew those nails were empty, she had known it since she was fifteen. She still bet I'd come back. She had lost to me twelve times, and this time she put her money on me.

She was only going to pay what she owed.

No one was waiting for me. How had I ever thought anyone was waiting?

On the wall in the very centre of the great hall hung our banner, chewed to holes by rats. You could still make out the circle and the eight rays. I stood under it and looked for a while. The shield on my back was heavy. I untied it and leaned it against the wall, right under the banner, and compared the two.

They were exactly the same. Even the number of rays.

There was no one in the hall to tell. I put the shield back on my back.

I went to the wall in the great hall. All the guardians' plates were still hanging there, over a row of empty nails. I found my master's plate. Then I found the last one, hung slightly crooked and still quite new, with my name on it.

I took my own plate down.

It was a simple movement. I thought about it for a long time. Then I got charcoal from the yard, went to each plate in turn, and wrote beside the nail. I didn't know what day any of them had fallen, so I wrote only what I knew. On the captain's I wrote: Held three hundred years. Relieved. On my master's I thought all night and wrote only: Letter received.

The other plates, the ones I didn't know, I wrote: Someone asked after you.

Then I walked out and pulled the door shut behind me.

I thought that would be the end of it.

I went back to the village and helped bury the people in the street. The families that were left gradually came out and asked me how it was down there. I said it was over. Some cried. One man knelt and kissed my hand, and it happened to be the black one, and then his face changed. I didn't tell him the hand was still warm.

The burying took the whole winter. The ground was frozen hard, and we had to light fires to soften it first. There were only twenty-odd survivors, old and sick. I could dig faster on my own than all of them put together, and they didn't much like coming near me. One woman held on to her husband's body and wouldn't let go. She said this was the third time he had died. The first was his lungs. The second was her, with a shovel. She asked me, is it real this time?

I said it was real.

She asked how I knew. I said I had counted the stairs. She looked at me as if I were mad, but she let go.

The news travelled faster than I expected. The dead had stopped walking, and all the nearby villages knew it.

The next spring I was helping someone put a roof back on when I heard hooves on the road.

There were a lot of banners, in one long line. I watched from the roof. They were royal banners, brand new, much like the crest on the stone house door on the sixteenth floor, only in different colours. A very well-dressed man walked up the mound, opened a sheet of paper and began to read.

I went over to listen.

The King was very pleased. The King thanked the Order for its sacrifice. The calamity below the Well had been lifted, and from this day these lands were Crown land.

Behind him stood a line of people, several hundred of them, with picks and lamps, some of them very young, with very clean hands. Wagon after wagon carried timber. Someone was already measuring the mouth of the Well for a winch frame.

No one mourned, and no one asked who was down there. They said there was a story of warm stones down there that could make any weapon stronger, and now nothing was guarding them.

I found a rock to sit on, laid my sword across my knees and watched those people line up, all afternoon. That's what the picture shows.

There was a very young miner in the line, sixteen or seventeen. As he passed me he took a second look at me, and a second look at the shield behind me. He asked the man next to him, very quietly, who that was. The man said he didn't know, probably some lunatic.

At his age, I'd have said the same.

Eleven cores this month, six men lost. The Crown is pleased.

That paper was still in my coat.

## The Keeper

::panel.f35-3

You should recognise this picture. It's my hut, the way it first looked.

I'll be honest with you. I was no good at all at blocking anyone.

I stood the shield and the great sword beside the mouth of the Well, because I didn't want to carry them any more. The day the winch frame first lowered people down, I stood in front of the Well and told them not to go. The foreman looked at my hand, looked at the great sword, and told me to move. I didn't. That night I chopped the frame down.

I was still very strong then. They spent ten days building another, and I chopped that down too. The third time, they brought soldiers.

I could have beaten them. I had seventeen stones in my sword, and few people in the world could have touched me. Those young soldiers held up their spears and their hands were shaking. I put my hand on the hilt. My hand was very steady.

I had seen this hand that steady once before, in the gatehouse on the twenty-fourth floor, with the point of the knife against the wall.

I can hold this. I alone can hold this.

If this went on, I thought, sooner or later there would be a wall beside this Well too, covered with those words.

That night they camped on the hillside. I sat alone by the Well with my sword across my legs, and with that old knife I prised the stones out of their sockets one at a time, seventeen in all, and lined them up on the rim. They were very pretty, with a little red glow in the dark. I swept my hand along, and they rolled over the edge one after another. I listened for a long time and never heard one land.

I picked up the sword. I could hardly lift it.

Four years of wounds all came back. I lay on the ground for seven days and thought I would die. Ribs, left arm, the back of my hand, there wasn't a place that didn't hurt, and the pain made my whole body cramp. Then came the hunger. I threw up the porridge the village sent, twice, and ate it three more times. Only then did I remember that I hadn't really been hungry in four years.

On the eighth day I slowly got up, and a big handful of my hair fell out. My left arm never fully healed. You know that. The hand still works. It can hold things and pin down a page. The arm just won't lift past the shoulder. The black on my right arm slowly drew back, over several years, and in the end it drew back to my palm and stopped. That patch is still there.

You've touched it. You said it felt like a stone that had been sitting in the sun.

From that day on, I couldn't beat anyone. The first soldier who came to shove me knocked me over. They carried me to one side and went on lowering people down.

So I tried something else. It wasn't impressive at all. Laugh if you like.

I started drawing.

I had learned a little as a child, because the Order made you learn to draw maps. I put up a hut beside the Well and bought some old paper, because old paper is cheap. During the day I sat by the road down, where everyone going below had to pass me, carrying their lamps, in a very long line. My strength couldn't stop them, so I used my mouth.

"Can you read?"

Most couldn't, so I read to them. I read them every page.

"Eleven cores this month, six men lost. The Crown is pleased."

"All hands accounted for. All hands. Only hands."

I showed them the pictures, the mountain, the wall, those eyes. I showed them my hand. Whatever I had seen, I told them all of it. Some laughed at me. Some called me a madwoman. The foreman had me thrown out, and I came back the next day.

The first year, not one person turned back.

The second year, a young man listened to the end, stood there a long time, put down his pick and left. He didn't look at me as he went. I've always remembered his back.

Twenty years later he came back once, a plump middle-aged man who sold cloth in the south, with three children. He stood at my door, pushed the children in front of me one at a time and told them to greet me. I didn't recognise him. He said, "That day you read the roll from the last shift. All hands accounted for. I was seventeen, and I thought about my hands." He held up both of them to show me. They were very plump, with all the fingers on. He gave me a bolt of cloth. These clothes I have on now are made from it.

The third year, there were three.

The fourth year, a lord came, very well dressed, with two clerks. He stood at the door of my hut with a handkerchief over his nose and was extremely polite.

He said the King had heard of my deeds. The first guardian in a thousand years to come back alive was the pride of the nation. The King wished to rebuild the Order, with a great hall on the mountain ten times the size of the old one, and that shield and that sword enshrined at its centre. I would be the first Grand Master, with a stipend and an estate. Every year the best children in the country would be sent for me to teach in person.

I asked what I would be teaching them.

"Guarding, of course," he said. "You have been down the Well, you know better than anyone. Someone must always stand guard, just in case."

I asked him where those children were going to stand guard.

He laughed and said such details could be worked out in time. And there was just one small thing, he said: it would be best if I stopped saying those things to the miners. It was bad for morale. Those old papers could be handed over to the new hall for safekeeping, where qualified people would sort them and copy out the suitable parts.

The suitable parts.

I asked him to wait, went into the hut, brought out the sealed letter from the twenty-second floor, and came out and read it to him. **Every guardian since the founding hath fallen to the thing they guarded. We have not told the candidates. Who then would go?**

His handkerchief came down. He looked at me as if seeing me for the first time. He was a clever man and didn't ask where the letter came from. He bowed and left. The two clerks followed him, and one of them looked back at me.

The next year that clerk came back alone and asked if he could make a copy.

I let him. So now there are at least two copies in the world, maybe more.

**She stood not against what lay below, but against those who would go down.** That's what I wrote under the picture, and I thought it was too grand even as I wrote it. In truth I didn't stop many. Far more went down than turned back. Every spring several hundred went below. Some came up and some didn't. The thing at the bottom of the Well was dead, but a mine is still a mine. Timbers collapse, rock walls crack, and the fire was still burning. I took up a ledger too and kept it line by line, as those before me had, recording the ones who didn't come up. The difference was that I recorded their names, because I could ask.

Some of those who came up would come to find me, because their hands had started to go warm. A small patch on the palm, the colour of a coal nearly gone out. They showed me with a laugh and said, look, I'm not tired at all. I told them to take the stones out of their pockets and throw them back, and that it would hurt for a while after. Most refused. One agreed. It hurt so much he bit through his tongue. Afterwards he stayed and helped me fix my roof, for two years, and one day he went off down the hill. He walked with a limp, but he walked down on his own.

The first winter nearly killed me.

With the stones gone I was an ordinary person again, worse than ordinary. My left arm wouldn't lift past the shoulder, my ribs hurt on cloudy days, and the wind came through the walls of the hut. I couldn't cook and I couldn't chop wood. In fifteen years the only thing I had learned was how to fight. I tried growing beans once, from the ones I had picked in the old woman's yard, and they all rotted. One day I came down with a high fever. I lay on the floor looking at the roof and thought, this time I really will die, and there won't be a stone ring.

I was very afraid. I've always remembered how afraid I was. I also remember knowing very clearly that this was how it should be. A person ought to be this afraid of dying.

On the third day the door was pushed open and a miner came in with half a bucket of soup. The foreman had sent him to throw me out. He saw me on the floor, swore, put the soup down and left. The next day another one came. I don't know who told them to. I drank a lot of bad soup that winter, worse than the cook's porridge.

In spring I got up and went back to sitting by the road and reading out papers. The people who had brought soup went down as usual and laughed at me as usual.

Every evening at dusk I would go to the Well and sing that song from the library on the thirteenth floor, once through, with its beginning and its end. My voice was as bad as ever. The miners laughed, and some threw stones. There was nothing left down there that needed reminding, but I felt someone should still tell it that it had walls. You've heard it. You said it was awful.

Something happened in the sixth year. Your mother has probably heard about it. You can ask her.

That year the seams got narrower, and a grown man's shoulders wouldn't fit. The Crown sent a new foreman, very capable. One morning he led twelve children up the mound, the oldest eleven, the youngest looking no more than seven, strung together by a rope around their waists. Their parents all owed the Crown money. The children were carrying little picks, newly made, the handles still white.

By then I couldn't beat anyone, and my left arm wouldn't lift past the shoulder.

I walked to the front of the winch frame, sat down and stuck my legs under the rope.

The foreman had me carried off. I walked back and sat down again. The third time, he told two soldiers to beat me until I couldn't walk back. They were very thorough. I heard my ribs break. I know that sound well. When they had finished, I crawled back. By then the first child was already tied into the cage, the youngest, a little girl. Her pick had dropped, and she bent to pick it up and the rope held her. She wasn't crying. She was looking at me, and in her eyes was a person with red hair.

My hair was still red then.

My hand couldn't reach the rope. I lay there with my mouth full of blood and thought, this is as far as it goes, I can't hold this, and this time I mean it.

Someone came over and sat down beside me.

It was a miner. I still remember his name, because he's in my ledger, and after his name it says "alive". He had listened to me read for five years, never once turned back, and laughed at me every time. He didn't look at me or speak to me. He just took down his pick, laid it across his knees and sat there.

After a little while another came.

The people in the line stepped out, one by one. No one shouted anything. They weren't heroes, and many kept glancing at the foreman's face. But they all sat down. That morning there were more than four hundred people sitting on the mound, all the way around the winch frame. The soldiers stood there with their spears up and didn't know who to stab.

The foreman roared for a long time. At noon he told them to bring the cage back and untie the rope.

I've held up a lot of things in my life. In front of my master, in front of those eyes, I was always alone. I had always thought that was the only way it could go. Every guardian thought so.

That day I lay on the ground and held up nothing.

I've never been able to draw that one. If you want to know what it looked like, go and look at the sheep on the hillside. When it rains they all lean together, and not one of them is holding anything up.

That youngest girl was untied that day and carried off. Her mother was the woman in the street who wouldn't let go of her husband. I'll get to her shortly.

That girl is nearly fifty now. She has scars on her hands from the rope, and she can't read. The day you read the eighth floor, she stood at my door and wouldn't come in, and listened to me read a few pages. If you ask her about the sixth year, she probably won't tell you much. She still walks the long way round whenever she sees what's left of the winch frame, those few beams.

But she let you come, and with the sheep too. You should know how much strength that takes her.

There's one more thing I have to tell you.

One day an old woman with a walking stick came from a long way off. She had heard there was a woman by the Well who could read out what the dead had left. She said her father had gone down the Well sixty years ago, when she was six.

I took out the stack of papers and found the one I had picked up on the third floor, the one where the writing went through the paper.

She couldn't read, so I read it to her.

**"Tell my daughter I found the bottom."**

I don't know if that was her father. The paper has no name on it, and no one knows what year it was written. Every year after that a few more people like her came, and I read them this page. Each of them felt sure it was their father.

When I finished, she was silent for a long time.

"Did he really find it?" she asked me.

I had picked up that paper on the third floor, with other people's pickles right over my head. Things in chests get moved around, and he may have gone further. But I don't believe it.

I promised you I wouldn't hide things from you. I never promised her.

"He found it," I said. "He went all the way to the bottom."

She nodded, touched the paper, said thank you, and walked off on her stick.

I lied to her. Now you know. Think what you like.

At night I sat at the door of my hut and drew, and this picture is what I drew. Those people with their lamps, in a very long line, crossing that arched bridge with no bottom you could see. Aislinn's shield and sword standing beside them. The chains lying broken all over the ground, torn apart when they put up the winch frame, and no one ever joined them again. My right palm was warm. When I got tired of writing, I held it against my face.

Let me tell you about the days in those years.

Once the mine revived, the village actually came back to life. With several hundred more people in the valley, someone had to sell drink and bread and lamp oil. The woman who wouldn't let go of her husband opened up a bakery again, on top of her old cellar, which she had filled in with stones. Every few days she came up the mound and brought me half a loaf. She put it at the door and didn't come in and didn't speak. I asked how much I owed. She said you're a madwoman, madwomen don't pay.

It was two years before she started coming in. She didn't like me, and I didn't know how to be around people, so we just sat. She mended clothes and I drew. One day she looked at a picture I was halfway through and asked, "Who's this?" I said it was me. She said it didn't look like me, I was uglier. I laughed. That was the first time I had laughed since I came back.

I asked what her name was. We had known each other two years, and it had only just occurred to me to ask.

"Aisling," she said. "It's an old name round here. Why?"

I said nothing. I put my head down and went on drawing, and my hand shook a little. That was your grandmother.

Her daughter grew up and had you. Your grandmother lived to be very old. Her legs were bad for her last few years, so it was my turn to walk down the hill and see her every day. We argued for forty years. She never knew whose name she had, and I never told her. I didn't know how to start. What she said to you at the end, I don't know. That's between you and her. The last thing she said to me was, "Your porridge is still terrible."

I helped carry her coffin. I carved the plate. I carved it very slowly. That name had come down bent out of shape, in its spelling and its sound, but it was still there. I thought, Maeve, look.

The miners had a hard life. They went down for two shifts a day and drank the Crown's red potion, so they didn't feel tired, and at thirty they looked fifty. I knew an old miner who could hardly write a word. He came to me every day before he went below, not to hear me read but to tell me his grandson's name. He said, "If I don't come up, put me in your ledger, and put him in while you're at it." I said your grandson hasn't gone down the Well. He said, "He will."

Some nights the people coming off shift would drink and sing at the foot of the mound, and what they sang was the song from when we were small. Stand the wall, draw the line. They had made the last line very dirty. I sat at my door and listened and thought of Mavis. Hers was much better.

That went on for a good many years.

In the autumn of the ninth year of digging, a foreman came up with a stone in his hand and stood there staring at it. I went over to look.

The stone was grey, and it was cold.

That year more and more of the stones they dug up were cold. I thought about it afterwards. They had always been that thing's fire. The thing had died, the fire had burned on a few more years, and it was time for it to go out.

I held that cold stone in my hand. It was a very ordinary stone. I badly wanted to laugh, and I badly wanted to tell someone.

When the Crown's people came to look, they brought an assayer. In the foreman's shed he put that year's stones in water and in fire, one by one, for three days. Finally he stood in front of the winch frame and looked into the mouth of the Well for a long time.

I walked over. I was thirty-five then, with my hair starting to go white, and he paid me no attention. I asked him, do you know what these things are?

He turned to look at me. He wasn't young, and he wore gloves.

"I do," he said. "We have the records in our library. We bought them from the Order."

I asked him, so you knew, and you still dug?

He thought for a moment, his face very polite. "You must understand," he said. "The war in the east has gone on for twenty years, and it all rests on this. Do you think His Majesty has a choice?"

He left. I heard later that he resigned when he got back.

The cold stones were heaped at the foot of the mountain, a little hill of them that nobody wanted. Later people took them to pave the road. The road you take with your sheep every day is those stones. You walk on them, and they're stones, and they mean nothing else.

The miners left, one batch after another. Half the people stayed in the village, broke new fields and kept sheep. The bakery's still there. People put up new houses on the hillside and had children, the children grew up, and no one sends children into the Well any more.

The Crown sent people to look twice more, and then they stopped coming. By the spring of the eleventh year, no one was carrying a lamp on the road. No one repaired the winch frames, and they fell over, one beam at a time. Grass grew out of the cracks in the stones of the Well's rim.

No one told me I could leave.

I didn't leave, either.

## The Well Remembers

::panel.f35-4

This is the last one. I drew it just last month.

You know when this is. You're in it too, lying on that stone. I drew your hair too messy. You can be angry.

I'm standing behind you. Look at my hair, almost all white, with one lock that still has a little red. My right hand is on your shoulder. You said your shoulder felt very hot at the time, and you thought it was the sun.

You asked me what's down there and I said nothing much. Now that you've read all sixty-nine pages, you should know that was half right and half wrong.

That thing is dead. I killed it myself.

So what was it that called you in your grandmother's voice?

I've thought about it for many years, and here is what I think. For a thousand years the Eye learned, and it learned everyone who ever spoke near it: the captain's orders, the priest's prayers, what a mother said to her dead son. It died, and those voices are still there. You shout into a valley and walk away, and the echo takes a while to stop. Something that learned for a thousand years takes a very, very long time to stop echoing.

It isn't hungry. There's nothing behind it waiting to eat you. It just still remembers your grandmother's voice, and that's all it has left.

That's why I told you that day: The Well still remembereth. But it shall not take our children anymore.

Truth is, when I said it I wasn't that sure. I'm someone who lied to that old woman, after all. I'm old now and don't have much strength left. After I die, will someone hear some rumour and come here to build something or dig something? I don't know. How long will that echo take to stop? I don't know that either. People always want more, and when will that stop? My guess is it won't.

That's why I'm giving you all this.

I don't want you to stay. Listen carefully, this is the most important thing. In case I go soft in the head, I'm writing it here first: **You do not have to stay.** Don't take my place. I wrote all of this so you'd know where everyone who took someone's place ended up. If you want to keep a hundred sheep, go and keep them. That's very good.

I only want you to do one thing, and it's fine if you don't.

If anyone asks, lend them this book. If they can't read, read it to them. Don't tear out a single page, especially the ugly ones, the frightening ones, the ones you think a child shouldn't know. The one who tore the scripture on the thirteenth floor, the one who locked up the letter on the twenty-second, they were good people. They had good hearts. That's how all of it starts.

I've copied all sixty-nine pages into this. You can count them.

Today's number is zero. We're back on the surface. The sunset is very fine, and the wildflowers by the Well have come out again these last few days. That shield is covered in vines. I don't plan to clear them.

Every year I pull the vines aside and look once. Those few cracks of gold, after all these decades, haven't dimmed at all.

The sword is the same. You've touched it. It doesn't rust.

There's one more thing. I hadn't planned to write it, but I said I wouldn't hide things from you.

That sword and that shield are both here. I dragged them up from the very bottom with my own hands. There's nothing left down there. All the eyes have closed. I saw it.

But sometimes, on nights with no moon, I walk to the Well and look down, for a long time, until my eyes get used to the dark. At the very bottom, very, very far away, there's a very thin line, shining.

Straight. Gold.

I don't know what it is. My eyes are old, and maybe I'm seeing wrong. Maybe it's this old wound in my palm playing tricks. Maybe she left something behind that I didn't bring up. Maybe it isn't hers at all.

Don't go and look. I'm not telling you not to be curious. I'm telling you not to go and look alone.

There's also a pair of socks, in the cupboard with the short wooden sword. You asked me once why I don't throw them out when they're so worn. That IOU is folded up inside them, and the two dice are in the toe.

When I walked sixteen days to the coast with the ring, I went to look at the sea as well. I stood there for an afternoon. It's very big, it stinks, and there's not much to see. The wind blows and the waves come and go, busy with who knows what. When I got back there was no one to tell, so I told the cupboard. I made it sound a bit better than it was. She did say I could lie.

I never hung the plaque. I never carved the name. That heap of rubble on floor eighteen, I never went down to again. In the years when the mining was at its height it would have been easy, and I thought about it many times. It was harder than killing that thing.

I'm past seventy now. She is still twenty-three, very short, laughing like a chicken being stepped on. Sometimes I think that if she had gone over the wall she would be a very annoying old woman by now, living by the sea and owing the whole town money.

My bed stands against the right-hand wall, with one bed's space left on its left side. On winter nights when my feet are cold I stretch them over there, and they touch nothing. Every year around then the scar on my heel starts to hurt. When it hurts I swear at her, and then it's better.

That queen wouldn't end, and she held on for a thousand years. She sold herself to the dark so that someone would remember a name. My friend sat down in a corridor, did the sum, and decided that this far was fine. I lived, and I eat every day. That isn't because I'm stronger than anyone. She told me to.

My hand is still warm. It will probably stay warm until the day I die. When that day comes, bury me, and not near any cellar. Put me on the hillside where you can see the sheep.

If I walk back, you'll know what to do.

This book is still missing one thing. I didn't tell you my name until the twenty-fifth floor. I never wrote yours, because I wanted you to write it yourself. You've been coming to me for three years, and you've been able to read for nearly one. I've left a line below.

You've probably forgotten the day I taught you to read. After your grandmother was gone, you often brought your sheep up the mound. You didn't do anything. You just sat there. One day you asked me what the words carved on the door said. I said it was the first name in the ledger. You asked me who that was. I said you'd have to read it yourself.

The first word you could write was "sheep". For the second you wrote your grandmother's name, with one stroke wrong. I didn't correct it.

Names matter. Everything that exists has an edge. Like my name, Aisling. You start at the top of your head and you end at your toes, and that's all the size you are. That's a very good thing.

Write it neatly.

You finished reading today. The sun is nearly down. You're sitting on the threshold with the last sheet of paper in your hand, and you haven't said anything for a long time.

The little black lamb runs over and nibbles your sleeve. Have you named it yet? You haven't told me. That's fine. It's enough if the two of you know.

You asked me what that line really is. I said I don't know. You asked me what happens from here. I don't know that either.

Then you asked what it felt like, that letting go on the thirty-fifth floor.

I hold out my right hand, make a fist and slowly open it. The patch on my palm is still warm.

"Like this," I say.

You copy me. You make a fist and open it again. You look at your hand. It's very small and very dirty, with mud under the nails and all five fingers on.

You pick up the pen.
