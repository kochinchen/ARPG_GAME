# Prologue

## At the Well

The evening I pulled you back from the Well, you asked me what was down there. I said nothing. You didn't believe me. You were right not to.

You were lying on the stone rim with half your body hanging over, and you said someone down there was calling you. I asked what they were calling. You said your name. I asked whose voice it was. You thought about it and said it was your grandmother's. Your grandmother has been dead three years. I helped carry her coffin.

So I am getting these papers out to show you.

There are sixty-nine pages, and none of them are my words. Thirty-four are things I picked up at the bottom of the Well: letters, ledgers, words cut into walls. The words on the walls I copied down. Ten are notes left by people who went down before, tucked into chests. I didn't know a single one of the people who wrote them. The other twenty-five are what the dead said, one line to a page. I was standing right there when they said it. I wrote it on my sleeve on the spot and copied it onto paper when I got back to the surface.

There are also twelve pictures. I drew them, and I wrote the words underneath them too. You'll notice the person in those words is always "she." The red-haired one is me. I drew most of them when I was young, and back then, if I had to write "I," I couldn't draw at all. I'm old now and I can write it. The last one I drew only last month. The words are still the same way, out of habit.

The Well goes down thirty-five floors. I'll tell them to you one at a time, one floor a day. Come over and read when you've finished with the sheep. Ask me about any words you don't know.

Two things before we start.

First, I won't hide anything from you. The business of this Well has been hidden for a thousand years, and in every generation someone thought hiding it was for the best. I met those people later. All of them are down there.

Second, I won't tell you my name until floor twenty-five. I'm not being coy. Until I got there, I didn't know what it meant myself.

You also asked me whose they are, the shield and the sword at the mouth of the Well, the ones overgrown with vines. I said wait a while. This wait is going to be a long one, probably until floor thirty-five. For now, just keep looking at them.

# Book One: The Cellars

Floors 1 to 5

## Floor One

In the autumn I was twenty-two, the Order sent me down the Well.

The Order sounds like a big thing. By then there were nineteen people left in the great hall up on the mountain. Seven of them couldn't walk and three were cooks. The Grand Master called me in, read me a passage in the old language, and handed me a sealed paper. I asked who else was going with me. He said guardians always go alone. I asked who went last. He stared at the table and said, "You know who."

I knew. The last one was my master. He had gone down ten years before.

You've never seen the Order, so I should tell you what it was.

A thousand years ago someone built the first great hall near this Well, and from then on there were always people wearing the same crest and doing one job: when the seal came loose, send someone down. The crown paid out money every year, and the villages around the Well paid a Well tax every year. By the time I was small, not much of that money was coming in anymore. The crown had cut us off ten years before I went down, and the villages paid a few sacks of wheat if they could manage it. We grew our own vegetables, kept chickens and sold charms. A charm was a wooden tag with the crest burned into it. We sold them to people who were afraid of the Well, and they were supposed to keep you safe. I sold a great many. I never heard of anyone being saved by one.

You've seen the crest in the pictures, a circle with eight rays inside. It was on our shields, our flags and our chests. I asked an instructor once what it was, and he said it was the sun. I asked what you'd want with a sun underground. He told me not to ask so many questions.

While I was packing, the cook came in. She was a plump woman with very rough hands, and the porridge she made was terrible. She pushed three small bottles into my pack. They held a red potion, with wax over the necks.

"Don't smell it when you drink it," she said. "It's bitter and it tastes of iron. It'll make you hot."

I asked her what it was made from. She said she didn't know either. The recipe had been handed down in the Order. There was a bag of grey powder, and all she did was add water. The bag was nearly empty.

She looked at me, then took a piece of burnt crust out of her apron and pushed that into my pack too. She had been doing that since I was five. When the porridge burned, she saved the crust from the bottom of the pot for the children who couldn't sleep at night.

On the morning I left, the people of the great hall stood in the yard and, as the rule requires, raised their swords and drew one straight line in the air. No one said anything. When I reached the main gate someone came chasing after me, running and yelling in a very ugly voice.

It was Mavis.

She was the only friend I had on the mountain. She came the same year I did, never grew much, and had the second training post, right next to mine. She couldn't sit still, she stole bread, and she laughed like a chicken being stepped on. Her name, they say, is an old word for a songbird, the thrush. Her mother picked it. It was probably the biggest mistake her mother ever made about her. Over the years she had come to owe me twelve gold coins, all lost on bets, and she had never paid back one.

She ran up to me, too out of breath to talk, and shoved a pair of socks at me.

"You come back," she said, "and I'll pay you the twelve."

"Where would you get the money?"

"I'll steal it." Then she laughed, that ugly laugh, and it stopped halfway.

I put the socks away and got on the horse. I didn't look back. A knight doesn't look back, that's the rule. Really I was afraid I'd see her still standing there.

I rode down off the mountain, two days. The village with the Well sits in a valley, and from a long way off you can see the church spire. It looks like any other village. Only when I got close did I see there was no one in the street. Every door stood open. Every cellar hatch had boards nailed across it, nailed crooked, the way you do when you're in a hurry.

I had been to this village before, at seventeen, when I came with Old Bow from the Bow-hall to collect the Well tax. It was a lively place then. The mill was turning, people were washing wool at the river, and children ran after our horses. The village elder gave us some terrible wine and said the harvest had been bad this year, could the tax wait till next year. Old Bow said fine. She said fine every year.

People in the village didn't talk much about the Well back then. It sat on the little hill, locked, with the grass grown tall around it, and sheep went up there to graze. When grown-ups scolded their children they'd say "Go jump in the Well," the way people elsewhere say "Drop dead." Apart from that, everyone went on farming, quarrelling and marrying off their daughters. After a thousand years, you can't be afraid of one thing every day.

The year I was nineteen, rumours began. People down the mountain said their cellars made noises in the night. The year I was twenty-one, the Grand Master sent me down to take a look. A man came up from the baker's cellar, still in his burial clothes. I knew him. He had sold me bread and once gave me an extra piece. I called to him. He didn't answer, and he kept walking toward me.

I cut him down. It was the first time I had ever cut down a person, and he wasn't really a person anymore, but there was still flour on his hands. When he fell, a woman was standing at the top of the cellar stairs, her apron covered in flour too. She looked at me. She didn't cry and she didn't speak. I went back up the mountain and told Mavis nothing. I finished my porridge that day and then threw all of it up.

Back to the day I rode into the village. Only one house on the whole street had anybody out front. An old woman sat on a low stool with a winnowing basket in her lap, picking over beans, with a few chickens walking around her feet. She looked up at the crest on my chest and looked back down.

"Another one," she said.

I asked her why she hadn't left. She said her legs wouldn't go that far and the chickens wouldn't move. I asked her what had happened here. She lifted her chin at the boarded hatches and said, "Got eyes, haven't you?"

I got off the horse and held the reins out to her. I told her the horse was hers, I wouldn't be needing it. I felt very noble saying it.

She didn't take them.

"The last one left me his horse too," she said. "Stood just like that, with his elbow held low. That horse only died of old age last year. Ate like anything."

My hand was still out in the air. I asked her what he had said to her.

"He asked what I wanted for the beans." She tossed a bad one to the chickens. "When you get back, I'll cook you some."

She said it in exactly the voice she had used to say the chickens wouldn't move. She didn't believe for a moment that I'd be back. It was just how she said goodbye to people. Then she took the reins after all and tied them to the post by the door. I have thought a lot since about that bowl of beans.

The Well is on a little hill in the middle of the village.

I first saw it when I was nine. That spring my master had business down the mountain and took me along. Passing through this village, he led his horse up the little hill. The grass around the Well was tall, and two sheep were grazing beside it. He stopped three paces from the Well and went no further. I ran ahead to look in, and he grabbed me by the back of the collar, lifted me clean off the ground and set me down beside him.

"Don't go near it," he said.

I asked what was down there.

He looked at it for a long time, so long that the horse started eating the grass. His elbow was held low, and his hand stayed on his sword hilt the whole time, as if he were waiting for the Well to move first.

"Nothing," he said.

You see, I learned it from him too.

The day I was twenty-two, I walked up that little hill again.

I drew this many years later, but that is how it looked that day. Three iron chains crossed over the mouth of the Well and locked. The lock was bigger than my head and its keyhole had rusted solid long ago. I looked down and saw nothing. Then the cellar hatch of the house on the right started to make a noise.

It wasn't the wind.

It was a hand, very patient, working at the boards one finger at a time. I drew my sword, went over, and pried the boards off. I figured I'd rather go in after it than wait for it to come out. I learned later how stupid that was, and that every guardian had thought the same thing.

The mouth of the Well had always been locked. No one had gone down that way in a thousand years. The real way in was the cellars. The thing at the bottom of the Well had been breaking through them from below, one after another, until the whole village was sitting on a mouth.

The cellar was low, and I had to keep my head down. There were wine barrels, pickles and a broken broom. There was also a skeleton in chain mail with its back to me and one hand held up in the air, as if reaching for something on the shelf.

I took it for properly dead and walked past it.

It turned its head.

The skull came most of the way around before the body moved at all. The hand swung down off the shelf with a rusty short knife in it. The knife went past my ear and took off a lock of my hair. I backed away, hit my head on a beam, and everything went black. It was already on me, both hands around my neck. The bones were ice cold, and they were far stronger than bones have any right to be. We went into the wine barrels together and knocked them over, and sour wine poured all over me. My sword was too long to swing in a place that low.

Everything the instructors had taught me, I couldn't remember one bit of it.

I used my forehead. I butted it in the face, its jaw fell off and its hands loosened a little. I got one hand free, grabbed its ribs, tore out the whole row, and smashed its head in with my sword hilt. It made very little noise coming apart, about what you'd get from someone tipping out a basket of kindling.

I sat on the floor, covered in wine, and it was a long time before I got my breath back. There were ten finger marks on my neck, and they took days to fade. In any fight I'd ever been in, there had at least been someone next to me. Here there was only me.

Further in, the brick wall had a hole in it, and behind the hole was a corridor that had no business being there. There were a dozen or so dead in it. Some were bare bones and some hadn't finished rotting.

The half-rotted ones can run.

No one had ever told me that. They had meat on them and tendons, their knees still bent, and when they saw me they charged with their mouths wide open, making a sound like a leaking bellows. I cut the first one's arm off and it hit me anyway. Its teeth went into my left shoulder and bit clean through the leather. I could smell its mouth. I threw it against the wall and needed three strokes to get through the neck, and the head was still biting after it hit the floor. The second and third were already there. The corridor was too narrow, so all I could do was keep backing up and cutting, until my arms went numb. I stepped on somebody's guts, slipped, and came down on one knee.

All three landed on me at once.

I stayed there on my knee, raised the sword over my head and stabbed upward. Their blood was black and cold, and it ran all over my head. I shoved them off with my shoulder, got up and cut some more. By the time the last one went down, I could hardly lift my left arm.

I took out the first bottle of potion. The cook had told me not to smell it, and I smelled it. It was like licking an old key. Once it was down, my belly got hot, and the heat crept all the way up to my shoulder. The wound itched like mad, and I watched it slowly close.

I'd had only three bottles and now one was gone. This was only floor one.

Beside me was a chest. On the chest lay a letter, sealed and never sent.

I thought of the night at the mill.

That was a month before I went down the Well. For three nights in a row the dead had been crawling out of several villages down the mountain. The villagers ran to hide in the stone mill house and sent someone up to beg for help. The great hall had only eight left who could still fight, counting Old Bow of the Bow-hall, Two-Fingers of the Art-hall, Mavis and me. The Grand Master said go.

We got there at dusk. The mill stood by the river, with harvested wheat fields all around it. After the sun went down, things began to stand up in the fields, one, two, and then the whole field. They wore all kinds of clothes. They didn't walk fast, and they walked toward the lights of the mill.

Old Bow climbed onto the roof, sat down, and stuck her arrows in the gaps between the tiles, row after row. Her hands already shook by then. When she raised the bow, they stopped.

"Don't let them touch the door," she said.

Mavis and I held the front of the door. It was simple at first. Cut the knees, smash the head. The arrows came down from the roof one by one, and every one landed in a head. But they kept coming. Past midnight the bodies were heaped knee-high, and the ones behind walked over the ones in front. The Ward-hall instructor stood on my left, shield up, elbow against his ribs, holding off three by himself, and his stance was perfect. A rotten hand came in under the shield, grabbed his ankle and pulled him over, and several more piled on. By the time I got to him, he had no face.

Two-Fingers was behind me throwing fire. He threw it all night, with that two-fingered hand of his smoking. Toward the end he couldn't stand anymore, so he sat on the doorstep and kept throwing, cursing as he did it. At some point Mavis had climbed the wall. She tripped the bodies one by one with a rope, and when she saw one get through she jumped down, finished it with a knife, and climbed back up. I saw her laughing, that ugly laugh, with her face covered in blood.

I don't remember how many I cut down. I only remember that near dawn I couldn't open my hands anymore, and my fingers were stuck to the hilt. Then the things in the field all stopped, turned around, and slowly walked back into the ground.

The sun came up. Old Bow was still sitting on the roof, without a single arrow left beside her. I climbed up. She looked up at me, and her hands had started shaking again.

"How many?" she asked.

Eight of us went and five came back. Half the villagers were dead. The Grand Master heard the report, looked at the table, and said he understood.

It was after that night that he opened the roster, to pick the next one to go down.

I found out later her mother wasn't the only one buried that harvest. Everyone who had died that year came back. They walked to their own cellars first, then up the stairs. They were quiet going up. All they wanted was to go home.

I put the letter inside my coat. I didn't know the woman who wrote it or her brother. It was the first thing I ever picked up down there. After that it became a habit.

## Floor Two

Floor two was bigger than floor one, which made no sense.

The village had only forty-odd houses. Even with every cellar knocked through, you wouldn't get corridors that long. But I walked for most of a day and didn't reach the end. Bit by bit the walls changed from brick to stone, and the chisel marks in the stone were old, older than the village.

There were more dead on this floor, and I was doing very well against them. I'm not boasting. That's truly how it felt at the time. The five halls of the Order are Blade, Bow, Art, Way and Ward. I had trained in all of them, and every instructor said I learned fast. These things were slow and didn't dodge, and I cut them down like firewood. I even wondered how those guardians had failed to come back. Maybe they hadn't practised enough.

Those few on floor one had given me a real fright, but I learned quickly. The corridors here were wide enough to swing a sword. When a skeleton came, I cut the knees first and smashed the head once it was down. With the half-rotted kind, I waited for it to lunge, stepped aside, let it hit the wall on its own, and cut the neck from behind. I started counting paces and counting the gaps between them. I could put down five in one go and hardly breathe hard.

At the end of the corridor was an open space with a ring cut into the floor. It was a big ring, cut deep and worn with age. I stepped inside and the cut lines lit up for a moment, a very pale white light.

My shoulder stopped itching and my feet stopped aching. My wounds slowly closed, slower than with the potion, but without the heat. I stood in the ring for a while, then stepped out. The light dimmed but didn't go out completely.

The Order's books said nothing about this. I figured the guardians before me must have left it for those who came after, as a place to rest. I even felt grateful to them.

Just remember this ring for now.

I should tell you how I grew up, so you know how stupid I was.

Until I was five I lived in a little village in the east. I no longer remember what it was called. That spring plague came. People got a fever first, then their necks swelled up, and in three days they were dead. My parents were in the first batch, then the neighbors. By the end there was no one left in the village but me and my mother's sister, and she was sick too, lying in the barn. I sat beside her and kept the flies off her.

After she died I went on sitting there keeping the flies off, for two days. I didn't know what else to do.

On the second day I heard hooves. A knight came up the road in old armor, with a crooked nose. He got off his horse, walked into the barn, looked at my aunt and looked at me. He didn't ask my name or whether I was afraid. He took half a loaf of bread out of his saddlebag and gave it to me, waited until I had finished eating, and lifted me onto the horse. I was still gripping the bunch of grass I had used on the flies. He tried once to pry it loose and couldn't, so he let me keep it, and I held it all the way up the mountain.

He burned that barn. I watched the fire from the horse, and he watched it too.

Then we travelled for two days. He didn't say much, and at night he wrapped me in his cloak. When we reached the mountain he took me to the kitchen and said to the cook, "This one needs feeding." Then he left.

That night I had that terrible porridge for the first time. I slept in a long dormitory, wet the bed, and didn't dare tell anyone. In the next bed a skinny little girl sat up and looked at me for a long time. Then she threw her blanket over to me, dragged my wet one across to her own bed, and went to sleep under it. The next day she charged me one copper coin for the work.

That was Mavis. The twelve gold coins she owes me came later.

Children of the Order start learning the Ward-hall stance at seven. There was a row of wooden posts in the yard and each child got one. Mine was the third. Left foot forward, shield at the height of your chin, elbow tight to your ribs, weight on the back foot. And you stand there. At first for as long as a stick of incense burns, later for half a day. If your legs shook you got the stick. Not hard, just a reminder. The instructor said the Ward-hall teaches only one thing, and you've learned it when you've stood so long you forget you're standing.

You had to sing while you stood, to keep from falling asleep. The song was very stupid. Every child knew it, and people in the villages below used it to quiet their babies:

Stand the wall, draw the line, / till the Well is quiet, then you dine.

We all said it was about supper. Mavis changed the last word to something rude, "...then you shit," and got hit for it several times.

The first year I hardly spoke. I never mentioned my village and no one asked, because every child on the mountain had much the same story. After my master dumped me on the cook, he didn't show up again for ten days. When he did, he had a wooden sword in his hand. He had whittled it himself, and made the handle so thick that I needed both hands to hold it.

"Hold this," he said.

I held it. He stood by and watched. I held it for a long time, and when my arms ached I didn't dare put it down. At noon he took half a biscuit out of his pocket and gave it to me. Then he took the sword from my hands, looked at the handle, shaved it a little thinner with a small knife, and gave it back.

That was the first day's lesson.

The second day I held it again. On the third I started swinging. He never told me when I swung right. He only told me where I went wrong, and he didn't use words for it. The backs of my hands swelled up and went down, and swelled up and went down, and by winter they couldn't swell anymore, because they had grown a layer of hard skin. When the cook saw them she rubbed lard into them, cursing him the whole time, and it was very foul cursing. He stood in the kitchen doorway and listened. When she was done, he took me back to the yard.

The children on the mountain belonged to the instructors, a dozen or more to each. Not me. The cook told me this later. In my first year the Grand Master went to put me on the dormitory list under the Ward-hall instructor, and my master stood in front of his desk and said, "This one's mine." The Grand Master said, you're a knight, you're always away, when would you have time to raise a child? He said it again, "This one's mine," and walked out.

The cook said I wasn't the only child he brought back from down the mountain. He tried each of them with the wooden sword, dropped them back in the kitchen and never asked after them again. Only me he came back to see after ten days, carrying a wooden sword with a grip cut too thick.

That first year I often woke in the night, dreaming of the barn. I didn't dare cry and wake the others, so I felt my way out into the yard. One night I found him sitting on the dormitory step, cleaning his sword. Who cleans a sword in the middle of the night? He saw me. He didn't ask anything and he didn't send me back. He lifted one side of his cloak, the same one he had wrapped me in those two nights after the barn. I went over and sat inside it. It smelled of sweat and rust, and I fell asleep at once. In the morning I woke in my own bed.

After that, whenever I woke in the night and went to look, he was sitting there. I was grown before it occurred to me to wonder when he slept in those years.

I don't know what day I was born. Everyone who knew died in that village. The year I was eight, I walked up to the third post one morning and found half a loaf of bread sitting on it. I asked him who had put it there. He said, "Today's the day you got on the horse." Every year after that, on that day, there was half a loaf on the post. None of the children on the mountain had a birthday. Only me.

The year I was nine I had a bad fever, so bad I didn't know anyone. I took hold of a hand and wouldn't let go, and I called it Father, for a long time. I had taken him for the man who died in that village. When I woke he was sitting by the bed with four of my fingernail marks in the back of his hand. I asked him whether I had said anything.

"No," he said.

He never could lie, and that was the worst lie he ever told.

He wasn't my father. I knew that, and so did he. Awake, I never once called him that, and he never called me his daughter. All he ever called me was "Hand." The cook swore at him like this: "You think she's your daughter? Who raises a child like this?" He stood in the doorway and listened, and he didn't answer.

I worked out later why, of all those children, he took me. Big Lad was stronger, the twins were faster, and Mavis was far cleverer. I was nothing special. I just didn't let go. The cook said he had tried every one of them the same way, told them to hold the wooden sword, and inside the time it takes a stick of incense to burn one would be crying, one would run, one would throw it down. I held it till noon. When he drew it out of my hands they stayed in the same shape.

"He was like that when he came up the mountain at five," the cook said, scraping a pot. "No father, no mother. Tell him to stand and he stood till he dropped, and when they carried him back and he woke up he went and stood again. You two are the same. When I swear at one of you I've sworn at both."

He had a stance with the elbow held low, and he wouldn't let me learn it. He said my arm wasn't as long as his. I watched him do it in the yard once, and that night I sneaked out and practised. The other children practised whatever the instructors taught them. I was the only one who went after his moves. The next day he saw it at a glance and the stick came down on my elbow. The day after that, when he practised that stance in the yard, he did it a little slower. He didn't say he was teaching me, and I didn't say I had noticed.

We even fought the same way. Once I didn't speak to him for three days, and he didn't speak to me. We still fought in the yard every morning, and afterward went our separate ways. On the fourth day there was half a cake on the post, and neither of us mentioned it.

I was very pleased with myself in those days, sure that I was the one on the whole mountain most like him. I had to go a long way down before I learned that, to him, this was no good thing.

After the standing you learned the rest. The Blade-hall taught cutting. In the Bow-hall was Old Bow, a white-haired woman who had never praised anyone, and all she ever said was "Another hundred." The old instructor in the Art-hall had two fingers left, so we all called him Two-Fingers. He taught us to put fire and ice on a sword, and every year some child burned off their eyebrows. The Way-hall was the dullest. The instructor spent most of his time lying flat on the ground, so we called him Floorboards, and what he taught was looking at floors: which stone was the wrong colour, which corridor was too clean, how to find your way back out first when you got to a strange place. I came first in every hall. There were only eleven children left on the mountain by then, so it wasn't hard to come first, but nobody told me that.

Then the eleven got fewer, one at a time. The winter I was thirteen, Stammer coughed himself to death. The year I was fifteen, the twins went over the wall in the middle of the night. In the morning their beds were empty. The great hall didn't send anyone after them, and all the Grand Master said was "That saves two rations." Big Lad was two years older than me and was always stealing Mavis's bread. When I was fourteen I broke his nose, and after that he was very polite to me. The year I was sixteen, the crown's recruiting officer came. The crown wouldn't send money, but it was quick enough asking for people. He took Big Lad and two others.

By the year I went down the Well, the only ones left on the mountain were me, Mavis and one little one, and Mavis and I weren't really children anymore.

The instructors all praised me. My master never did. The day I split my post, I ran to show him. He gave it one look, said "Hand," and rapped my hand with a stick.

Back to floor two. The same afternoon I passed that stone ring, I came to a room with a long table in it, six chairs, and bowls set out. A family sat around the table, the father at the head and the youngest child on its mother's lap. They were all in their burial clothes, and some had rotted past recognizing. The bowls were empty. All of them were looking at the door.

I raised my sword and waited for them to come at me. They didn't move. I took a step forward, and the father slowly turned his head. His jaw worked and made a very dry sound. It was only later that I worked out what it meant. He was telling me to sit down.

I killed all of them, starting with the nearest and leaving the youngest for last. They moved slowly. None of them fought back.

I said just now that I was doing very well. Now you know what that means.

That night I slept in a small half-collapsed room. There was a table in it with a ledger lying open, and an inkwell tipped over beside it, long dry. I guessed this was the village elder's cellar. Upstairs probably wasn't safe anymore, so he had brought his ledger down to keep it here.

The front of the ledger was ordinary things: so many sacks of wheat, so many sheep, who owed whom a chicken. On the last page the writing suddenly got very large and very messy.

I read "one more guardian is sent" and stopped for a moment before it came to me that he meant me.

When he wrote that line, the Order's rider had only just left, and I was probably still up on the mountain packing. He sat here listening to the noises upstairs and entered it in the ledger, right after the sacks of wheat and the sheep. He was waiting for me. He waited until he had filled the last page, and I still hadn't come. I didn't see him in the village either.

"He would not say when the last came back."

I read that line a few more times, then tore out the page. I told myself I was taking it because it mentioned me.

I didn't need the rider to tell me, in any case. I grew up in the great hall on the mountain. The nameplates of all the guardians hung on the wall, and under each plate was a nail, meant for the date they came back. Every nail was bare. When I was small I asked the Ward-hall instructor why, the same one who later died at the mill. He said, "Because they're still watching over us," and patted my head. I didn't understand then. I just thought it was a grand thing to say. Looking back, his eyes were as full of sorrow as mine are now.

## Floor Three

Yesterday you asked me if the dead people's supper was real. It was. You looked ill after you read it, and you took the wrong path driving the sheep home. Do you still want to read today? You say yes. Then read.

There was a body on floor three wearing a very good pair of boots, and I didn't take them. I've done few stupider things in my life. My own boots gave out on floor ten.

He sat against the wall with a pack beside him. He wasn't from the Order. By his gear he was an adventurer from the south, the kind you see in taverns, who'll tell you after three drinks what he's off to dig up. In the pack were half a loaf of hard bread, a small knife, a coil of rope and a sheet of paper.

::lore.f3

I ate the half loaf.

At the very bottom of the pack was a stone wrapped in cloth, the size of an egg, the colour of coals about to go out. It gave me a start when I opened the cloth, because it was warm. This man had been dead a long time. His body had dried out. And the stone was still warm, as if someone had just been holding it.

I held it up to my sword. The sword was Order issue, and it had an empty socket in the guard. When I was small I thought that was a defect. As soon as the stone came near, it slid in on its own and sat tight, as if the socket had been cut to its shape.

The sword got lighter.

I swung it a couple of times. It wasn't just lighter. Every stroke came a little faster and a little truer than I expected. I was so pleased I nearly laughed. So this was the gold from the stories, I thought. No wonder the man came.

Remember this part. If you forget it later, come back and read it again. In all my life, the thing that has frightened me most is how pleased I was at that moment.

That afternoon I opened the first chest on this floor.

You'll probably ask how there come to be chests underground. I asked too, and later I worked it out. For a thousand years the people coming down have brought things with them: supplies, weapons, purses, armor tied up in bundles. The people get swallowed and the things stay. The things get moved somewhere else, packed into chests, and left by the side of the road. As for who moves them, I don't think anybody does. You always get a bit of meat stuck between your teeth, and nobody put it there on purpose.

In the chest were gold coins and a usable short bow. Under the coins was a piece of paper.

Only afterward did it strike me how odd that chest was. The space around it was perfectly clean and nothing was guarding it. Good luck like that doesn't come for free.

As I bent down for the paper, a little dust fell from above. I looked up. Four ghouls were clinging to the ceiling, fingers and toes hooked into the cracks in the stone, heads upside down, looking right at me.

The first one dropped straight onto my back.

They were thinner than the half-rotted ones on floor one, and faster, with claws half as long as my hand. It slammed me down on the chest. My chin hit the iron bands and I bit through my tongue. Claws came down between my shoulder blades, the leather split, and I heard my own back make the sound of tearing cloth. I drove my elbow backward into something soft, rolled over and kicked it off. The second and third landed, one on each side. The fourth stayed on the ceiling and crawled down the wall behind me.

I grabbed the short bow. I had no arrows, so I used it as a club and hit the one on the left in the face. The bowstring cut into its eye socket. The one on the right bit my calf. I backed up toward the wall dragging it with me, and the one crawling down from behind ran onto my sword. It had run itself through, and it still came crawling up the blade, holding on, until it reached my hand and opened its mouth to bite. I let go of the sword, took its head in both hands and slammed it into the wall, once, twice, three times, until all I was holding was something wet.

When it was over I got my sword back and sat on the chest shaking. The short bow's string had snapped. I didn't find a new string and half a bag of arrows until just before floor five.

I thought of Mavis. When we were eight, she took me to steal bread from the kitchen in the middle of the night. There was clearly no one outside the door, and still she wouldn't go in. She squatted there looking for a long time. I asked what she was waiting for. She said, "That table's set too nicely." As it turned out, the cook was sitting in the dark that night with a wooden spoon, waiting for us. Mavis ran for it and I got ten whacks.

She was right. Anything set out too nicely has someone waiting behind it.

::note.p08

That was all of it. The writing was pressed so hard that the last word went through the paper.

I looked around. This was only floor three. Not far above my head were other people's pickles.

This man thought this was the bottom of the Well.

Nobody knows what became of him. Maybe he got this far and couldn't go on, so he told himself this was the bottom. Maybe he truly believed it, because it did look as though there was no way forward. Who his daughter was, where she lived, whether she knew her father had gone down the Well, I have no idea.

I kept that paper too. At the time I had no thought of passing on his message. I just didn't want to leave it there.

A little further on, I came to another stone ring.

This time there was someone beside it.

My sword was already up, and he didn't move. He was a very old man, sitting on a rock in a ragged robe that covered his feet. A cloth was spread on the ground in front of him with some bottles lined up on it. They were red, the same as the ones the cook gave me. There were also a few old knives and a few pieces of armor nobody would want.

"Twelve a bottle," he said.

I asked who he was. He said he sold things. I asked what he was doing here, with nothing but dead men down here, and how he had stayed alive.

"It's business," he said.

I asked where the potions came from. It's business, he said. I asked if he had seen a knight go down, ten years ago, with a crooked nose. He looked up at me. His eyes were very cloudy, and I couldn't tell where they were looking.

"Twelve a bottle."

He gave me the creeps. But I had gold from the chest, and only two potions left. I bought three bottles. He put the coins inside his robe, and I didn't hear them clink.

When I'd gone a good way off I looked back. He was still sitting there, in exactly the same position.

After that I saw him beside every stone ring. He was there on floor five, on floor ten, on floor twenty. The same ragged robe, the same face, the same words. I went down thirty-five floors, and he got to every one of them before I did. I didn't dare think about that until somewhere past floor ten, and I never asked him another question. But I went on buying from him. Without the potions I couldn't have gone on.

That night before I slept, I found a piece missing from the hard bread in my pack. It wasn't rats. It had been cut with a knife, very neatly. I assumed I'd cut it myself and forgotten.

## Floor Four

Floor four is where I started counting steps.

It was because of a line on a wall. The path on this floor led to a staircase, and on the wall in front of it someone had written in chalk. Chalk doesn't fade much underground, so there was no telling how long ago it was written.

::lore.f4

I laughed. I'm not one to take that sort of thing on faith, so I tried it right away.

I went back to the top of the stairs and counted on the way down. Thirty-one steps. At the bottom I turned around and counted on the way up.

Thirty-seven.

I stood at the top for a while. Then I went down again, very slowly, knocking the wall with my scabbard at every step. Thirty-four. I came back up. Twenty-nine.

I stopped trying.

I sat down and started to feel cold. It isn't actually cold underground. I was thinking about the person who wrote in chalk. He had stood here counting too, and sat down just like this, and then he wrote the result on the wall for the next one. He didn't write "Run." He didn't write "Don't go down." He just told you the facts and left you to do your own counting.

I liked him very much. He was the first honest man I met down there.

I got this habit of counting things from Floorboards.

One day every month the Way-hall made us walk the cellars in the dark and report afterward: how many corners, how many paces, where there was water. If you got it wrong you walked it again. Mavis always made up her report and got locked in the cellar for the night. I used to sneak down in the middle of the night with some bread and push it under the door, and she would play dice with me from inside, guessing the numbers by the sound. That's how she came to owe me the twelve gold coins.

Floorboards said something I've always remembered. He was lying flat on the ground and didn't look up. "Places lie," he said. "Numbers don't."

He was wrong about that. The stairs on this floor were lying to my numbers. But he wasn't entirely wrong, because you need to have a number before you can tell whether it's lying.

When I was ten, Floorboards took us down the mountain to walk a real cellar. It was an old wine cellar in the village next to this one. He gave the owner a copper coin, took a few of us children inside, shut the door, and shouted from outside, "How many paces?"

It was too dark in there to see your own hand. Stammer started crying. Big Lad said he wasn't scared, and his voice was shaking. I felt my way along the wall, counting as I went. I walked to the end and back, and reported through the crack in the door. "Forty-two paces, one corner, water on the left."

The door opened. Floorboards lay on the ground looking at my feet for a long time, and then he said, "You took two extra paces on the way back."

I said that was impossible.

He said, "You got scared. Your steps got smaller."

That was the day I learned that numbers get scared along with you.

I decided to keep counting, accurate or not, one number for each step I went down. I needed something of my own. The sword was the Order's, the stone was a dead man's, the road was the Well's. Only this number was mine.

I had only just taken out my charcoal when something started coming up the stairs.

At first it was only footsteps, one at a time, very slow, very even. I took it for skeletons, stood at the top with my sword, and planned to cut them down one by one. The first one came up, I cut it, and it went over backward, rolled down the stairs and knocked over the one behind it. The ones behind kept coming. The second and the third went down easily. At the seventh I noticed something was wrong. There were more and more footsteps below, I couldn't cut them down fast enough, and the sound wasn't only coming from below.

It was also in the corridor behind me.

An old farmer in his burial clothes, holding a shovel still dripping with dirt. Behind him came a little girl and two women. They had walked over from the cellars nearby. They didn't walk fast. They just kept walking. I was caught at the head of the stairs with them on both sides.

The stairs were narrow and I had nowhere to go. The shovel hit my arm and my hand went dead, and I nearly dropped the sword. My back was against the wall with the chalk writing on it. A hand grabbed my hair and pulled, and my head hit the wall. I saw white spots. The little girl wrapped herself around my leg and bit. She was only that big, and she bit very hard.

I kicked her off.

I'm telling you this because I still remember the sound she made hitting the wall. I had no time to think about it then. I knocked the old farmer aside with my shoulder, turned and ran down the corridor. Twenty paces on I looked back and threw my first fire. It was the first time I'd used fire in a real fight since coming down. Two-Fingers always said to write down first what you meant to do, and I certainly had no time for that. The fire went wide and burned on the wall. The second one hit, and it caught those dried-out shrouds.

They still came on toward me with the fire on them, walking just as slowly.

I stood and watched them burn, and only then noticed I had been counting all along. Eleven. I wrote that number on my sleeve too, and later I rubbed it out. That wasn't a number I meant to keep.

From that day on, every time I reached a new floor, I wrote the number on the inside of my sleeve with a stick of charcoal. When one sleeve filled up I moved to the other. When I finished those stairs I wrote down my first number: thirty-one.

I hadn't counted the floors above. For now, just remember that I was counting steps. At the end you'll see why I've gone on about it this much.

Before I slept that night, I heard a dull sound from far below. One knock. A long gap. Another knock.

I heard another sound too, very close, in the corridor behind me.

Someone sneezed.

I ran out with my sword. There was nothing in the corridor except a few footprints in the dust, a little bigger than mine. I stood and listened for a long time and heard nothing more. I told myself the dead don't sneeze, so I must have heard wrong.

The dull sound went on, as if someone had a hammer, and knocked at a door once in a long while.

## Floor Five

The stone in the walls had changed. Here it was cut square, with lead poured into the joints, and along both sides ran rows of tombs. Each niche held one man in armor and helmet, with his hands on his weapon. The first thing I noticed was how neatly they lay. The dead don't lie that neatly unless someone has ordered them to.

At the end of the corridor an especially large gravestone stood in front of a flight of stairs.

::lore.f5

I read it twice. The second time I read it aloud.

The dull sound stopped.

::panel.f1-2

When I climbed the stairs he was already standing there, just as I drew him. Both hands rested on a great hammer, and a shield bigger than a door leaned against his leg. His cloak had rotted into strips, a pair of curved horns had grown out of his helmet, and under it was a bare skull with two points of red light in the sockets. Other people's skulls were set into his shoulder plates. Behind him was a very large gate. The sound I'd been hearing was him striking the floor with the hammer's handle, once and then again, like a man on watch calling the hour. I think now he probably was calling the hour. He'd been doing it for three hundred years.

He said nothing. He just raised the hammer.

All around me the tombs began to stir at once.

I ran up, thinking that once I knocked him down I could go on. I was twenty-two and first in all five halls. I had cut down nearly two hundred dead on the first four floors, and a potion had fixed every wound I took.

Halfway there, he came at me with the shield.

I had never seen anything that big move that fast. From ten paces away he was suddenly right in front of me, and all I had time to do was raise my sword. When that shield hit me, I thought a wall had fallen over. I flew through the air, landed on the steps and rolled down them. All the ribs on my left side were broken, and every breath was like breathing in a handful of broken glass.

I got up with everything swaying in front of me. A skeleton's hand came out of a niche beside me and took me by the ankle. He didn't chase me. He stood on the stairs, raised the great hammer, and brought it down on the ground.

The ground jumped.

My knees gave way and I went down, ears ringing, unable to hear anything. Three more sat up in their niches. I cut one down and the other two pinned my arms. I saw him coming down, one step at a time, in no hurry, with the hammer raised very high. I tried to raise my sword and my arms were held.

I remember the hammer's head had a notch in it.

Then I died.

I want to be clear about this. I didn't pass out, and I didn't nearly die. The hammer landed on my chest, I heard myself split open, blood sprayed out of my mouth, and the world shrank to one small point. Then the point was gone too.

And then I took a breath.

I was lying on the ground under a stone ceiling. I jumped up and pawed at my chest with both hands. My chest was whole and my bones were whole, but they still hurt, so badly that I squatted down and threw up. I looked down. I was sitting in a stone ring glowing white, the one at the entrance to floor five that I'd stepped through on my way past. The old peddler was sitting beside it, looking at me.

"Twelve a bottle," he said.

I sat in that ring for a long time, shaking. I was trained by the Ward-hall. The instructors taught me how to die usefully. No one ever taught me what to do after you're dead. I had thought the guardians before me left these rings as a place to rest. Now I knew they did something else as well.

I didn't know whether this was a gift or something else. You'll read the answer later on. I didn't find it out myself until floor twenty-eight.

I turned my sleeve over and on the other side I made a stroke with the charcoal. That was a different number. I kept that number all the way to the bottom too.

Before I went up the second time, I thought for a long while.

The captain of the Watch was only one man, but he had a whole corridor of soldiers, a shield bigger than the gate, and a hammer that could make the ground jump with one blow. I couldn't get near him. My swordwork had nothing to do with how I beat him. I beat him because he only knew one thing.

First I tried something. I took out the bow, stood halfway up the stairs and shot at him. He raised his shield and charged down at me. I rolled aside and he went past me and smashed through a whole row of niches, and the flying stone left my face covered in blood. But when he got to the seventh step from the bottom, he stopped, turned around, walked slowly back and stood in front of the gate again.

He didn't chase me.

He couldn't. His orders were to let no one past, so he guarded that gate, and when he got too far from it he walked back. For three hundred years, this was the only thing his body had remembered.

I started shooting at the joints of his gauntlets, one arrow after another. I'd shoot, he'd charge, and I'd roll away. He'd walk back, and I'd shoot again. His men came down in waves and I fought them at the foot of the stairs. The skeletons weren't so bad. The later ones wore heavy armor, and my sword bounced off it so hard the web of my thumb split open. I had to lead those onto the path of his charge and let him smash them himself.

When I ran out of arrows I went and picked them up. I drank one potion and then another. Once I rolled too slowly and the edge of the shield clipped my left leg. The whole leg went numb. I dragged it for a dozen paces, and his hammer came down right where I had just been. A chip of stone went through my cheek, and my tongue could taste my own teeth.

It wasn't a glorious way to fight. Any instructor in the Order would have cursed me for it. But I had no wish to see the notch in that hammer again.

By the end his right gauntlet bristled like a hedgehog, and that hand had started to shake when it raised the hammer. He didn't dodge, and he didn't stand anywhere else. He stood there holding up that shield until he couldn't hold it up anymore. The bottom edge touched the ground with a very loud noise.

Then I walked up.

My sword went in under his helmet and the two points of red light went out. He didn't fall. He lowered the hammer slowly to the ground, set it straight, and only then knelt, the way you'd hand over to the next watch.

Then a thread of smoke came out of the gaps in his armor.

The smoke was white and very clean. I stood still. I had never seen anything like it. It drifted up in front of my face and stopped, and I heard a very tired voice.

::soul.f5#0

I said yes. I don't know if he could hear me.

::soul.f5#1

I stood holding my sword with the blood running down my arm into my glove, and suddenly I didn't know what I had just killed. He wasn't a monster. Someone told him not to let anyone past, and he didn't let anyone past, for three hundred years.

And I had come to bring him down.

::soul.f5#2

The smoke faded.

The gate behind him opened a crack, and a wind came out that had been shut in for a long time.

I left his hammer where it was, set very straight.

Then I went on down. The number on my sleeve had reached two hundred and twelve.
