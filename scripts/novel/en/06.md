# Book Seven: Echoes

Floors 31 to 35

## Floor Thirty-One

It rained today and you didn't come, so I wrote a bit more.

::panel.f31-1

This picture took me longer than any other. The red-haired one standing on the broken step on the left is me. She looks very small because she was very small.

When I reached the bottom of those stairs I looked back. The steps I had just come down were dropping away one at a time into the fog below, without a sound. I looked up. From underneath, the temple floor was only a thin shell. There was a hole in the shell, and out of the hole came an arm as thick as a tower, reaching from up there to very, very far below.

I followed that arm down with my eyes.

Then I saw it.

I had always thought the bottom of the Well was a place, with a floor and walls and something sitting inside. There was something down there, all right, but my drawing can't show you how big it is. It started where I could see and went on to where I couldn't, like hills made of piled black roots.

There were eyes all over the roots.

Some were as big as my head, and some were smaller than this page. Every one was open, and every one was looking the same way.

They were all looking at me.

I stood there waiting for it to move. It didn't move. It didn't need to.

At the very bottom, in the middle of that heap, was a thin curved line of gold. I narrowed my eyes. It was the edge of a shield. Beside it was a long straight line of light. That was a sword.

Everything about that thing was crooked. That was the one straight place.

Parts of the temple were still around me, corridors and altars hanging in the air. I walked a few steps and saw a stone tablet lying on one of the altars.

::lore.f31

It is no demon.

I read that line out loud once. I wanted to be angry and didn't know who at.

All the way down, killing as I went, I had been thinking one thing: when I reach the bottom, I'll personally butcher whatever killed them all. For my master, for the captain, for those hands on the wall on the nineteenth floor. I was going to ask it to its face why. And there was no one to ask. It didn't hate us. It didn't even know we were there. It was like water running downhill. It only kept trying to get back to a time when there were no edges at all.

That's worse than a demon. A demon, at least, you can hate.

Then the ground started to get hot.

Something was coming down the corridor, leaving a pool of lava with every step. I knew that tread.

It was the mountain.

I had beaten him on the fifteenth floor. I had watched his smoke blow away. And here he came again, bigger than before, grey all over, as if he'd been modelled from memory. The same fists, the same wall of fire, even the same walk. But this time he wasn't like rain at all.

He chased me. He worked out which way I was going to jump.

But I wasn't the me of the fifteenth floor either, and I finished it faster than the first time. I drove my sword in and the mountain fell. The smoke this time was grey, thin and short, and it could hardly rise.

::soul.f31#0

It was still that blacksmith's voice, very small and very annoyed, like someone woken up.

So he really was here. It had swallowed him, and now it had brought him out to show me.

The captain and the priest didn't appear. Probably they had fallen too far from here for it to swallow them. It could only collect the ones who came too close.

I thought about that for a long time. I still had closer to go.

## Floor Thirty-Two

This floor had the Abyss Priests, those things in long robes walking back and forth. They used to be people. They built a shrine here and prayed to those eyes. Their scripture was cut into a broken pillar.

::lore.f32

"It heareth not."

I sat down against the pillar. I suppose I had spoken to it too: I had cut that crack on the twelfth floor, and I had said those words three times on the bridge. It only listened, learned, and spat them back at me. It never once answered me. Don't you go thinking it's answering you, either.

The priests were still there, calmer than anyone I had seen on the whole way down. They tended their altars and lit candles that couldn't be lit. When they saw me, they raised a hand and sent a lump of black stuff my way, as if shooing a fly, not the least bit angry.

I grabbed one, pushed him against the pillar and pulled back his hood. Inside was a very young human face with its eyes wide open. There were no eyeballs, only two holes. I asked him: you worship it, so what has it given you?

His mouth moved. "It gives nothing," he said, in a voice like water dripping on stone. "That is why it is real."

I let him go. He straightened his robe and went back to lighting candles.

That was the craziest thing I heard down there, and to this day I can't think how to argue with it.

I sat and waited for my master that day. I knew he would come.

He walked out of the fog, and I could see at once that something was wrong.

His cloak was grey. His sword caught fire, fire, then ice, then thunder, in the right order, with the right footwork. I drew. He charged.

His elbow was high.

I nearly laughed, and I nearly cried. His elbow was up high, very correct, just like the drawings in the book. There wasn't a fault in the whole style. It had put my moves and his together and ground away everything bad, and ground it very beautifully.

It was the finest swordsmanship in the world, and it wasn't him at all.

He had long arms and had to keep his elbow low to reach the inside angle. That was his flaw, and it was his signature too. He had spent thirty years working it out stroke by stroke, and he used a stick on me to stop me copying it.

This thing didn't know why the elbow was low, so it had corrected it.

I fought fast and without much effort. It knew everything, so I went only for what it hadn't learned. It only guarded the places I knew how to cut, so I cut under that too-high elbow. The first time I fought him I lost the use of a hand. This time I didn't get a scratch.

When it fell, only a little grey smoke came out. I knelt there, wanting to hear that voice through the nose one more time.

::soul.f32#0

I nodded.

I knew. I had known from the start.

I sat for a while, then said to the smoke, which had already gone, "Thank you for that elbow." The thirty-second floor was very quiet. No one answered. If the Eye heard, let it learn one more thing.

## Floor Thirty-Three

The thirty-third floor had nearly stopped being a place. There was still a path, but no walls on either side, only soft black stuff that kept breathing. I walked very carefully and tried not to touch it.

I found a very old stone tablet, with writing older than any I had seen. It took me a long time to make it out.

::lore.f33

I copied those lines onto my sleeve and sat down to look at them.

**and became both.**

That's her story, all of it, in one sentence. Whoever wrote it was probably there, maybe the last person to see her to the mouth of the Well. He didn't write whether she was afraid. He didn't write whether she looked back.

The tapestry on my back got a little heavier.

**This picture is a guess.** But of all of them I'm surest of this one, because I saw how it ended with my own eyes.

It must have been a cold morning. The mouth of the Well was still open ground, with no village and no chains. Half the kingdom had already fallen. The ministers stood a long way off, and no one dared come closer. Aislinn was in full armour, shield in her left hand, sword in her right. Those two things were too heavy. She had complained they were too heavy since she was small.

The Queen walked beside her. By custom the Queen should have stayed on the platform and waved, but she came down from the platform and walked all the way to the Well, and no one dared stop her.

They didn't say anything. Whatever needed saying they had already said, in the words only they understood. At the Well, Aislinn needed a free hand to climb over the rim, so she stuck her sword in the ground.

Maeve took that hand.

I guess they stood like that for a moment. One hand that held a pen, one hand all callus. Someone nearby started coughing. Someone reminded Her Majesty that it was time.

Aislinn drew her hand back, pulled up her sword and went down.

She didn't look back. A knight doesn't look back. That's the rule. Whether she really looked back or not, I don't know. The tablet doesn't say.

Nearby was a chest, older than anything around it. By then I no longer found it odd that there were chests in a place like that. There was nothing in it but one sheet of paper, placed exactly in the middle.

::note.p10

I looked at it for a long time.

I had read so many people's words on the way down. Some told me not to come. Some told me to watch the sky. Some told me shields break. But no one had ever said this to me.

All my life people had taught me how to go down the Well, how to guard, how to hold. Even my master's letter only told me not to come. No one had ever told me that once I got here, I could go back.

Go home.

I had never thought about going home, not once. So I had been planning all along to come here and die, and hadn't known it. It took thirty-three floors for me to notice that I had prepared nothing for the way back.

I stood up and wrote one word on the back of that paper with my charcoal, very badly: Yes.

I put the paper away. I was going to carry it up myself.

Then I heard silk pulling tight.

She came down from above, grey-white, made entirely of threads, and I could see through her body to what was behind. She looked just as she does in the picture. Her face was empty, only a knot of thread. She didn't look at my hand or at my hair. Her eight legs came down one after another, each one horribly exact.

That moment's hesitation on the twenty-fifth floor was gone.

I shouted as I fought. I think I had gone a little mad. "Someone remembers! Up there it's everywhere! My name is Aisling! Do you hear me?"

Her leg went through my shoulder. There was no reaction at all.

I cut off her last leg and she came apart, all of her falling to the floor as thread. A very faint wisp of grey smoke rose out of it. I crawled forward a little, gasping, and put my ear close.

::soul.f33#0

She hadn't heard.

She couldn't hear any more. When it swallowed her, it ate those thousand years of longing as well. All that was left was this one small wisp, which still remembered to hurry me.

Make haste.

I got up and started to run.

## Floor Thirty-Four

There was only the last stretch left.

This floor was little more than one road going down. Halfway along, beside the road, was a stone with writing cut into it, very deep and very messy. Everything around it was soft. Only this one piece was still stone.

::lore.f34

I looked at it for a long time. Then I sat down and took out every paper I had.

Some were torn out, some I had copied, and some were dead people's words I had written on my sleeve. There in front of that stone I laid them on the ground, one at a time. It was the first time since going down the Well that I had spread them all out.

In a thousand years, no one had done this.

The captain had only his orders. The priest had only his scripture, and had torn a page out of it. The forgemaster had only his mountain. The Grand Master had only the sealed letter locked in his iron chest. Each guardian knew only his own stretch, and the more he knew, the less he dared tell anyone. Everyone held one small piece and took it for the whole.

I was the only one who had picked it all up along the way. That had nothing to do with being clever. I just have a habit of picking things up.

I put the parchment roll from the tenth floor in the middle.

**What hath no walls cannot be held; what hath walls can.**

I copied the words from the stone in front of me and put them beside it.

**It weareth her shape, for hers is the only shape it hath ever known.**

I stared at those two pieces of paper.

My master had said: **Seal it no more. End it.** I had never understood that. The thing had no edges and nowhere to cut. How do you kill something that's everywhere? So no one had ever thought of killing it. They all came to seal it, to hold it, to be a wall, and that was all the Order taught.

But now it had edges.

Aislinn walked in and refused to be digested, so it had to grow around her. It grew for a thousand years, and it grew into her shape. It got shoulders and hands, an up and a down, a left and a right, and it was carrying her shield and sword. A thing with no shape had become a thing with a shape. Day by day, she had turned it into something you could get hold of.

**She held for a thousand years, and that held it up to the day it could be killed.**

Did she know that herself? I asked her later. I'll tell you shortly what she said.

For a thousand years she had been waiting for someone who wasn't coming to take her place.

And every guardian came to take her place.

I sat there with both hands shaking, warm all over, the black on my right arm up to the elbow. I gathered up the papers one by one, in order. I had thought I was collecting things off the dead. I had been collecting a map.

That's why I have to give you all of them.

Then something dropped from above and stood in the middle of the road.

The same wings, the same horns, grey all over, bigger than on the thirtieth floor. This time his copies were using my moves.

I hardly felt like standing up. I was only afraid the papers in my coat would burn, so I fought fast. Before the rain of fire came down, I had already looked up.

His head hit the ground. The grey smoke was a single thread.

::soul.f34#0

"I know," I said. Then I got up and walked on.

## Floor Thirty-Five

I came down the last step and wrote the number on my sleeve.

Eleven thousand four hundred and seven.

The stairs ended there. In front of me there was no wall, there was nothing, and you couldn't even call it dark. The lamplight went out and found nowhere to land and just disappeared. The ground was soft underfoot and now and then moved a little, like standing on a sleeping cow.

I told you on the first floor that the whole village sits on a mouth. Now I had walked into its belly.

Then I heard it.

Very slow, very long. I stopped and held my breath. It wasn't me.

A breath in.

A long, long time.

A breath out.

It was a person breathing. **There is a woman in there. I heard her breathing.** The one who wrote that note on the twenty-fourth floor had got this far too, and had stood where I was standing.

I walked on. All around me, one by one, the eyes lit up. They all turned to look at me, without the smallest sound.

When it stood up, I thought the ground was tilting.

Its lower half had four legs as big as city walls. But from the waist up it was shaped like a person. A far, far too big person, piled up out of black stuff, with shoulders and arms and something you could just about call a head. It didn't know what a person was. It had only grown around one for a thousand years and memorised every inch of her.

It held a sword in its right hand and raised a shield in its left.

I knew that stance.

Left foot forward, shield at chin height, elbow against the ribs, weight on the back foot. Of course I knew it. I had stood like that in the yard at seven years old until my legs shook, at the third post. **The Ward-hall was founded in the name of her who went first.** The Ward-hall's stance was her stance. For a thousand years the Order had handed down the way she stood to every child, and the children didn't know whose it was. They only knew it was the rule.

Now it stood in front of me like that.

I looked down. Without noticing, I had taken the same stance.

We faced each other like that for a moment.

It moved.

I'm not going to take you through this fight cut by cut. One, I don't remember it clearly. Two, you wouldn't sleep. I'll tell you three things.

The first thing.

Its claws were bigger than a house, and one swing of the great sword cracked the ground in a line. Burning rocks fell from the sky, and I looked up first. I used everything I had learned on the way: the captain taught me not to meet force head on, the priest taught me to run with the light, the mountain taught me to climb, my master taught me to parry. But every cut I made was stopped by that shield, with a very dull sound. It kept the shield up the whole time, in her stance. My arms were about to break.

**Whoever readeth this: the shield breaketh first.**

On the twenty-ninth floor I had taken that for a tip about fighting. He was telling the next one where to hit.

When one person is holding on, he keeps that shield up, keeps it up even when his arm has broken. No one who came down dared hit that shield, because it was hers. They had all come out of the Ward-hall. They had all come to protect that shield.

I hit the shield.

I put all my strength into one spot. The shield was covered in a crust of black shell as thick as a wall, and it came apart piece by piece under my sword. I hit it for a very long time. There was a tremendous crash, the whole crust broke, and its left arm suddenly went slack. The shield dropped, rolled twice on the ground and fell over, with a very clear ring.

That was the first clear sound I had heard in that place.

The shield hadn't broken. Only the crust had. It was a round old shield, with a few new cracks of gold.

The breathing missed a beat, and then the whole thing went mad.

The second thing.

Without the shield it got faster. It dropped every stance and went crashing everywhere on four legs and a tail, its claws coming one after another. I don't remember how I stayed alive. I only remember my left arm broke, and I tied it to my belt. I kept my eyes on its right hand, on that sword. It had never let go of that sword. She had never let go. I cut at its wrist, I don't know how many times.

The sword fell.

It stuck straight into the ground, the only straight thing there. The breathing changed again, very, very faint. I knew she was close to giving out. That was her last line.

Its back split open and wings spread out.

By now it no longer had a person's shape. With no shield and no sword, nothing was telling it what it was. It began to loosen. Its edges began to melt. The sky was all fire and ice, too thick to dodge, and then thunder came too. Fire, then ice, then thunder, my order.

The third thing.

I couldn't hit it any more.

It dodged every cut I made. Those eyes had watched me the whole way, through thirty-four floors, watched every move, watched me change moves, watched me change them again. However I started, it got there first. It knew me better than my master did, and better than I did. And its edges were melting. In a little while it would be the Unbounded again, and then no one could ever kill it.

My right arm was black to the shoulder, and every stone on me was burning. I gripped the sword as hard as I have ever gripped anything.

I couldn't hit it. I gripped harder, and I still couldn't hit it.

**Thou hast grown. Thy grip is still too tight.**

I don't know if that was him talking or me remembering.

Seven years old, in the yard, early morning, my hands swollen like bread rolls. He crouched down, touched my hand with the stick and said, "Hold it like that and it only ever goes to one place."

It had watched me for thirty-four floors and learned every move I had. But every move I had came out of a hand gripping that sword tight. What it had learned was that hand.

My master beat me for five years to make me let go.

I thought of his letter. I thought of that wall in the gatehouse, and how hard it was to lower the knife once the point was on the wall.

I let go.

My fingers loosened one by one, and my arm came slowly down from the shoulder. The sword almost dropped, hanging from my knuckles by a little of its own weight. For the first time in my life I felt how long that sword was, and where its point was. It wanted to fall, so I let it fall.

It wasn't any move. I hadn't started anything. I didn't know myself where it would land.

So it didn't know either.

All the eyes went wide.

As the sword fell, I took one step forward. I didn't aim. I only listened to that breathing. Very slow, in the middle of that melting mass, there was a very small shape that hadn't melted, the shape of a person. She was still holding. In the whole thing, that was the only place with an edge left.

The sword came down on that edge.

It didn't scream. It had nothing left to scream with. All the eyes looked at me together. Then, starting from the farthest one, they closed, one by one.

I knelt there, I don't know how long. The sword had dropped to the ground and I didn't pick it up. It was so quiet I could hear my blood dripping.

The breathing stopped.

I thought she was dead. I crawled over the black stuff, which was starting to harden, to where the sword had fallen. There was a split there, and out of the split rose a wisp of smoke.

It was pale gold.

It rose very slowly, like someone standing up after sitting for a long time, and it stopped in front of me. It had no face, but I knew she was looking at me.

Her voice wasn't loud at all. It was very hoarse, as if it hadn't been used for a long time. It was just a very tired girl.

::soul.f35#0

I cried. I knelt there and cried.

She waited for me to finish. She must have been very used to waiting.

::soul.f35#1

I wiped my face and asked her: did you know? You held on for a thousand years so that it could be killed. Did you know?

She didn't answer. Maybe she didn't understand the question. Maybe she smiled a little. Whether smoke can smile, I don't know.

I said I would tell you what she said. This is her answer: she said nothing.

Then I remembered. There were words I had carried all the way here. I untied the tapestry from my back and opened it out. Half of it had burned and the rest was soaked with blood, but the two girls were still there. I held it up in front of her.

"She told me to tell you," I said, and my voice was barely a voice, "she never left."

The wisp of smoke did not move at all.

Then, slowly, it drifted over, up to the worn place in the tapestry, very close, very lightly.

::soul.f35#2

I looked up.

Only then did I see, in the grey beside her, another faint wisp of smoke. It must have travelled a long time to get here. It was thinner and shorter, and it came to points at the top, like a crown.

There she was. She really never had left.

I had one last thing to say.

"Someone remembers her name," I said to the thinner wisp. "It's everywhere. It's my name."

They didn't look back. They were looking at each other.

The golden wisp turned toward me and paused. These last words of hers were for me.
