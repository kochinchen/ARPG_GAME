# Book Seven: Echoes

Floors 31 to 35

## Floor Thirty-One

It rained today and you didn't come, so I wrote a bit more.

It rained all day. Toward evening I went to the Well to sing, and on the stone of the rim I saw two small wet handprints. They were fresh.

I stood there and looked at them for a long time.

I walked half an hour to your house. You were indoors, and your mother was cooking something. You saw me and looked down. I didn't say anything. I only asked how the little black lamb was. You said fine. So I walked back.

You don't have to explain. When I was twenty-two I heard the Well call me too. I'll finish writing this floor, and when you come tomorrow we'll read it together.

::panel.f31-1

This picture took me longer than any other. The red-haired one standing on the broken step on the left is me. She looks very small because she was very small.

When I reached the bottom of those stairs I looked back. The steps I had just come down were dropping away one at a time into the fog below, without a sound. I looked up. From underneath, the temple floor was only a thin shell. There was a hole in the shell, and out of the hole came an arm as thick as a tower, reaching from up there to very, very far below.

I followed that arm down with my eyes.

Then I saw it.

I had always thought the bottom of the Well was a place, with a floor and walls and something sitting inside. There was something down there, all right, but my drawing can't show you how big it is. It started where I could see and went on to where I couldn't, like hills made of piled black roots.

There were eyes all over the roots.

Some were as big as my head, and some were smaller than this page. Every one was open, and every one was looking the same way.

They were all looking at me.

I stood there waiting for it to move. It didn't move. It didn't need to.

At the very bottom, in the middle of that heap, was a thin curved line of gold. I narrowed my eyes. It was the edge of a shield. There was some faint pattern on its face, too far off to make out. Beside it was a long straight line of light. That was a sword.

Everything about that thing was crooked. That was the one straight place.

Parts of the temple were still around me, corridors and altars hanging in the air. I walked a few steps and saw a stone tablet lying on one of the altars.

Beside the altar knelt a row of knights in old armour, each one holding up a sword, none of them moving. I put my hand on my hilt and walked over slowly. When I reached the third one, they all stood up at once.

They were fallen templars, older than the ones on the twenty-third floor, so old the armour and the skin had grown together. The crest was still on their chests, though you could hardly recognise it. Seven of them made a ring around me, in step, with me in the middle. The hanging corridor was only four paces wide, and right beside it was the abyss.

I had learned that formation in the Ward-hall. Seven in rotation: the first blocks, the second cuts, the third fills the gap. Against that formation alone, the instructor said, there's only one answer: don't get surrounded. I was already surrounded.

I backed toward the edge of the corridor until my heels were over nothing. They came on, step by step. The first one rammed me with his shield. I didn't block. I grabbed the edge of his shield and threw all my weight sideways, went with his push and slung him right past me. He stepped on air. He didn't even shout. He dropped straight into the fog.

The formation had a corner missing. I charged out through the gap and turned with a wave of fire. The corridor was old timber and stone, and it caught, and the fire cut them off. The second one came through the flames with his body burning. I brought my sword down on his elbow, that elbow that always stays against the ribs, and it shattered.

By the time it was over, half that stretch of corridor had fallen in. I stood on what was left and looked down. Those eyes were still open, and not one had blinked.

I went back to the altar. The tablet was still there.

::lore.f31

It is no demon.

I read that line out loud once. I wanted to be angry and didn't know who at.

All the way down, killing as I went, I had been thinking one thing: when I reach the bottom, I'll personally butcher whatever killed them all. For my master, for the captain, for those hands on the wall on the nineteenth floor. I was going to ask it to its face why. And there was no one to ask. It didn't hate us. It didn't even know we were there. It was like water running downhill. It only kept trying to get back to a time when there were no edges at all.

That's worse than a demon. A demon, at least, you can hate.

Then the ground started to get hot.

Something was coming down the corridor, leaving a pool of lava with every step. I knew that tread.

It was the mountain.

I had beaten him on the fifteenth floor. I had watched his smoke blow away. And here he came again, bigger than before, grey all over, as if he'd been modelled from memory. The same fists, the same wall of fire, even the same walk. But on the fifteenth floor he had come down on me like rain, nothing personal. This time it was different.

He chased me. He worked out which way I was going to jump.

I jumped left, and his fist was already waiting on the left. I climbed onto the top of his foot, the way I knew so well, and on the third hold my foot went through. The crack I remembered had closed up, and my whole leg was caught in it. He looked down and aimed his vent at me. I didn't even have time to shout.

The tenth stroke.

I woke in the stone ring on the thirty-first floor, right beside those falling stairs. The old peddler was there too, sitting by a broken altar hanging in the air, with his cloth spread and his bottles in a neat row. Behind him was a drop with no bottom. He didn't seem to have noticed.

On my way back I thought: this isn't him. The mountain never worked me out. He never even noticed me. The thing at the bottom had taken his body and fitted it with an Eye that learned. It remembered me climbing his knee. It remembered I'd go for the chest.

Then I wouldn't go for the chest.

The second time, I stood still. His fists came down on both sides, where he had worked out I'd jump, and I didn't move one step. The flying rock covered my face in blood. It had counted on me moving. It hadn't counted on me staying put. When he took his next step, all his weight came down on his right foot, and only then did I run. I went in through the crack under that foot and drove my whole sword upward into the newest split in his sole, where the stone was still soft. Fire, then ice, then thunder, each harder than the last. His leg blew apart from the inside. The whole mountain toppled sideways and crushed half the hanging corridor.

He fell. I stood beside his face and put my sword in.

I wasn't the me of the fifteenth floor any more. The mountain fell apart. The smoke this time was grey, thin and short, and it could hardly rise.

::soul.f31#0

It was still that blacksmith's voice, very small and very annoyed, like someone woken up.

So he really was here. It had swallowed him, and now it had brought him out to show me.

I sat down to drink a potion, and as I reached out, I took a look at my hand. The black had nearly reached my elbow. Under the skin ran thin red lines, like the veins in the rock on the seventeenth floor. I made a fist. It was stronger than the other one. I drew my little knife across the back of it. No blood came, only a little red light, and the cut closed at once.

I pulled my sleeve down.

What will I do with this hand when it's all over? I thought. I thought about it for a moment and then stopped. I didn't know yet if there was any such thing as over.

The captain and the priest didn't appear. Probably they had fallen too far from here for it to swallow them. It could only collect the ones who came too close.

I thought about that for a long time. I still had closer to go.

## Floor Thirty-Two

You came today with your hands behind your back. I said show me your hands. You showed me. Both were dry, and already washed. I didn't say anything. I gave you this page.

What did you hear yesterday? You don't have to tell me. But now you know what it was you heard, don't you? It has only that one line. It only knows that one line. Next time you hear it, you can answer: I know. It won't hear you, but you will.

This floor had the Abyss Priests, those things in long robes walking back and forth. They used to be people. They built a shrine here and prayed to those eyes. Their scripture was cut into a broken pillar.

::lore.f32

"It heareth not."

I sat down against the pillar. I suppose I had spoken to it too: I had cut that crack on the twelfth floor, and on the bridge I had said the words I never finished saying to my master, three times. It only listened, learned, and spat them back at me. It never once answered me. Don't you go thinking it's answering you, either.

The priests were still there, calmer than anyone I had seen on the whole way down. They tended their altars and lit candles that couldn't be lit. When they saw me, they raised a hand and sent a lump of black stuff my way, as if shooing a fly, not the least bit angry.

I grabbed one, pushed him against the pillar and pulled back his hood. Inside was a very young human face with its eyes wide open. There were no eyeballs, only two holes. I asked him: you worship it, so what has it given you?

His mouth moved. "It gives nothing," he said, in a voice like water dripping on stone. "That is why it is real."

I let him go. He straightened his robe and went back to lighting candles.

That was the craziest thing I heard down there, and to this day I can't think how to argue with it.

Besides the priests, there were shadows.

Abyss shadow fiends have no shape. Mostly they lie flat on the wall and look like a patch that's a bit darker than the rest. I was resting against the pillar when my shadow moved. I hadn't moved.

It stood up from under my feet, taller than me, with very long hands. My shadow was gone. Its first stab came through from behind. I looked down and saw a length of something black sticking out of my belly. There was no blood, only cold. I jumped forward and cut at it. My sword went through and hit nothing. It stood there copying my stance, left foot forward, elbow against the ribs.

It only had a shape where there was light. My own lamp was giving it one. I threw the lamp down and stamped it out.

Everything went black. Far off, the purple lightning struck once and lit the whole shrine for an instant, and I saw seven or eight shadows on the wall, all turning to face me.

I shut my eyes. Floorboards had taught me: when you're afraid, your steps get smaller. I listened to my heart. I listened to them sliding over the stone, a very faint scrape. They liked to come from behind. I stood still, waited for that sound to get behind me, took one step forward and pushed fire out backhanded. The moment the fire lit, the thing had a shape, and I cut it in two. Then I put the fire out.

Light, cut, dark. Light, cut, dark. It went on for a long time, and each time the light came up, they were a little closer. By the end my hand was so burned I couldn't feel it. The lightning struck again. There was only one shadow left on the wall, my own, an ordinary one, very small.

I picked up the lamp. The glass was broken. I never had a lamp again. For the rest of the way I lit the road with the fire on my sword.

When the shadows were done, I sat and waited for my master. I knew he would come.

He walked out of the fog, and I could see at once that something was wrong.

His cloak was grey. His sword caught fire, fire, then ice, then thunder, in the right order, with the right footwork. I drew. He charged.

His elbow was high.

I nearly laughed, and I nearly cried. His elbow was up high, very correct, just like the drawings in the book. There wasn't a fault in the whole style. It had put my moves and his together and ground away everything bad, and ground it very beautifully.

It was the finest swordsmanship in the world, and it wasn't him at all.

He had long arms and had to keep his elbow low to reach the inside angle. That was his flaw, and it was his signature too. He had spent thirty years working it out stroke by stroke, and he used a stick on me to stop me copying it.

This thing didn't know why the elbow was low, so it had corrected it.

I fought fast and without much effort. It knew everything, so I went only for what it hadn't learned. It only guarded the places I knew how to cut, so I cut under that too-high elbow. The first time I fought him he broke my right hand. This time I didn't get a scratch.

When it fell, only a little grey smoke came out. I knelt there, wanting to hear that voice through the nose one more time.

::soul.f32#0

I nodded.

I knew. I had known from the start.

I sat for a while, then said to the smoke, which had already gone, "Thank you for that elbow." The thirty-second floor was very quiet. No one answered. If the Eye heard, let it learn one more thing.

## Floor Thirty-Three

Two-Fingers told a story once. Only once. I was nine, and he had just burned off Mavis's eyebrows, and I suppose he wanted to make us feel better.

He said that in the beginning the world had nothing in it. Not empty. There wasn't even empty. No up or down, no you or me. Then came a band of craftsmen with a rule and a pen, and they drew the first line. On this side of the line was earth, and on that side was sky. They kept drawing. They drew the rivers and the mountains and the village fences, and the edge of everyone's skin.

"And when they finished?" Mavis asked. She had no eyebrows, so she looked surprised the whole time.

Two-Fingers thought about it for a long while. He said the last line came out crooked, and one little piece didn't get closed up, and then they left.

I asked, so what about the piece that didn't get closed up?

He looked at his hand, the one with three fingers gone.

"That's why you stand at the posts," he said.

I had always thought that was just something you tell children. I had barely looked at that mural on the thirtieth floor. Now, walking this path, with no walls either side and soft ground underfoot, I remembered it, and I thought maybe he had known all along.

The thirty-third floor had nearly stopped being a place. There was still a path, but no walls on either side, only soft black stuff that kept breathing. I walked very carefully and tried not to touch it.

The path got narrower. The ground sank a little under each step and slowly sprang back, and it was slightly warm. After a while I realised it wasn't ground. I crouched and felt it. The surface was coarse, with very fine hairs on it, and it had a pulse.

A man in armour was sunk into the side of the path, half swallowed already. The black stuff had grown up to his chest like bark. The face inside the helmet was still there, an old man with his eyes shut, looking only asleep. As I went past, his lips moved.

I didn't stop to listen. I knew what it would say. It would be the thing I most wanted to hear.

I covered my ears and walked on. There were a lot more of them along that path. Some were just one hand. One hand lay palm up with a small charm resting on it, the kind we sold.

I found a very old stone tablet, with writing older than any I had seen. It took me a long time to make it out.

::lore.f33

I copied those lines onto my sleeve and sat down to look at them.

**and became both.**

A shield for a wall. A sword for a line.

I sat there, and my mouth started moving by itself. I wasn't trying to sing. The song just came out, the one I sang at seven at the third post, sang until my legs shook.

Stand the wall, draw the line, / till the Well is quiet, then you dine.

I stopped halfway through.

It was never about dinner.

It was about her. A thousand years ago someone made that rhyme, probably so people would remember. Then people forgot what it was about and went on singing it, to children at the posts, to babies down the mountain who wouldn't sleep. Mavis changed the last word to shit and got beaten for it more than once. The instructors didn't know what they were beating her for.

I sat in front of that tablet for a long time.

That's her story, all of it, in one sentence. Whoever wrote it was probably there, maybe the last person to see her to the mouth of the Well. He didn't write whether she was afraid. He didn't write whether she looked back.

The tapestry on my back got a little heavier.

The next part is a guess too, but I'm surer of it than of the others, because I saw how it ended with my own eyes.

It must have been a cold morning. The mouth of the Well was still open ground, with no village and no chains. Half the kingdom had already fallen. The ministers stood a long way off, and no one dared come closer. Aislinn was in full armour, shield in her left hand, sword in her right. Those two things were too heavy. She had complained they were too heavy since she was small.

The Queen walked beside her. By custom the Queen should have stayed on the platform and waved, but she came down from the platform and walked all the way to the Well, and no one dared stop her.

They didn't say anything. Whatever needed saying they had already said, in the words only they understood. At the Well, Aislinn needed a free hand to climb over the rim, so she stuck her sword in the ground.

Maeve took that hand.

I guess they stood like that for a moment. One hand that held a pen, one hand all callus. Someone nearby started coughing. Someone reminded Her Majesty that it was time.

Aislinn drew her hand back, pulled up her sword and went down.

She didn't look back. A knight doesn't look back. That's the rule. Whether she really looked back or not, I don't know. The tablet doesn't say.

Beside the tablet was a chest, older than anything around it. By then I no longer found it odd that there were chests in a place like that. There was nothing in it but one sheet of paper, placed exactly in the middle.

::note.p10

I looked at it for a long time.

I had read so many people's words on the way down. Some told me not to come. Some told me to watch the sky. Some told me shields break. But no one had ever said this to me.

All my life people had taught me how to go down the Well, how to guard, how to hold. Even my master's letter only told me not to come. No one had ever told me that once I got here, I could go back.

Go home.

I had never thought about going home, not once. So I had been planning all along to come here and die, and hadn't known it. It took thirty-three floors for me to notice that I had prepared nothing for the way back.

I stood up and wrote one word on the back of that paper with my charcoal, very badly: Yes.

I put the paper away. I was going to carry it up myself.

Then I heard silk pulling tight.

The Queen came down from above, grey-white, made entirely of threads, and I could see through her body to what was behind. Her face was empty, only a knot of thread. She didn't look at my hand or at my hair. Her eight legs came down one after another, each one horribly exact.

That moment's hesitation on the twenty-fifth floor was gone.

I shouted as I fought. I think I had gone a little mad. "Someone remembers! Up there it's everywhere! My name is Aisling! Do you hear me?"

Her leg went through my shoulder. There was no reaction at all.

The first time, I lost quickly. I cut off one of her legs. It came apart into a handful of thread, wrapped around my arm, climbed it and went into my mouth. I bit it off and there was more. Thread went in through my nose and ears and tightened around my throat. I choked to death. I was still shouting those words when I died.

The eleventh stroke.

The second time, I never stopped running. She wove faster than I could run. The path closed up ahead of me and opened again behind. I stopped cutting legs and burned all the thread instead, fire and then ice. The fire baked the silk brittle, and at a touch of ice it shattered into flakes of white ash that floated in the dark. She lost one leg after another. Each time she lost one, she wove another, a little shorter than the last. That was what she had left. She was weaving herself out of herself.

I cut off her last leg and she came apart, all of her falling to the floor as thread. A very faint wisp of grey smoke rose out of it. I crawled forward a little, gasping, and put my ear close.

::soul.f33#0

She hadn't heard what I shouted.

She couldn't hear any more. When it swallowed her, it ate those thousand years of longing as well. All that was left was this one small wisp, which still remembered to hurry me.

Make haste.

I got up and started to run.

## Floor Thirty-Four

These last few days you've been reading slowly, and you know all the words now. There are only a few floors left. You asked if the next part is where I die. Aren't I sitting right here? You laughed. Don't laugh. Read it and you'll see. That question isn't as simple as you think.

There was only the last stretch left.

This floor was little more than one road going down. Halfway along, beside the road, was a stone with writing cut into it, very deep and very messy. Everything around it was soft. Only this one piece was still stone.

::lore.f34

I looked at it for a long time. Then I sat down and took out every paper I had.

Some were torn out, some I had copied, and some were dead people's words I had written on my sleeve. There in front of that stone I laid them on the ground, one at a time. It was the first time since going down the Well that I had spread them all out.

In a thousand years, no one had done this.

The captain had only his orders. The priest had only his scripture, and had torn a page out of it. The forgemaster had only his mountain. The Grand Master had only the sealed letter locked in his iron chest. Each guardian knew only his own stretch, and the more he knew, the less he dared tell anyone. Everyone held one small piece and took it for the whole.

I was the only one who had picked it all up along the way. That had nothing to do with being clever. I just have a habit of picking things up.

I put the parchment roll from the tenth floor in the middle.

**What hath no walls cannot be held; what hath walls can.**

I copied the words from the stone in front of me and put them beside it.

**It weareth her shape, for hers is the only shape it hath ever known.**

I stared at those two pieces of paper.

My master had said: **Seal it no more. End it.** I had never understood that. The thing had no edges and nowhere to cut. How do you kill something that's everywhere? So no one had ever thought of killing it. They all came to seal it, to hold it, to be a wall, and that was all the Order taught.

But now it had edges.

Aislinn walked in and refused to be digested, so it had to grow around her. It grew for a thousand years, and it grew into her shape. It got shoulders and hands, an up and a down, a left and a right, and it was carrying her shield and sword. A thing with no shape had become a thing with a shape. Day by day, she had turned it into something you could get hold of.

**She held for a thousand years, and that held it up to the day it could be killed.**

Did she know that herself? I asked her later. I'll tell you shortly what she said.

For a thousand years she had been waiting for someone who wasn't coming to take her place.

And every guardian came to take her place.

I sat there with both hands shaking, warm all over, the black on my right arm up to the elbow. I gathered up the papers one by one, in order. I had thought I was collecting things off the dead. I had been collecting a map.

That's why I have to give you all of them.

Then something dropped from above and stood in the middle of the road.

The same wings and the same horns as on the thirtieth floor, grey all over now, and bigger. This time his copies were using my moves.

I hardly felt like standing up. I was only afraid the papers would burn. I pushed them down to the very bottom of my coat and tied the cord tight.

The three of them moved at once, all exactly alike. My rising cut, my second stroke, my turn. I watched three of me with wings come charging, and for an instant it seemed very funny. I used the trick from the twentieth floor and took a cut on the back of my hand on purpose. They all pulled back their swords and waited for mine to drop. That was my master's habit, and it had learned that too. I didn't drop it.

I cut the one on the far left to smoke and glanced up. The purple clouds had lit, and the rain of fire was on its way. I didn't dodge. I ran to the real one and stood pressed against his chest. He pulled his wings right in to cover himself, and the rain of fire came down on them and punched three holes through. It had worked out my every step. It didn't know I could no longer be bothered to be afraid.

I put my sword up through his jaw. He kept laughing. I kept pushing.

His head hit the ground. The grey smoke was a single thread.

::soul.f34#0

"I know," I said. Then I got up and walked on.

## Floor Thirty-Five

I came down the last step and wrote the number on my sleeve.

Eleven thousand four hundred and seven.

The stairs ended there. In front of me there was no wall, there was nothing, and you couldn't even call it dark. The fire on my sword shone out and found nowhere to land and just disappeared. The ground was soft underfoot and now and then moved a little, like standing on a sleeping cow.

I told you on the first floor that the whole village sits on a mouth. Now I had walked into its belly.

Then I heard it.

Very slow, very long. I stopped and held my breath. It wasn't me.

A breath in.

A long, long time.

A breath out.

It was a person breathing. **There is a woman in there. I heard her breathing.** The one who wrote that note on the twenty-fourth floor had got this far too, and had stood where I was standing.

I walked on. All around me, one by one, the eyes lit up. They all turned to look at me, without the smallest sound.

When it stood up, I thought the ground was tilting.

Its lower half had four legs, each one thicker than my whole body. But from the waist up it was shaped like a person. A far, far too big person, with a black-red body and orange light in the cracks, covered in golden spikes. I suppose those were its try at her armour, and it hadn't got close. It had a pair of curved horns, a pair of wings folded behind it, and a long segmented tail dragging on the ground. It didn't know what a person was. It had only grown around one for a thousand years and memorised every inch of her.

It held a sword in its right hand and raised a shield in its left. Both were cased in a hard crust. The blade was all saw teeth, with fire running in the cracks. The shield had grown into a huge beast's head, and its eyes were still lit. All four legs ended in claws, and it could lift the front two and sweep them at you.

I knew that stance.

Left foot forward, shield at chin height, elbow against the ribs, weight on the back foot. Of course I knew it. I had stood like that in the yard at seven years old until my legs shook, at the third post. **The Ward-hall was founded in the name of her who went first.** The Ward-hall's stance was her stance. For a thousand years the Order had handed down the way she stood to every child, and the children didn't know whose it was. They only knew it was the rule.

Now it stood in front of me like that.

I looked down. Without noticing, I had taken the same stance.

We faced each other like that for a moment.

It moved.

The first time, I thought I was ready.

It reared up its front half and brought all four feet down together. The whole floor jumped. While I was in the air, it raised that shield and dropped its body low. I knew that move. I did it ten thousand times in the yard as a child. It's called the shield-ram. Only my shield was smaller than a door, and its shield was the size of half a church.

It charged.

That wasn't running. That was a whole city wall falling at you. Where it passed, the ground was ploughed open in a trench. I threw myself sideways, and the rim of the shield still caught my foot. Everything below the knee was gone. I flew a long way. Before I could even feel it, I saw burning rocks coming down out of the sky.

The twelfth stroke.

I woke in that last stone ring. My leg was back. The old peddler sat beside me, with his cloth on the ground and those few bottles. Even here he was doing business.

The second time, I dodged the charge. When it raised the shield to build up, the ground shook a little, and I could hear that. Floorboards taught me. After the charge it needed time to turn, and that was my chance. I ran to its flank and cut its leg. The great sword came down from above, as slow as a falling tree. I saw it very clearly and took one step aside. The sword cut into the ground, and a split shot past my foot, long and straight, with red light in it. Then it twisted its upper body and swept the great sword along the floor. I jumped it and turned side-on again.

I fought very well. I had never in my life fought so well. The captain taught me not to meet force head on. The priest taught me to run with the light. The mountain taught me to climb. My master taught me to parry. I used everything I had learned on the way. I cut I don't know how many dozen times, and every cut landed where it should.

Every cut was stopped by that shield.

Whichever side I cut from, it got round in time. I hit it and heard only a very dull sound, and I left not one mark. It held up the shield in her stance, left foot forward, elbow against the ribs, and it didn't move. I ran out of potions and went on cutting, until my arms wouldn't lift and a claw scooped me up off the ground.

The thirteenth stroke.

I sat in the stone ring with no potions left and no gold left either. I took the papers out of my coat and looked through them. The one from the twenty-ninth floor, with that frighteningly steady hand.

**Whoever readeth this: the shield breaketh first.**

On the twenty-ninth floor I had taken that for a tip about fighting. On the thirtieth floor my shield really did break, and I thought he meant my shield.

He was telling the next one where to hit.

Someone who is holding on keeps that shield up, keeps it up even with a broken arm. No one who came down dared hit that shield, because it was hers. They had all come out of the Ward-hall. They had all come to protect that shield. Just like me. Twice I had gone around it without thinking, cutting the legs, the arms, the body, and when I did hit the shield it was only because it got in the way. In a thousand years, everyone who got this far probably fought it just like that.

The third time, I didn't go around.

I walked up in front of it and took my stance, left foot forward, elbow against the ribs. It raised the shield. I raised my sword. I put all my strength into one spot, right in the middle of that shield. It charged. I rolled aside, got up, ran back and hit the same spot. A claw swept over and tore my back open. I ignored it. I hit the same spot. The shield was covered in a crust of black shell as thick as a wall, and at first I left no mark at all. After fifty blows, there was a white line. After a hundred, the crust started to shed pieces, one after another, each one bigger than me.

The edge of my sword curled. I burned it straight and hit again. My palm split, blood ran into the grip and I couldn't hold on, so I tied my hand to the sword with a strip of cloth.

There was a tremendous crash. The whole crust broke, and its left arm suddenly went slack.

The shield dropped, rolled twice on the ground and fell over, with a very clear ring.

That was the first clear sound I had heard in that place.

The shield hadn't broken. Only the crust had. It was a round old shield, with a few new cracks of gold. It lay there face up, a few steps from me, and for the first time I got a proper look.

A circle. Eight rays inside.

I stood and looked, with blood dripping off my chin. For a moment I forgot about the claw.

It wasn't the sun.

The crest on my chest, the banners in the yard, the mark burned into the charms, those shields on the stairs on the thirtieth floor. All of it was this. A thousand years ago someone watched her walk in, looked at the shield in her hand, and drew it. Then they built a great hall. Later they hung plates on a wall and sewed that picture on every child's chest, and nobody ever again asked what it was.

For a thousand years we had been wearing her shield.

The breathing missed a beat, and then the whole thing went mad.

Without the shield it got faster. It dropped every stance and went crashing everywhere on four legs and a tail. Front claw, great sword in the right, a stamp of the front foot, one after another. It swung the great sword forward and five cuts flew off it at once, fanned out, each too long to see the end of. The only way to live was the sliver of space between two of them. I stepped into the gap and the second wave came. A sweep one way and a sweep back, the second faster than the first. That's when my left arm broke, with the bone sticking out through the skin. I tied it to my belt with rope and kept walking.

I kept my eyes on its right hand, on that sword. It had never let go of that sword. She had never let go. I waited for the moment it cut into the ground, jumped onto the back of the blade and ran up it to the wrist. One cut, then another. It flung its hand up, and I hung in the air by nothing but the sword I had stuck in. I cut I don't know how many times, until I could see inside.

The sword fell.

It stuck straight into the ground, the only straight thing there. The breathing changed again, very, very faint. I knew she was close to giving out. That was her last line.

The wings it had kept folded behind it spread out, all the way.

By now it no longer had a person's shape. With no shield and no sword, nothing was telling it what it was. It began to loosen. Its edges began to melt. Its tail came round and swept the spot behind me that I had thought was safe. The sky was full of burning rocks, ten times as many as on the thirtieth floor. Along the ground came a wave of fire and a wave of ice, ring after ring, too thick to dodge. Then thunder came too. Fire, then ice, then thunder, my order.

And then I couldn't hit it any more.

It dodged every cut I made. Those eyes had watched me the whole way, through thirty-four floors, watched every move, watched me change moves, watched me change them again. However I started, it got there first. It knew me better than my master did, and better than I did. And its edges were melting. In a little while it would be the Unbounded again, and then no one could ever kill it.

My right arm was black to the shoulder, and every stone on me was burning. Every potion I had ever drunk was burning in my belly. I untied the cloth from my hand and gripped the sword as hard as I have ever gripped anything.

I couldn't hit it. I gripped harder, and I still couldn't hit it.

**Thou hast grown. Thy grip is still too tight.**

I don't know if that was my master talking or me remembering.

Five years old, in the yard, early morning, my hands swollen like bread rolls. He crouched down, touched my hand with the stick and said, "Hold it like that and it only ever goes to one place."

It had watched me for thirty-four floors and learned every move I had. But every move I had came out of a hand gripping that sword tight. What it had learned was that hand.

My master beat me for seven years to make me let go.

I thought of his letter. I thought of that wall in the gatehouse, and how hard it was to lower the knife once the point was on the wall. I thought of that black hole on the twenty-sixth floor, the moment I let go of my sword and flew.

I let go.

My fingers loosened one by one, and my arm came slowly down from the shoulder.

The sword almost dropped, hanging from my knuckles by a little of its own weight. For the first time in my life I felt how long that sword was, and where its point was. It wanted to fall, so I let it fall.

It wasn't any move. I hadn't started anything. I didn't know myself where it would land.

So it didn't know either.

All the eyes went wide.

As the sword fell, I took one step forward. I didn't aim. I only listened to that breathing. Very slow, in the middle of that melting mass, there was a very small shape that hadn't melted, the shape of a person. She was still holding. In the whole thing, that was the only place with an edge left.

The sword came down on that edge.

It didn't scream. It had nothing left to scream with. All the eyes looked at me together. Then, starting from the farthest one, they closed, one by one.

I knelt there, I don't know how long. The sword had dropped to the ground and I didn't pick it up. It was so quiet I could hear my blood dripping.

The breathing stopped.

I thought she was dead. I crawled over the black stuff, which was starting to harden, to where the sword had fallen. There was a split there, and out of the split rose a wisp of smoke.

It was pale gold.

It rose very slowly, like someone standing up after sitting for a long time, and it stopped in front of me. It had no face, but I knew she was looking at me.

Her voice wasn't loud at all. It was very hoarse, as if it hadn't been used for a long time. It was just a very tired girl.

I wiped my face and asked her: did you know? You held on for a thousand years so that it could be killed. Did you know?

She didn't answer. Maybe she didn't understand the question. Maybe she smiled a little. Whether smoke can smile, I don't know.

I said I would tell you what she said. This is her answer: she said nothing.

Then I remembered. There were words I had carried all the way here. I untied the tapestry from my back and opened it out. Half of it had burned and the rest was soaked with blood, but the two girls were still there. I held it up in front of her.

"She told me to tell you," I said, and my voice was barely a voice, "she never left."

The wisp of smoke did not move at all.

Then, slowly, it drifted over, up to the worn place in the tapestry, very close, very lightly.

"Someone remembers her name," I said to the thinner wisp. "It's everywhere. It's my name."

They didn't look back. They were looking at each other.
