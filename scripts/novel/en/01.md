# Book Two: The Bells

Floors 6 to 10

## Floor Six

Behind the gate there were more tombs. I had thought that once the captain was down, this stretch of the road would be over. It wasn't. Where he stood was only the entrance. His men all slept behind it, and I had to get through them to get out.

This is where I started hearing the bells.

At first I thought it was my ears ringing. The sound was very low and came up through many layers of stone. It came every so often, and each time a little dust fell off the walls. Why would there be bells underground? I couldn't think of a reason, so I kept going down.

::panel.f6-1

I didn't see this with my own eyes until floor ten. I'm putting it here because floor six is where I first heard the bells. By the time the sound had come up four floors, it no longer sounded like a call to anything. It sounded like something that couldn't stop.

That day I walked into a small guardroom with one bed and two benches. Three of the walls were shelves, packed with notebooks all the same size. I took out the first one. It was a watch log. It began "Day One" in very tidy writing: who stood first watch, who stood second, what time they changed over, how many barrels of lamp oil were left.

I went through dozens of them and they were all like that. Who was sick, who had fought with whom, whose boots needed replacing. Reading them, it finally came to me that these men had been alive once. One of them was nicknamed the Rat, because he kept a rat. Someone had written jokes in the margins.

The later the notebook, the thinner it got and the fewer words it had, until each page held one line. The last one had only a few pages written and the rest blank.

::lore.f6

Day nine thousand comes to about twenty-five years.

But they stood guard for three hundred. After this log stopped, there were two hundred and seventy-five years to go. Those years had no log and no changing of the watch, and no one wrote any more jokes.

I put the notebook back on the shelf and straightened the row. A place like that isn't one where you should leave things in a mess.

In the niche next door to the guardroom lay a man with his helmet crooked. He was the only one in the whole corridor not lying quite straight. His gauntlets were slightly cupped, and when I leaned in I saw a set of very small bones inside, thin as fish bones, with a long tail trailing behind.

It was the rat. I laughed out loud, and for the first time since I came down it sounded like a person laughing. When I'd finished I reached over and straightened his helmet. Then I thought about it and knocked it crooked again.

"What's so funny?"

I spun and swung, and stopped with the blade a finger's width from someone's neck.

It was a living man. It took me a moment to take that in. He was skinny, hadn't shaved in a long while, carried a pack bigger than he was, and had both hands up very high. The little finger of his right hand was missing its end, and he wore a copper ring on the ring finger.

"No no no," he said. "I'm a person. Smell me. The dead don't stink this bad."

He was from the south and talked with the accent. He said he'd come to dig for gold. He had hung around floor one for two weeks, too scared to go lower, until he saw a red-haired woman cutting her way down, and then he followed her. Whatever I knocked down stayed down, so it was an easy walk for him. Any chest I opened he went through again, to see if I'd missed anything.

"I saw the whole fight with the captain from the bottom of the stairs," he said. "Lovely stuff. When you were shooting him in the wrist, I nearly clapped."

I asked him why he hadn't come to help.

"I'm no knight," he said, as if that settled it.

I should have sent him off. The Order doesn't allow idle hangers-on. But I thought it over, and there was no Order down here, only me, and I hadn't heard anyone speak in days. I put my sword away and told him to stay out of my way. He asked my name. I said it was none of his business, so he started calling me "Red." I didn't ask his name either. He told me to call him the Southerner, because it sounded more mysterious.

## Floor Seven

The paper was tucked inside a leather-bound book, locked in an iron cabinet that had rusted right through. I knew the seal on the wax at a glance, because the paper that sent me down had the same seal on it.

::lore.f7

"Not king, not kin, not knight."

I read it several times and my chest felt tight. This was an order of the Order. What the captain had been guarding was our command. Three hundred years ago, my predecessors sat in the hall where I grew up, wrote these lines, set the seal on them, and sent men down, and those men never left. Three hundred years later, the same seal went on another paper, sending me down to bring them down.

The captain stood in my way, and the people who sent me were his own masters. Everyone had long since forgotten what happened in between.

"Let this stand until the Well be quiet."

I kept this paper too. By then it was a habit. Anything with writing on it, I kept. Numbers on my sleeve, papers inside my coat.

On this floor the monsters began to take cover. Skeleton archers would find a pillar, loose one arrow, and step back behind it. I took my lesson from the fight with the captain, and now I was the one fighting from cover.

This floor also started to have traps. All those dull Way-hall lessons finally paid off. One stretch of corridor was especially clean, with no dust on the floor, which meant something had been sweeping the dust away. I tossed a rock in, and a row of rusty iron spikes shot out of both walls. The Southerner whistled behind me.

"Pretty useful, what your Order teaches," he said. "They teach you what to do once you're out?"

I asked him, out of what.

"Done digging, done fighting, gone home," he said, going through a dead man's pockets. "I'm buying a house by the sea. I'll keep a dog that doesn't bark, and sleep till noon every day. You?"

I opened my mouth and had no idea what to say. So I said a guardian's work is never finished.

He looked up at me. I still remember that look. It's how you'd look at a sheep walking into its pen on its own.

"Red," he said, "you poor thing."

I nearly hit him.

Later I wanted to go back up for a bit. I wasn't running away. I was nearly out of arrows, and I remembered leaving a quiver on floor five. I walked back to the entrance of floor seven and stopped.

The stairs were gone.

I remembered perfectly well. I had come down right there, I had counted every step, and the number was on my sleeve. But now there was only a wall, of stone so old that it had grown a hard white crust, as if it had been there a thousand years. I knocked on it with the hilt of my sword. Solid.

I put my hand on the wall and stood there for a long time.

The captain had said, "Turn not back." Only now did I see that it wasn't just encouragement.

The Southerner came over and looked at the wall, then at me. He felt it with his hand. Then he started slapping it, then pounding it with his fist, and he pounded for a long time. He dropped his pack on the ground, dug out a small pick, and hacked at the wall. All he got was a few chips of stone, and the tip of the pick broke.

"There were stairs here," he said. "There were. I just came down them. Red, you saw them too, right?"

I said yes.

He sat on the ground and panted for a while. Then he slowly started to laugh, and it was an ugly thing to see.

"All right," he said. "Then I'll just have to get rich."

That night I opened a chest. It had arrows in it, and this paper.

::note.p02

The writing shook badly. There must have been more above it, but that part had been torn off, and only these two sentences were left.

This person had stood where I stood, with a hand on the same wall. He had been more frantic than I was. Seeing someone more frantic than me made me a little less so.

You can do this too, when you're scared. Find someone more scared than you are and finish their sentence for them. It doesn't always work, but that's how I got through the other twenty-eight floors.

That night the Southerner built a small fire out of coffin boards. He had calmed down, and was laying out the day's finds on the ground and counting them. He gave me half a piece of salt meat and asked, "What about you? What did you come down here for? You can't really be holding anything up."

I said I'd come to find my master.

"How long ago did he come down?"

"Ten years."

He chewed his meat and glanced at me. "Ten years? Then he's dead."

I punched him in the mouth. He went over backward and the meat fell in the fire. He lay on the ground feeling his teeth, found they were all there, sat up, picked the meat out of the fire, blew on it, and put it back in his mouth.

"Sorry," he said, with his mouth full. "My wife keeps telling people I'm dead too, and look at me."

He didn't bring it up again, and I didn't apologize. The fire slowly died and we each found a niche to lie down in. I could hear him tossing in the one next to mine, counting his gold coins over and over. Each time the count came out different. He began to curse this damned place.

## Floor Eight

Yesterday after you finished reading and went home, your mother came to see me. She stood at the door and wouldn't come in, and asked me what exactly I was teaching you. I showed her these pages. She can't read, so I read them to her. She listened for a while and left without saying anything. Today you came anyway, and you brought the sheep. You don't have to tell me what you said to her.

This floor is short. There weren't many fights and there isn't much to tell, except for those few words on the wall.

They were cut with the point of a knife, inside one of the tombs along the wall, right above your eyes when you lie down. You could only see them from inside. I saw them because I was tired and had found an empty niche to sleep in.

::lore.f8

After I read that, I no longer wanted to sleep.

But tired people sleep anyway. That night I heard it.

The sound is hard to describe. It had no words, and the tune wasn't really pretty. It was like a lot of people in one room a long way off, all humming, none of them aware they were humming or that anyone else was. As you listen, you start to feel there's someone beside you. In all those days underground, I had never once felt that. It felt very good. I wanted to listen a little longer.

When I woke up, I was standing.

I was standing in the middle of the corridor with no shoes on. My sword was back in the niche, more than thirty paces away.

I ran back for the sword. Only then did I think of the niche next door.

The Southerner was gone. His blanket and pack were still there, and his boots were set neatly in front of the niche. This was a man who slept hugging his pack. A line of bare footprints led away down the corridor, very straight, in no hurry at all.

I ran along the footprints until I reached a fork, and there they stopped. I started shouting "Southerner," which is a stupid name to shout. I shouted until my voice gave out and all I could hear was my own echo and that humming. It was still going. It sounded very satisfied.

I waited there two days. He didn't come back.

I opened his pack. It was full of things taken from the dead, along with a small bag of gold and a badly drawn picture of a house by the sea. I put the picture inside my coat and left the pack and the boots in his niche. He'd need them when he came back.

From that day on, I tied my wrist to my sword's hilt with rope when I slept.

"The captain doth not sleep."

The one who cut that line was very frightened. I was frightened that day too. But looking back now, I feel more for the captain. Those young men fell asleep and heard the singing from below, and in the morning they went and asked him, and he told them it is only the stones. He knew it wasn't. He didn't sleep because he could hear it. He stood there listening all night, and in the morning he still had to tell his men it was only the stones.

Later I was that kind of person too. That kind of person always thinks they're right.

## Floor Nine

The last room on floor nine was the captain's.

I knew at a glance. In every other room things had rotted, and here everything was still laid out in perfect order. A narrow bed with the sheet pulled very tight, looking as if no one had lain on it in three hundred years. On the table were an inkwell, a pen and a sheet of paper, with a small stone holding it down.

::lore.f9

I picked up the paper, read it, put it down, and picked it up again.

"It is very kind."

He meant it. That's what makes it terrible. If it had been sarcasm, he would still have been himself. But I held it under the lamp, and those words were written very steadily, steadier than every line before them. When he wrote that, his hand didn't shake at all.

**This picture is a guess.**

I never actually drew this one. I just think about it often. It was maybe the two hundredth year, or the two hundred and fiftieth. His men were all asleep, and no one would ever come to report or to ask him anything again. He had a tiny bit of himself left. He stood before the gate, and that great hammer was very heavy, and for the first time in his life he thought: I'm tired.

He was an honest man, so he asked. He asked the dark, the way you'd ask a superior: If I set this down, who holdeth it?

He waited a long time. Patience was the one thing he had never been short of.

In the end the dark answered. It didn't lie to him. I only understood that later. It never lies to anyone. It just listens for what you want and then says yes. He asked whether he could put it down. What it heard was what he really wanted to ask: can I keep from failing them? So it told him yes. Hold forever.

He thought that was very kind. After that he didn't have to think anymore.

I sat on that bed and thought about my master. He came down alone too. Had he asked the same thing somewhere?

I couldn't bear to think any further. I put the paper inside my coat and put the small stone back on the table.

## Floor Ten

I went out through the last door of the tombs, and the walls on both sides suddenly fell far back. I was standing in the side aisle of a sanctum. The ceiling was too high to see. All I could see were ropes hanging down out of the dark, each one tied to a bell. The biggest bell was bigger than the house I lived in as a child.

No one was pulling the ropes. The bells were swinging on their own.

This is where my boots gave out, and I tied the soles on with rope and kept walking. It's also where I did some arithmetic and found I hadn't eaten in at least two days, and I wasn't hungry.

Not hungry at all, and not very tired either. There were four stones on my sword by then, all picked up along the way, every one of them warm. The sword had started with one socket, and at some point it had grown three more. I certainly hadn't drilled them. At the time I thought this was good news. It would save a lot on rations.

In the side aisle stood a lectern with a parchment scroll spread out on it.

::lore.f10

I read it three times. Since coming down, this was the first thing I'd read by people who understood what they were doing.

So they had known all along. They didn't build this sanctum to worship the thing at the bottom of the Well. They built it to sing at it every dusk, and tell it again and again: you have walls, you are here, you are not everywhere. The kind of song on floor eight has no beginning and no end. The song the people here sang had both. That turned out to be enough to get a grip on something. They were guardians too, and they did it by singing.

I walked into the main hall, and they were still singing.

Two rows of people in long robes with their heads covered, holding candles, not moving even as the wax ran over their hands. But what they were singing was the floor eight kind, with no beginning and no end.

The flagstones in front of the altar had a crack through them with red light showing. An old man lay there with his face to the floor and his ear to the crack, just as I drew him. His robe dragged on the ground, worn through. He had been down there so long that there were two knee-shaped dents in the flagstone.

He heard my footsteps and stirred. He slowly got up and slowly turned his head.

I still remember the look on his face. It's how you look when you're concentrating on something and someone runs in and interrupts you.

He raised one hand, and three balls of blood-red fire flew at me.

The captain only knew one move. This one knew everything, and he could do it standing still. He stood on the altar while the floor blew up in one spot after another, always the spot I was about to step on. The people with covered heads put down their candles and came over. They had no weapons. They just reached out and grabbed me. I cut one down and another pushed up behind it, its hands still dripping wax. Then he raised both hands and beams of light shot out of him, turning slowly like the spokes of a wheel as they swept toward me. One touch and my whole arm went numb. All I could do was run with the light and squeeze inward as I ran, getting a little closer to him with each turn.

When he was nearly finished, the beams got thicker. For the first time I used all the power of the stones at once. I thrust forward and the whole blade caught fire. My master used to do this too, but I had never made it burn so high. I charged through the last beam, hit him in the shoulder, and carried the stroke on down.

He was very light. There wasn't much left inside that robe.

He fell beside the crack, and the red light in it dimmed. The candles went out one by one. The singers sat down one after another and didn't move again. A thread of white smoke rose from the robe, thinner than the captain's.

::soul.f10#0

The voice was very old and very young, like a child who has just found out for the first time that he's been lied to.

::soul.f10#1

He was quiet for a moment and I thought he'd gone. Then the bells stopped. Only then did I notice they had been ringing all this time, for four floors, until I'd forgotten they were a sound at all.

The smoke drifted a little closer to me.

::soul.f10#2

I held my burned sword, and my hands were still shaking.

Offerings.

I told myself there's no need to listen to a madman's dying words. That's the kind of person I was then. It didn't once occur to me to wonder why, of all things, he would use his last breath to tell me that.

I wrote it down, though, one line to a page.
