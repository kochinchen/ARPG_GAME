# Book Two: The Bells

Floors 6 to 10

## Floor Six

Behind the gate there were more tombs. I had thought that once the captain was down, this stretch of the road would be over. It wasn't. Where he stood was only the entrance. His men all slept behind it, and I had to get through them to get out.

This is where I started hearing the bells.

At first I thought it was my ears ringing. The sound was very low and came up through many layers of stone. It came every so often, and each time a little dust fell off the walls. Why would there be bells underground? I couldn't think of a reason, so I kept going down.

::panel.f6-1

I didn't see this with my own eyes until floor ten. I'm putting it here because floor six is where I first heard the bells. By the time the sound had come up four floors, it no longer sounded like a call to anything. It sounded like something that couldn't stop.

That day I walked into a small guardroom with one bed and two benches. Three of the walls were shelves, packed with notebooks all the same size. I took out the first one. It was a watch log. It began "Day One" in very tidy writing: who stood first watch, who stood second, what time they changed over, how many barrels of lamp oil were left.

I went through dozens of them and they were all like that. Who was sick, who had fought with whom, whose boots needed replacing. Reading them, it finally came to me that these men had been alive once. One of them was nicknamed the Rat, because he kept a rat. Someone had written jokes in the margins.

I sat there for half a day reading what was in those margins. The Sixth Watch had forty men, most of them farm boys from the villages below, on the crown's pay, to be relieved every three years. In the first year they were still complaining about the food. Someone wrote: Today's soup, even the Rat won't drink it. Beside it, in another hand: The Rat drank it. The Rat says thank you.

In the third year they began waiting for someone to come and relieve them. Every day the log said "No one came today," and after it someone had drawn a smiling face.

By the fifth year the smiling face was gone. Someone wrote that the crown had a new king, and who knew if he remembered them. Under it the captain had added a line: Order not withdrawn. Continue the watch.

In the eleventh year, someone started writing letters and tucking them in the log. They were to his mother. He asked her whether the cow had calved. That letter was never sent. After it came another, with the same question. By the fifth letter his writing had started to go wrong, and he was still asking about the cow.

I put the letters back. I didn't keep them, so they aren't among the sixty-nine pages you have. I don't know why. By then I had already started to be afraid of reading any more.

The later the notebook, the thinner it got and the fewer words it had, until each page held one line. The last one had only a few pages written and the rest blank.

::lore.f6

Day nine thousand comes to about twenty-five years.

But they stood guard for three hundred. After this log stopped, there were two hundred and seventy-five years to go. Those years had no log and no changing of the watch, and no one wrote any more jokes.

I put the notebook back on the shelf and straightened the row. A place like that isn't one where you should leave things in a mess.

Walking out of the guardroom, my hand touched my sword hilt. I had two stones by then, and just resting my hand there made me feel better. I had died once on floor five and learned to be afraid. Then I went back up, won, and forgot half of it again. That's what people are like.

In the niche next door to the guardroom lay a man with his helmet crooked. He was the only one in the whole corridor not lying quite straight. His gauntlets were slightly cupped, and when I leaned in I saw a set of very small bones inside, thin as fish bones, with a long tail trailing behind.

It was the rat. I laughed out loud, and for the first time since I came down it sounded like a person laughing. When I'd finished I reached over and straightened his helmet. Then I thought about it and knocked it crooked again.

"What's so funny?"

I spun and swung, and stopped with the blade a finger's width from someone's neck.

It was a living man. It took me a moment to take that in. He was skinny, hadn't shaved in a long while, carried a pack bigger than he was, and had both hands up very high. The little finger of his right hand was missing its end, and he wore a copper ring on the ring finger.

"No no no," he said. "I'm a person. Smell me. The dead don't stink this bad."

He was from the south and talked with the accent. He said he'd come to dig for gold. He had hung around floor one for two weeks, too scared to go lower, until he saw a red-haired woman cutting her way down, and then he followed her. Whatever I knocked down stayed down, so it was an easy walk for him. Any chest I opened he went through again, to see if I'd missed anything.

"I saw the whole fight with the captain from the bottom of the stairs," he said. "Lovely stuff. When you were shooting him in the wrist, I nearly clapped."

I asked him why he hadn't come to help.

"I'm no knight," he said, as if that settled it.

I should have sent him off. The Order doesn't allow idle hangers-on. But I thought it over, and there was no Order down here, only me, and I hadn't heard anyone speak in days. I put my sword away and told him to stay out of my way. He asked my name. I said it was none of his business, so he started calling me "Red." I didn't ask his name either. He told me to call him the Southerner, because it sounded more mysterious.

From that day there was someone talking on the road.

He talked a lot. He really did talk a lot. He said he grew up by the sea, that his father was a ship's captain, and that he could sail a boat at nine. Half a day later he said his father was a fisherman and the boat was rented. I asked him which it was. He said it depended on his mood.

I asked him what happened to his little finger. He held up his hand and looked at it, as if noticing for the first time.

"Lost it gambling," he said. "Dice, with a one-eyed fellow. I bet the finger. I could have welshed, but I'm a man of my word."

A while later he said it had actually been caught in a door. A while after that, a dog had bitten it.

The copper ring on his hand never changed, though. He said his wife gave it to him. His wife was very beautiful, the most beautiful in town, with hair down to here. He pointed at his waist. Then he sighed and said she had a bad temper and was always telling people he was dead. He was going to dig up some gold, go back, buy a house by the sea, and shut her up.

I grew up on a mountain, and all my life what I had heard was old proclamations, rules and passwords. No one talked the way he did. Not one word could be believed, and every word was funny. All I knew how to do was keep a straight face and tell him to be quiet, or he'd bring the monsters.

The monsters did come.

The niches on this floor were packed closer than on floor five. Halfway along, there were sounds on both sides at once. Three armoured skeletons climbed out of the niches with a screech of rusty iron, one in front of us and two coming around behind. They were a head taller than the ordinary kind, and their iron gloves had spikes on them.

"Red," said the Southerner.

I looked back. He had already climbed into an empty niche on the row above, curled up very snugly with his arms around his pack.

"You can do it," he said.

You can't cut heavy armor. I learned that on floor five. I crouched, hit the first one behind the knee with my hilt, and as it pitched forward I put my sword into the gap between helmet and breastplate and twisted. The helmet came off. The second one's iron fist hit me in the back. I went into the wall with my mouth full of blood, turned, and put fire on the sword, the way Two-Fingers taught us. That usually worked for me about seven times in ten. This time it worked. The fire ran in through the gaps in the rusty armor and the bones inside crackled. The third one grabbed my hair and dragged me backward. I cut off its hand with a backhand stroke and a patch of my scalp came away. I screamed with the pain, pulled the severed hand out of my hair with the hair still in it, threw it away, and went back in cutting.

When it was over I leaned against the wall with my head covered in blood and my right eye glued shut. The Southerner climbed down out of his niche and started going over their armor, prying off the spikes one at a time and putting them away.

"These'll sell," he said. He glanced at me, took a dirty rag out of his pack and held it out. "Wipe yourself off, Red. You're scary enough to look at already."

I took the rag. It was the first time since I came down that anyone had handed me anything.

The next day he got hurt.

A skeleton in a corner I had missed reached out and raked his leg. It wasn't deep, but he was scared silly. He sat on the ground watching the blood run with his mouth hanging open, and for the first time he had nothing to say. I walked back, cut off the hand, and squatted down to look. The wound was filthy. A dead man's nails carry grave dirt, and if you leave that alone it rots.

I took out a potion. I had three bottles on me then, at twelve gold coins a bottle. I looked at the bottle and I looked at him.

He looked at the bottle and his eyes lit up, and then right away they went dull. "That's expensive," he said. "You keep it. I'll just tie this up."

That was the only time I ever saw him turn down something from someone. I pulled the cork, poured half on his leg and pushed the other half into his mouth. He choked, and his face screwed up.

"That's disgusting!" he said. Then he stopped and looked down at his leg. The wound was closing. He felt it and looked up at me.

"What is this?"

I said it was the Order's medicine.

He was quiet for a moment and worked something out under his breath. Then he said, "Red, if you ever want to sell this, I could set up the shop for you."

I turned and walked off. He limped along after me and started talking again. I didn't tell him that when he yelled about the taste, he looked a lot like Mavis.

Over the next few days he started asking questions.

"What does your Order pay a month?"

I said no pay, just room and board.

He stopped and looked at me as if I'd said I ate rocks every day. "Since you were little?"

Since I was five.

"So why didn't you run?"

I said some did. It was the first time I had told anyone about the twins, and when I'd finished it felt strange. He nodded and said, "They'll do all right. People who go over the wall do all right. I've gone over seven times."

He asked if I had ever had a drink, ever seen the sea, ever slept with a man. I kept a straight face and told him to shut up. "None of them?" he said. A while later he said, "Red, I'm not laughing at you."

I looked at him, and he really wasn't laughing. He had his head down going through a body's pockets, and he came up with a little charm with our crest burned into it. I might have sold that one myself. He held it up to the lamp.

"Does this thing work?"

I said no.

"And you sell them?"

"We have to eat."

He laughed, really pleased, and tossed the charm in his pack. "See," he said, "you can talk after all."

That night I thought of my first wolf.

The winter I was ten, the chickens on the mountain kept disappearing. My master took me to the back of the mountain and said this time I'd do it myself. I thought he meant the chicken thief. We waited in the snow until dusk, and a wolf came out, nothing but skin and bones, with very yellow eyes, taller than I had expected. My master took my wooden sword away and handed me a real short sword, for the first time. Then he stepped back ten paces and stood with his arms folded.

The wolf walked toward me very slowly. I forgot everything. When it sprang, all I did was close my eyes and stab forward. It bit my arm and we went down in the snow together. Its mouth stank. I kept stabbing with my other hand, stabbing and stabbing, with no idea where. When I opened my eyes the snow was red. It still had hold of my arm, and it wasn't moving anymore.

My master came over and pried the wolf's mouth open with his hands. He looked at my arm, then at my other hand, clenched on the short sword and unable to open. He pried my fingers loose one at a time. It was the only time he ever touched my hand without a stick.

He said, "Hand."

I cried. I thought he was scolding me, which made me cry harder. He carried me down on his back and didn't say a word the whole way. The next morning there was an apple outside my door.

## Floor Seven

The first thing I found on this floor was a paper, tucked inside a leather-bound book, locked in an iron cabinet that had rusted right through. I knew the seal on the wax at a glance, because the paper that sent me down had the same seal on it.

::lore.f7

"Not king, not kin, not knight."

I read it several times and my chest felt tight. This was an order of the Order. What the captain had been guarding was our command. Three hundred years ago, my predecessors sat in the hall where I grew up, wrote these lines, set the seal on them, and sent men down, and those men never left. Three hundred years later, the same seal went on another paper, sending me down to bring them down.

The captain stood in my way, and the people who sent me were his own masters. Everyone had long since forgotten what happened in between.

"Let this stand until the Well be quiet."

I kept this paper too. By then it was a habit. Anything with writing on it, I kept. Numbers on my sleeve, papers inside my coat.

On this floor the monsters began to take cover. Skeleton archers would find a pillar, loose one arrow, and step back behind it. I took my lesson from the fight with the captain, and now I was the one fighting from cover.

This floor also started to have traps. All those dull Way-hall lessons finally paid off. One stretch of corridor was especially clean, with no dust on the floor, which meant something had been sweeping the dust away. I tossed a rock in, and a row of rusty iron spikes shot out of both walls. The Southerner whistled behind me.

"Pretty useful, what your Order teaches," he said. "They teach you what to do once you're out?"

I asked him, out of what.

"Done digging, done fighting, gone home," he said, going through a dead man's pockets. "I'm buying a house by the sea. I'll keep a dog that doesn't bark, and sleep till noon every day. You?"

I opened my mouth and had no idea what to say. So I said a guardian's work is never finished.

He looked up at me. I still remember that look. It's how you'd look at a sheep walking into its pen on its own.

"Red," he said, "you poor thing."

I nearly hit him.

There was a great room on this floor, all pillars, probably the old mustering ground. When we reached the middle, the arrows came.

I only dodged when I heard the string. The first arrow went past my ear and the second went into my thigh. I dropped flat and crawled behind a pillar, snapped off the shaft and left the head in the meat. Two more arrows stuck in the ground on the other side of the pillar. I took a quick look. There were at least seven or eight skeleton archers, spread out behind the pillars all around, and one in a robe whose hands were starting to glow.

I shot back with the short bow and hit one. It wobbled and stepped behind its pillar. They took turns. When one stepped back another stepped out. Whenever I moved, an arrow came. The mage's ice spike hit the pillar I was hiding behind, and frost spread over the stone. The cold came right through, and my fingers began to stiffen. I had two potions left, but I couldn't spend one on a leg.

I couldn't see the Southerner. I figured he had hidden himself already, or run. I didn't blame him. He was no knight.

Just then, at the far end of the room, there was a terrible racket of iron on stone. Clang, clang, clang. All the archers turned their heads.

It was those spikes. The Southerner had strung a pile of scrap iron on a rope, and from behind a distant pillar he was slamming it against the floor, over and over. It sounded like a kitchen falling down.

I didn't stop to think. I jumped up and ran at the nearest one. My leg hurt as if it were on fire, and I didn't care. One stroke cut its bow in half and the next cut its neck. I grabbed the body to use as a shield and ran at the second. Arrows stuck in the bones I was holding, and splinters hit my face. The mage turned back and sent an ice spike at me. I threw the skeleton in the way, took one long step, and was in front of it. My burning sword went through the middle of its robe.

The room went quiet. The Southerner walked out from behind the pillar, slowly coiling up his rope. His face was white and his hands were shaking.

"I thought you said you weren't a knight," I said.

"I'm not," he said. "I'm scared that if you die I can't go on by myself."

When I dug the arrowhead out of my leg, he turned his head away, unable to watch. While he looked somewhere else he told me about the time his wife broke a neighbor's head with a frying pan, and he told it with great energy. I knew he was telling it for me, so I wouldn't keep thinking about that arrow.

I didn't thank him then. I still remember that.

Later I wanted to go back up for a bit. I wasn't running away. I was nearly out of arrows, and I remembered leaving a quiver on floor five. I walked back to the entrance of floor seven and stopped.

The stairs were gone.

I remembered perfectly well. I had come down right there, I had counted every step, and the number was on my sleeve. But now there was only a wall, of stone so old that it had grown a hard white crust, as if it had been there a thousand years. I knocked on it with the hilt of my sword. Solid.

I put my hand on the wall and stood there for a long time.

The captain had said, "Turn not back." Only now did I see that it wasn't just encouragement.

The Southerner came over and looked at the wall, then at me. He felt it with his hand. Then he started slapping it, then pounding it with his fist, and he pounded for a long time. He dropped his pack on the ground, dug out a small pick, and hacked at the wall. All he got was a few chips of stone, and the tip of the pick broke.

"There were stairs here," he said. "There were. I just came down them. Red, you saw them too, right?"

I said yes.

He sat on the ground and panted for a while. Then he slowly started to laugh, and it was an ugly thing to see.

"All right," he said. "Then I'll just have to get rich."

That night I opened a chest. It had arrows in it, and this paper.

::note.p02

The writing shook badly. There must have been more above it, but that part had been torn off, and only these two sentences were left.

This person had stood where I stood, with a hand on the same wall. He had been more frantic than I was. Seeing someone more frantic than me made me a little less so.

You can do this too, when you're scared. Find someone more scared than you are and finish their sentence for them. It doesn't always work, but that's how I got through the other twenty-eight floors.

That night the Southerner built a small fire out of coffin boards. He had calmed down, and was laying out the day's finds on the ground and counting them. He gave me half a piece of salt meat and asked, "What about you? What did you come down here for? You can't really be holding anything up."

I said I'd come to find my master.

"How long ago did he come down?"

"Ten years."

He chewed his meat and glanced at me. "Ten years? Then he's dead."

I punched him in the mouth. He went over backward and the meat fell in the fire. He lay on the ground feeling his teeth, found they were all there, sat up, picked the meat out of the fire, blew on it, and put it back in his mouth.

"Sorry," he said, with his mouth full. "My wife keeps telling people I'm dead too, and look at me."

He didn't bring it up again, and I didn't apologize. The fire slowly died and we each found a niche to lie down in. I could hear him tossing in the one next to mine, counting his gold coins over and over. Each time the count came out different. He began to curse this damned place.

I couldn't sleep, so I asked him if he played dice.

He stuck his head out of the next niche with his eyes shining. Knights play dice? I said no, I just had a friend when I was small who owed me money. He immediately dug two dice out of his pack, made of bone, with the corners worn round. We threw them across the stone slab between our niches for nearly two hours. He kept winning, and as he won he taught me how to listen to dice landing. On the last throw I bet one gold coin on double six, and it came up double six.

He stared for a moment, swore, and tossed a coin over.

"Red," he said, "go home and open a gambling house. It's got better prospects than being a wall."

I kept that coin in my boot. It's the only time in my life I ever beat him.

I woke once in the middle of the night.

It wasn't the singing. I hadn't heard the singing yet. It was because someone was touching my sword. I opened my eyes a crack. He was squatting in front of my niche with one hand on the guard, using a thin piece of iron to pry at the stones. The stones lit his face a little red.

My hand moved faster than my head. My dagger was already at his throat.

He didn't yell, he didn't flinch, and he didn't say sorry. He put the piece of iron down very slowly and raised his hands.

"They'd sell," he said quietly. "One would do it, Red. You know what one of these is worth? I've seen it. A blacksmith's in the capital. One little piece buys a whole street."

I asked him how much would be enough.

I don't know why I asked him that. He stopped, slowly sat down and leaned against the side of the niche. For the first time, he didn't answer right away.

"Don't know," he said. "I've never worked it out."

I put the dagger away. I ought to have cut off his hand. That's the Order's rule. I said, "Touch it again and I'll throw you to the skeletons."

He said all right. A while later he said, "It's a bit odd, that thing. Don't you think? When I touched it just now, my hand felt really good. I didn't want to let go."

I rolled over and told him to shut up.

I didn't tell him it was the same for me.

## Floor Eight

Yesterday after you finished reading and went home, your mother came to see me. She stood at the door and wouldn't come in, and asked me what exactly I was teaching you. I showed her these pages. She can't read, so I read them to her. She listened for a while and left without saying anything. Today you came anyway, and you brought the sheep. You don't have to tell me what you said to her.

This floor is short. There weren't many fights, and what mattered most was those few words on the wall.

They were cut with the point of a knife, inside one of the tombs along the wall, right above your eyes when you lie down. You could only see them from inside. I saw them because I was tired and had found an empty niche to sleep in.

::lore.f8

After I read that, I no longer wanted to sleep.

We had fought our way there. The corridor was narrow, with rows of niches overhead, and every few steps a skeleton dropped from above and landed on my shoulders. The Southerner had learned to walk in my shadow. When I went forward he went forward, and when I crouched he crouched first. An armoured skeleton slid out of a niche right in front of him, and in his fright he threw his pack at it. A whole bag of iron spikes hit it in the face, louder than my sword ever was. I pried its helmet off from behind, while he picked up the spikes, trembling, and didn't miss one.

When we reached a quieter place, we stopped. He didn't say much that day. I thought he was tired.

He sat on the edge of a niche, turning the copper ring on his finger, for a long time. He said, "Red, if I told you something true, would you believe it?"

I said had he ever told me anything true.

"I'm afraid of the dark," he said. "Have been since I was little. The reason I spent so long in gambling houses is they keep the lamps lit at night."

I looked at him. He wasn't laughing.

"That's why I keep talking," he said.

I didn't know what to say, so I said nothing. I lay down in my niche. He rustled around next door for a while and then said, "Don't blow out the lamp, all right?"

I didn't. The lamp stood between our two niches, and it was still burning when I fell asleep.

But tired people sleep anyway. That night I heard it.

The sound is hard to describe. It had no words, and the tune wasn't really pretty. It was like a lot of people in one room a long way off, all humming, none of them aware they were humming or that anyone else was. As you listen, you start to feel there's someone beside you. In all those days underground, I had never once felt that. It felt very good. I wanted to listen a little longer.

When I woke up, I was standing.

I was standing in the middle of the corridor with no shoes on. My sword was back in the niche, more than thirty paces away.

I ran back for the sword. Only then did I think of the niche next door.

The Southerner was gone. His blanket and pack were still there, and his boots were set neatly in front of the niche. This was a man who slept hugging his pack. A line of bare footprints led away down the corridor, very straight, in no hurry at all. It didn't look like the walk of a man awake. It looked more like someone being called down in his sleep.

I ran along the footprints until I reached a fork, and there they stopped. I started shouting "Southerner," which is a stupid name to shout. I shouted until my voice gave out and all I could hear was my own echo and that humming. It was still going. It sounded very satisfied.

I waited there two days. He didn't come back.

I opened his pack. I wanted to see if his name was in there.

It was full of things taken from the dead: iron spikes, belt buckles, a few rusty charms, the kind we sold. A small bag of gold, less than I'd expected. Two bone dice. At the very bottom was an oilcloth packet. I unwrapped it and found three papers.

The first was a debt note. The figure on it was huge, too big to pay off in his lifetime. Under it was a handprint, and the handprint had no little finger.

The second was a letter in a woman's hand, written very hard: Don't come back. They come every day. I can't pay what you lost at dice. They told me about the little finger. If you have any conscience at all, go and die a long way off.

The third was a badly drawn picture of a house by the sea. I turned it over. The back was printed. It was a handbill for an inn, welcoming sailors, with cheap drink.

His father wasn't a ship's captain. His wife wasn't telling people he was dead. She was telling him to die. He hadn't come to dig for gold. He had come to pay a debt, driven down to a place even madmen stayed away from. That house by the sea was nothing he could ever buy. It was just a handbill for an inn. His wife probably worked there. Why else would he carry that paper around?

Nothing he had told me all along the way was true, except the copper ring and the wife.

I sat in front of his niche looking at them for a long time. Then I wrapped the three papers up again and put them inside my coat, and left the pack and the boots in his niche. He'd need them when he came back.

From that day on, I tied my wrist to my sword's hilt with rope when I slept.

The floor was very quiet going on, so quiet that I started wanting to talk to someone. I came to a fork, turned my head and said, "This way's cleaner, watch the floor." Only then did I remember there was no one behind me.

"The captain doth not sleep."

The one who cut that line was very frightened. I was frightened that day too. But looking back now, I feel more for the captain. Those young men fell asleep and heard the singing from below, and in the morning they went and asked him, and he told them it is only the stones. He knew it wasn't. He didn't sleep because he could hear it. He stood there listening all night, and in the morning he still had to tell his men it was only the stones.

Later I was that kind of person too. That kind of person always thinks they're right.

## Floor Nine

The last room on floor nine was the captain's.

I knew at a glance. In every other room things had rotted, and here everything was still laid out in perfect order. A narrow bed with the sheet pulled very tight, looking as if no one had lain on it in three hundred years. On the table were an inkwell, a pen and a sheet of paper, with a small stone holding it down.

::lore.f9

I picked up the paper, read it, put it down, and picked it up again.

"It is very kind."

He meant it. That's what makes it terrible. If it had been sarcasm, he would still have been himself. But I held it under the lamp, and those words were written very steadily, steadier than every line before them. When he wrote that, his hand didn't shake at all.

What comes next is a guess. I think about it often. It was maybe the two hundredth year, or the two hundred and fiftieth. His men were all asleep, and no one would ever come to report or to ask him anything again. He had a tiny bit of himself left. He stood before the gate, and that great hammer was very heavy, and for the first time in his life he thought: I'm tired.

He was an honest man, so he asked. He asked the dark, the way you'd ask a superior: If I set this down, who holdeth it?

He waited a long time. Patience was the one thing he had never been short of.

In the end the dark answered. It didn't lie to him. I only understood that later. It never lies to anyone. It just listens for what you want and then says yes. He asked whether he could put it down. What it heard was what he really wanted to ask: can I keep from failing them? So it told him yes. Hold forever.

He thought that was very kind. After that he didn't have to think anymore.

Before I reached this room, I had finished off the rest of his men on this floor. They were still there after the captain fell, only there was no one to call them up anymore. In each niche the skeleton waited until I came close, then slowly sat up with its hands on its weapon, very orderly, as if waiting for the word of command. I fought my way along one niche at a time, very slowly and very tired. Nothing charged me anymore. They just stood up, raised their weapons, and waited for me to cut. One came apart with its arm half raised, and its spear fell on the floor. I bent down, picked it up and laid it back on its chest.

Only the alcove nearest the captain's room was empty. The straw mat in it was laid perfectly flat, the blanket folded into a square, and beside it stood a pair of boots, toes pointing out, as if their owner might come back at any moment and put them on. Every other alcove was thick with dust. This one alone was clean. Later I thought that the young man who first heard the song and went to ask him about it had probably slept here. The captain told him it was only the sound of the stones, and he went down and never came back. For three hundred years the captain had been making his bed. I had left the southerner's boots in his alcove too, so I understood.

I sat on that bed and thought about my master.

I was twelve when he left. For many years after that, I didn't mention him. When the other children asked, I said he was off on a mission and changed the subject. I broke his stick and threw it away, and then ran out in the middle of the night to get it back. Every day at dawn I went and stood at the third post until my hands shook, and in time I could outdo every instructor there was. When I was fourteen Big Lad laughed at me and said my master had died down the Well long ago. I broke his nose with one punch and his blood ran all over him. He was a head taller than me and he beat me badly too, but he never said it again.

The winter I was thirteen, Stammer coughed for a long time. He was the youngest of us. He had to repeat the first word of everything many times, so no one ever waited for him to finish. He slept next to me. There wasn't enough firewood that year and the long dormitory was very cold. He coughed half the night. The cook fed him the potion, and he'd get hot and it would go away, and when it went away he'd cough again.

One night he woke me up and said he had something to tell me. He said "I, I, I, I, I," for a long time, and I sat on the edge of his bed and waited. By then I didn't wait for people much anymore, but it was very quiet outside that night and I had nothing else to do. He was still saying it near dawn and never got to the end. Then he fell asleep.

In the morning he didn't wake up.

We dug a hole at the back of the mountain. The ground was frozen hard, and Two-Fingers had to burn a fire on it for half a day. The Grand Master read a proclamation and halfway through forgot Stammer's name. I stood there with my hands split by the cold and didn't shed a tear. By then I had stopped letting things show on my face. Mavis stood beside me crying very loudly, cursing him as she cried, because he still owed her two copper coins.

To this day I don't know what he wanted to tell me.

After I turned fifteen I started going down the mountain with the instructors, to mend roads and sell charms. I stood night watch on the mountain, and on a clear day you could just see the little hill in the valley from the highest watchtower. It was two days away by the mountain road and looked no bigger than a fingernail. I often thought back then that my master was right there under it, guarding something very important, and that once I was strong enough I would come and relieve him. I didn't really listen when Mavis talked to me. She used to throw stones at me from the foot of the watchtower and shout, "You think that Well'll grow any faster if you keep looking at it?"

My master came down alone too. Had he asked somewhere too: who holdeth it?

I couldn't bear to think any further. I put the paper inside my coat and put the small stone back on the table.

## Floor Ten

Your mother came again today. This time she came inside and sat on the bench. She said nothing, and she didn't tell you to go home. I kept you reading. She sat for a while and left. You needn't ask, because I don't know what she was thinking either.

I went out through the last door of the tombs, and the walls on both sides suddenly fell far back. I was standing in the side aisle of a sanctum. The ceiling was too high to see. All I could see were ropes hanging down out of the dark, each one tied to a bell. The biggest bell was bigger than the house I lived in as a child.

No one was pulling the ropes. The bells were swinging on their own.

This is where my boots gave out, and I tied the soles on with rope and kept walking. It's also where I did some arithmetic and found I hadn't eaten in at least two days, and I wasn't hungry.

Not hungry at all, and not very tired either. There were four stones on my sword by then, all picked up along the way, every one of them warm. The sword had started with one socket, and at some point it had grown three more. I certainly hadn't drilled them. At the time I thought this was good news. It would save a lot on rations.

I took out the crust. The one the cook gave me, which I had never been able to bring myself to eat. I bit it. It was so hard I could barely bite it, and it had no taste. I knew it ought to be burnt and a little salty. I ate that crust from the age of five to the age of twenty-two, and I could have picked it out with my eyes closed.

It tasted of nothing.

I held it in my mouth for a long time, then spat it back into my hand, wrapped it up and put it away. I told myself it had probably been kept too long.

In the side aisle stood a lectern with a parchment scroll spread out on it.

I read it three times. Since coming down, this was the first thing I'd read by people who understood what they were doing.

So they had known all along. They didn't build this sanctum to worship the thing at the bottom of the Well. They built it to sing at it every dusk, and tell it again and again: you have walls, you are here, you are not everywhere. The kind of song on floor eight has no beginning and no end. The song the people here sang had both. That turned out to be enough to get a grip on something. They were guardians too, and they did it by singing.

I walked into the main hall, and they were still singing.

Two rows of people in long robes with their heads covered, holding candles, not moving even as the wax ran over their hands. But what they were singing was the floor eight kind, with no beginning and no end.

The flagstones in front of the altar had a crack through them with red light showing. An old man lay there with his face to the floor and his ear to the crack, just as I drew him. His robe dragged on the ground, worn through. He had been down there so long that there were two knee-shaped dents in the flagstone.

He heard my footsteps and stirred. He slowly got up and slowly turned his head. Standing, he was much taller than me, and his back was still bent. His red robe had rotted full of holes, though the gold trim was still bright, and on his head was a gold crown with points like a row of knives. He leaned on a staff wound with gold, with a big red orb set in the top and a few red shards floating around him. His face was as white as flour. The corners of his mouth turned up. His eyes didn't.

I still remember the look on his face. It's how you look when you're concentrating on something and someone runs in and interrupts you.

He raised one hand, and three balls of blood-red fire flew at me.

The captain only knew one move. This one knew everything, and he could do it standing still.

I ran to the side. The fire burst where I had just been and stones hit my back. A circle of blood-red lit up on the floor. I knew that warning, because Two-Fingers had drawn it for us in class. I jumped out of it with everything I had, and I was still too slow. The blast threw me up, and the second and third came down right in front of me, each on the spot I was about to step on. He wasn't really hitting me. He was doing sums on me.

The people with covered heads put down their candles and came over. They had no weapons. I cut one down and another pushed up behind it and took hold of my sword with its bare hand. Its fingers came off and it still didn't let go, and it was still singing. Three of them wrapped themselves around my legs and wax dripped on my face. I looked down. The one holding me was an old woman whose hood had fallen back. She was smiling up at me, and she had no teeth. She kept murmuring something into the song, a word I didn't catch at the time.

Some others standing further back threw small fireballs from their hands, and my sleeve caught fire. My sleeve. My numbers. I rolled on the ground, smothered the fire, got up, and ran for the altar.

He raised both hands and four beams of light shot out of him.

The beams were like the spokes of a wheel, turning slowly as they swept toward me. I jumped the first one and didn't get clear of the second. It passed over my left hand. Where it touched there was no wound, but my whole arm lost all feeling at once, as if it weren't mine. I stumbled, and the third beam went through my chest.

That's where I died. The second time. It didn't hurt when the beam went through. It was only very cold, and then I saw my knees bend, and saw the floor come up to me.

I took a breath in the stone ring at the entrance to floor ten, rolled over and threw up.

The old peddler had the same thing to say. I bought four bottles, drew the second stroke on my sleeve, and sat in the ring waiting for my hands to stop shaking. Getting up this time was harder than the first. The first time I hadn't known what would happen. This time I knew how cold that beam was. But there was only the one road, and the bells were still ringing.

The second time I didn't charge at him.

I had thought it through in the stone ring. Those hooded people: cut one down and another pushed up from behind. There was no end to them. But they hadn't gathered there to fight me. They stood around the altar because the old priest was there, and they sang because he was listening. However many years he had lain on that crack listening, they had sung behind him for just as long, and he had never once turned around to look at them. I also remembered the word the old woman kept murmuring into the song while she held me. It wasn't part of the song. It was a name: Tobin.

This time, when the believers took hold of me, I didn't cut them. The old woman wrapped herself around my legs again, and I crouched down and said in her ear, "Tobin."

Her singing broke off for a moment. I said it again, and asked her, "When Tobin calls you, what does he say?"

She looked up at me. Her hood had fallen onto her back, and her eyes were cloudy, as if they hadn't been open in a long time. "He says he's cold," she said. After a while she said, "He died in the mine. Forty years ago. He never once said he was cold."

She didn't sing again. She let go of my legs, got slowly to her feet, turned, and walked toward the man lying on the floor before the altar. She began to speak to him, but it was no longer human speech. The sound that came out of her throat was like wind across the mouth of a bell, long and short, and I couldn't understand a word of it. All I could make out, threaded through it, was "Tobin."

The people near us stopped too. The singing broke off, starting around the old woman and spreading one voice at a time, like ice cracking on a pond. The hands holding me let go one by one. They put down their candles, pushed back their hoods and followed her, crowding around him, pulling at his hem and his sleeves, murmuring to him. Every one of them was talking, and I understood none of it. But he understood. I could see that.

The old priest turned his head. It was the first time he had ever turned around to look at them.

His face changed. It wasn't the interrupted look anymore. He looked at those faces, one after another, and his eyes went soft, as if he were looking at a roomful of sick children. It was the only time I ever saw pity on his face. He reached out, gathered the old woman into his arms and held her very tight, and his lips moved against her hood.

Then his palm lit up, and a ball of fire went out through her back. She came apart, into burning scraps of cloth and ash, and fell out of his arms.

The crowd around him broke up. Some went back, picked up their candles and started the song with no end again, louder than before. Some kept walking toward him, murmuring, as if they hadn't seen anything. And some, I saw, began to shake, and then their hands closed into fists. A tall man was the first to throw himself on him, grabbing the staff and wrenching it down. Another got an arm round his neck from behind, then a third, then a fourth. They beat him with their fists, with candlesticks, with their nails, and never said a word.

He raised both hands and four beams shot out of him, knocking down the ones on top of him and sweeping toward me. I didn't jump. I ran with the light, in the direction it turned, staying between two beams and squeezing inward as I ran, a little closer to him with each turn. I learned to listen for him raising his hand. When his sleeve rustled, the floor was about to light up, so I took two steps away from where it lit and then two steps back. I drank two potions. I was halfway through the third when a fire bolt smashed the bottle in my hand and put glass in my lips.

But more and more of them threw themselves on him. The singers sang, the murmurers murmured, the ones beating him beat him. He was dragged this way and that by all those hands, and his light turned more and more wildly and grew dimmer. He tried to shake them off and get back to the crack to listen, but the hands held tight, and even with their fingers burning away they didn't let go.

Pair after pair of hands dragged his arms down, and he couldn't raise them again. He shrank down among all those hands, saying over and over, "Just sing. Just sing."

I squeezed between two beams, and for the first time I used all the power of the stones at once. I thrust forward and the whole blade caught fire. My master used to do this too, but I had never made it burn so high. The last beam sliced across my left shoulder and my whole left side went numb. With my right hand I hit him in the shoulder and carried the stroke on down.

He was very light. There wasn't much left inside that robe.

He fell beside the crack, and the red light in it dimmed. The singers, the murmurers, the ones who had beaten him, sat down one after another and didn't move again. Where the old woman had come apart there was only a small heap of ash, with a half-burned candle lying beside it. The candles went out one by one. A thread of white smoke rose from the robe, thinner than the captain's.
