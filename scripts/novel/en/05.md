# Book Six: Two Girls

Floors 26 to 30

## Floor Twenty-Six

The moment you came through the door today, you told me your grandmother's name was Aisling too. I know. I carved the plate on her coffin.

You asked if she knew whose name it had been first. She didn't. She told me it was her grandmother's name, and that was all she knew. What called you that day at the Well was using her voice. You can think about that on your own, slowly, when you're a bit older.

Once the webs let go, the paths stopped moving. Only then could I see that those crooked passages had once been a palace.

Some rooms the webs hadn't filled. In the kitchen the pots were still lined up neatly. The musicians' room had a lute with broken strings. Further in was a small room with a very low door, made for children. Inside were two small beds. They were meant to stand apart. Someone had pushed them together, and the legs had left two scratches across the floor.

A very short wooden sword leaned against the wall.

I didn't touch it. I just stood in the doorway for a while. The dust in that room was thinner than anywhere else. Someone had kept on sweeping it, for years and years.

Standing in that doorway, I thought of the long dormitory on the mountain.

Mavis's bed and mine had started out one bed apart, with Stammer sleeping between us. After Stammer died, that bed stood empty. That winter was especially cold. One night I heard something being dragged. Mavis was hauling her bed toward mine, a little at a time, the legs squealing on the floor. It took her half an hour, and she didn't stop until the two beds touched.

"Cold," she said. She stuck her feet over. They were as cold as stones.

The next day the Ward-hall instructor saw it, gave us a telling-off and made us drag it back. We dragged it back. That night she dragged it over again. After a dozen or so rounds of that he gave up. When I left the mountain, those two scratches were still in the floor.

Past that was a long corridor. The windows on both sides looked out on solid rock, because the whole palace had later sunk into the ground. This used to be the surface. Sunlight used to come in through these windows. At the end of the corridor was the throne room. Half the roof had fallen in, and webs hung down through the hole, gone grey.

It wasn't only dust down here. The Queen was dead and her children were in chaos, but the Well held other things, and they were climbing up while she was gone.

In the middle of the corridor stood a black shape, taller than a man, with no face. There was a hole in its chest, and nothing in the hole. As I came closer, the air began to flow into it. My hair streamed forward and my feet started to slide. A fallen suit of armour beside me was pulled across the floor. It touched the edge of the hole and was gone, without even a sound.

I drove my sword into the floor. It pulled me out flat, boots off the ground. My sleeve streamed that way, and the charcoal writing on it blurred, line by line. I got my left hand to the short bow at my belt, but it was no use. The arrow was gone before I could draw.

I let go of the sword.

I mean that. I opened my hand and flew straight at it, and in midair I pulled my dagger. Just before I hit, I stabbed the dagger into the top edge of the hole and dragged down with all my weight, the way you slit a sack. The hole split. The black inside came bursting out and threw me back. I hit the floor numb all over, my ears bleeding, and for a while I couldn't hear a thing. The shape shrank into a lump of smoke and slowly came apart.

I crawled back and pulled out my sword. It was the first time I had ever let go so cleanly. I didn't think about that then. I thought about it later.

I walked through the whole palace. The banners, the hangings and the armour all bore the royal emblem, a thorned rose. Nowhere could I find our crest, the circle and the eight rays.

Of course not, I thought. The Order came decades after this palace. Still, something felt off. So many things had been handed down for a thousand years. Where had our crest come from?

The throne room wasn't empty.

The moment I walked in I stepped on a thread, very fine, hardly thicker than a hair. Something scraped overhead. I jumped back, and a spider bigger than a horse dropped onto the spot where I had been standing. It was covered in purple-black spines like crystal that shone purple. It wasn't one of her children. I could see that at once. It was older than any of them, and had probably spent a few hundred years up here eating whatever couldn't get up or down.

A crystal-spined weaver. It doesn't pounce. Once it had dropped, it climbed back up its thread and vanished through the hole in the roof. I stood in the middle of the hall with grey webs all around me, and I couldn't tell which threads were new.

I took two steps. Something tightened on my ankle, and I was hanging upside down.

All my blood ran into my face, and I nearly dropped my sword. It came crawling slowly down from above, all eight eyes lit. I cut at the thread on my foot and couldn't cut it. The thread had a coat of crystal. It opened its jaws, and one very long spike came stabbing down at my belly. I threw my weight sideways. The spike went through my side and pinned itself to the back of my armour, and the pain turned everything white.

I grabbed the spike. Not to pull it out. To climb it. I climbed along its jaw onto its face, drove my sword into the biggest eye and poured in fire. All the crystal on its body lit up together, and then it blew, spine by spine. The shards came down like rain and cut my face and hands to ribbons. The thread snapped, and the two of us fell out of the air together and smashed the steps in front of the throne.

It lay on top of me, still twitching. When it stopped, I crawled out from under. I had three broken ribs and a hole in my side I could have fitted a fist in.

That's how I came to crawl up to the throne and find that brass plate. I didn't go there to look at it. I just couldn't crawl any further, so I leaned there to rest.

Set into the floor in front of the throne was a brass plate, where people knelt to swear. Knees had worn two shallow dips in the middle of it. The words on it could still be read.

::lore.f26

Maeve.

I read that name twice. I knew someone just one sound away from it, who stood at the second post and laughed like a chicken being stepped on. One a queen, one a bread thief. I thought, when I get back I have to tell her. She'd make me kneel, and then charge me for it.

I laughed a little, standing there. I hadn't done much of that since I came down.

"Her sword for all her days."

I crouched and put my fingers in the two dips. They were small. Whoever it was didn't have big knees.

They must have grown up together. You can see that in the tapestry. One in a crown, one with a wooden sword, both still children, the crown still too big. The one with the crown couldn't just wander out of doors, so the other was picked to keep her company, like her shadow, and started on the wooden sword.

Someone must have rapped her hand every morning.

They must have fought, too. There must have been an afternoon when the one with the crown grabbed the wooden sword and said it was her turn to be the knight, and they both got a scolding. They probably had words only they understood, the kind they could still use when they were grown, in front of a full court.

Then one day the one with the crown sat down in this chair. The one with the wooden sword had a real sword now, and she walked out onto this plate and knelt. The whole court was watching. She raised the sword and spoke the oath in a loud voice, every word very solemn.

My guess is they were both trying not to laugh.

I really do guess that. They were young. Nobody yet knew what those words actually meant.

I knelt for a moment beside the plate, not on it. That wasn't my place.

On the way out there was a slanting crack beside the path, and through it I could see a long way down. It was purple down there. Lightning flashed in the clouds, and in the clouds hung blocks of stone with towers still standing on them. Farthest of all stood a very, very tall staircase.

## Floor Twenty-Seven

How that kingdom actually came to an end, I pieced together from the papers in the palace.

It was a small country, much smaller than the kingdom we have now, but it was the first. They were the first people to build city walls on this land. In those days the Well didn't look like a well. It was just a stretch of ground that had caved in, and it spread a little further each year. Villages vanished and people vanished. Nothing was left where they had been, not even the ground. You walked up to the place and found yourself back where you started, or somewhere else altogether. The King sent an army to fight it. The army walked in and never came out.

Nobody knew then what it was. The Queen's father called it "the wound". By the year he died it had swallowed half the country. Maeve took the throne at twenty-one, and on that very day her ministers told her that in three more years the wound would reach the palace.

The edict that sent someone down the Well is dated the second year of her reign.

After Aislinn walked in, the wound stopped. It really did stop. The country lived, and people began to rebuild. They put up a temple over the wound and a ring of wall around that. Their kingdom held on for another sixty years. The prayer on the twenty-eighth floor must have been written in the last of those years. Then the palace began to sink. Nobody knew why. Probably it was her, down there, still pulling. The kingdom ended with the Queen. The people moved away, and a hundred years later no one even remembered what the country had been called.

Only one name came down, and it came down bent out of shape.

The Queen's study was on the twenty-seventh floor. When I walked in, that desk had been sitting there for nearly a thousand years.

Her desk was much smaller than the Grand Master's and much older. She must have used it for many years. The ink had long since dried, and the pen was still in its stand. Everything was properly put away, the papers sorted by year and tied with ribbon, and even the webs went around this desk. She had turned into what she turned into, and her things were still kept like this.

I untied one of the bundles. One bundle a year, one sheet a day, and on each sheet only a date and one line.

Today, the Well is silent.

Today, the Well is silent.

Today, the Well is silent.

I went through five bundles. Only one day was different: Today, her birthday. The Well is silent. The bundles were stacked up three walls. She never had a clerk do it for her. It was all the same hand, getting smaller and shakier as the years went on.

In the middle of the desk lay a single sheet, with a neat clean square around it. She must have picked it up often and put it back in the same spot.

It was the draft of an edict. The writing was beautiful. Only the last line leaned a little.

::lore.f27

I read it a few times and sat down in her chair.

**I asked who would. She spoke before I had finished.**

I'm old now and I understand more than I did then, so I'll tell you what I saw.

She was the Queen, and she thought everything through. You could tell from her desk. She can't have failed to know who would speak first if she asked that question in open court. In the whole country there was one person certain to speak before she had finished asking. That person had sworn an oath to her. That person had been scolded beside her as a child.

She knew.

But she had to ask. The kingdom was falling, someone had to go down, and she was the Queen. She couldn't give an order. An order would have been pushing Aislinn in with her own hands. So all she could do was ask. Maybe while she was asking, she was begging Aislinn in her heart not to speak. She asked very slowly.

I can only guess at that day. The great hall was cold, with all the windows open, because by then the wound was near enough to smell. The ministers stood in two rows, the old ones, the fat ones, the well-dressed ones, every head bowed.

The Queen sat in that chair, twenty-two years old, the same age I was when I went down the Well. She read out the first part of the proclamation and paused. She never paused when she read proclamations.

"Someone must go," she said. "The crown may not. I ask—"

Her sworn blade stood three paces off her right hand. That was the distance the rules laid down. She had been standing there since she was twelve, and she could have measured those three paces with her eyes shut.

"I ask who would."

There was not a sound in the hall. The Queen looked straight down to the far end of the room and didn't dare look right. She was probably counting in her head: one, two, three. If she got to five, that would count as no one, and she could say, then let us think again.

She didn't get to two.

"I'll go."

She had been hearing that voice all her life. It had told her jokes in her ear when they were small. It had spoken the oath in this hall. It had asked her from the next bed, in the middle of the night, if she was cold. Now it spoke in the same tone it would have used to say the weather was fine.

The Queen didn't turn her head. She picked up her pen and wrote it down. Her writing had always been beautiful. Only that last line leaned a little.

She hadn't finished.

On the twenty-fifth floor she said to me, "It was I who sent her down."

Sitting in that chair, I finally understood what she meant.

As I walked out of the study, something came down from the ceiling and swayed in front of my eyes.

A thin little tube, with a small flower open at the tip. Before I could get a proper look, it had stabbed into my neck.

A soul-stealing flower. I only learned the name later. Their roots hide in the cracks of the palace stone. Once that tube was in, my arms and legs went weak. Something was being drawn out of me, warm and very pleasant. I tried to pull it out and couldn't raise my hand. More tubes came from all sides, one into my thigh, one into my waist. I could feel the stones on me heating up. They were being drained.

I went down on my knees. A flower bigger than a human head slowly unfolded out of the wall. In the very middle was a face, eyes closed, a young girl's face. Some palace maid from who knows how long ago.

I bit my lip until it bled, and the pain cleared my head a little. I couldn't raise my hands, so I wouldn't use my hands. I pushed all the heat down to the soles of my feet and lit the fire. It came up all over me and burned me too. The tubes burned through and pulled back, one after another. The face opened its eyes and began to scream, words I couldn't understand.

I crawled over and put my sword through her mouth and into the wall. The flower withered, and in a moment the face had aged a few hundred years.

I lay on the floor, drank my last bottle and peeled off the burned skin, one piece at a time.

I opened another chest that day. It held a few old clothes and a slip of paper, nothing of any use.

::note.p03

I looked up. The ceiling was rock and showed no sign of coming down.

Another lunatic, I thought, and I stuffed the note in my coat and walked on.

Remember this note. Twice in my life I nearly threw away the thing that saved me. One was this note. The other was my master's stick.

## Floor Twenty-Eight

On the twenty-eighth floor there was a small chapel, with room for one person to kneel.

It was the Queen's. The palace had started sinking while she was still alive. She came down every month to look and to wait. Later it was every day. Later still, when she was old, she simply moved down here and never went up again.

On the way to the chapel there was a patch of flowers.

Flowers have no business underground. These grew out of corpses. They were white, each one bigger than my head, with drooping centres that stirred a little. When I came near, one of them turned to face me.

"Hand," it said.

It was Master's voice, talking through his nose.

Another one turned too: "Then don't come back." A third had a southern accent: "Red, you poor thing."

My arms and legs went weak. All I wanted was to walk in among the flowers and get close enough to hear properly, and my legs had already started. The sword tied to my wrist swung as I went, knocking against my thigh, again and again. I looked down and saw it, and only then did I take hold of it.

I spent a whole afternoon burning that patch. They kept talking while they burned, all in the voices of people I knew.

There was a prayer cut into the chapel wall. It wasn't from any scripture. She wrote it herself. She was afraid paper would rot, so she cut it with a graver, very deep, going over every stroke many times.

::lore.f28

When I got to the last line, I went cold all over.

I'd guess she was very old by the time she cut these words, so old that no one in the country remembered her young, and the ministers had been replaced three times over. It had been fifty or sixty years since she sent anyone down the Well. She lived underground by then, and every day she had herself carried to a crack to talk to it.

One day she realised she was the last person in the world who had ever seen Aislinn.

She asked all through the palace. No one could say what Aislinn had looked like. The young chroniclers even got her name wrong. They all said: Your Majesty means the first knight to go down the Well? Yes, very admirable. What was her name again?

That same night she came here and knelt. She had never in her life cried in front of anyone, and this time she cried. She said: Let me not end. If I end, none will remember her name. She wasn't begging anyone. She just couldn't keep from saying it.

The dark heard.

It didn't lie to her. It never lies. It only told her, very gently, what she wanted to hear: **nothing need end.**

No one had ever talked to her like that. Everyone said: Your Majesty must, Your Majesty ought, Your Majesty, the realm comes first. She had waited alone her whole life, and no one had ever asked if she was tired.

**It was the kindest thing anyone had said to me.**

I read that line out loud, my finger on the cuts.

**nothing need end.**

I turned back my sleeve. There were seven strokes of charcoal on it.

The hammer on the fifth floor. The light on the tenth. Twice on the fifteenth. The time on the bridge. The twenty-fifth floor, her own foot, just three floors back.

I had always thought those stone rings were a kindness left by the ones before me. I bought potions from the peddler, woke in the rings, threw up, climbed to my feet and went back. I thought I had real grit. I thought I was strong. The captain, the priest, the forgemaster, my master, every one of them had died, and only I kept standing up.

It had never once spared me. Not one time. It just wouldn't let me end.

I stood in her chapel, cold all over. No one had saved me. No one before me had left me anything. This Well said the same thing to everyone who walked in: nothing need end. And they stayed. The captain stayed at his door for three hundred years. She stayed for nearly a thousand. I had already stayed seven times.

How many times would be enough? By the hundredth time, would I still be me?

I thought of that recipe on the eighteenth floor. I thought of that warm patch of hard skin on my palm.

I took out my charcoal and looked at my sleeve, meaning to mark something. In the end I marked nothing.

The captain on the ninth floor thought it was kind, too.

That's how it works. Yesterday you asked me why the voice under the Well sounds so lovely. I thought about it all night, and here's my answer: because it's the one thing in the world that will never tell you no.

Be especially careful of anything that never tells you no. I tell you no all the time, and you get angry with me all the time. So go on being angry.

## Floor Twenty-Nine

Yesterday that ewe gave birth. You told me the moment you came through the door: two of them, and one is black. I said very good, what are they called? You said you hadn't named them yet. I said you should. You asked why. I said you'd know when you finished this floor.

This was the last floor of the abyss passage. Below it was the temple.

The webs had started to rot and came down at a puff of wind, and the eggs had stopped moving. With the Queen dead, her children had lost their direction. They were still crawling around, but not in swarms any more. I walked through a hall with hundreds of them on the walls, and they saw me and stayed where they were.

One that had just hatched, no bigger than my palm, crawled down a pillar and up my boot. I raised my sword.

It didn't bite me. It climbed all the way up to the roll of tapestry on my back and stopped there, not moving.

I pulled it off and set it on the ground. It crawled back. I set it down again and walked faster, and it couldn't keep up. I looked back. It was still on the ground, crawling my way, very slowly.

Something else was fighting over that hall. With the Queen gone, void spores had drifted in through the cracks in the walls, one after another, like mouldy balls, and they were bursting among her children. When I reached the middle of the hall they were drifting over my head.

The first one burst. I took it for plain dust and breathed some in. My throat swelled straight away, my breath shrank to a thin thread, and my eyes went dark. The hundreds of spiders on the walls took fright and dropped all at once. They had no target. They crawled everywhere, and whatever they crawled on, they bit. I ran, clawing at my throat, unable to see the way, smashing through webs with my body, and another spore burst right in front of me.

I tore off a corner of my clothes, wet it and held it over my face. That was the last of my water. I put my head down and ran for the far end, because the webs were thickest there and the spores couldn't get in. I crashed in, wrapped in silk from head to foot, couldn't move, and just lay there gulping air. Outside it was all chaos, spiders and spores in one big heap, and it was a long time before it went quiet.

I lay in that clump of silk while my breath slowly came back, and my hand found a very old piece of paper. The writing was hard to read, but I had seen that hand in the study. When she wrote this, her hand was no longer a hand.

::lore.f29

"My daughters are many."

I held that paper and thought of the twenty-fifth floor, how she had charged at me straight over her own children and crushed them, and they hadn't even moved out of the way.

She truly did try to do what Aislinn did. She built walls too. She held on too. She couldn't find another way, so she borrowed strength from that thing at the bottom of the Well. But the strength was its strength, so the walls she built wouldn't stay put. She built for nearly a thousand years. What she got was a maze that turned, hundreds of thousands of children who didn't know her, and a self she had nearly forgotten. She kept stroking the same spot on the tapestry, probably for fear of forgetting.

That was who I had struck down. In the moment she stopped to look at me, I ran her through.

I walked back a little way, I can't tell you why. The small one was still crawling. It had come a long way.

I didn't take it with me and I didn't kill it. I just walked around it and left. I can't tell you what that counts as, so don't ask me.

Before I reached the stairs I looked back once. It was crawling very slowly, nearly out of sight now, still headed my way. I thought of myself at five, up on that horse, watching the barn burn, looking back and looking back just like that.

My master didn't look back. But he didn't tell me not to look, either.

At the top of the stairs going down was the last chest of that stretch, a big one with plenty of good things in it. The round shield on my left arm had several cracks by now, and the chest happened to have a piece of iron I could patch it with. Under the iron was a sheet of paper.

::note.p05

Just that one line. No name. The hand was frighteningly steady.

Why would he write that? Shields break. Who doesn't know that?

I took it for a tip about fighting, and I patched my shield.

I wouldn't understand what it really meant until the very end.

## Floor Thirty

This is the place I least want to walk through again. When you read it, you can stop any time you like.

That staircase was longer than I've drawn it.

I came out of the last archway and was standing at the very foot of it. The stairs ran straight up, with floating stones and broken bridges on either side and purple lightning under the bridges. At the top was a throne, so far off it was the size of a fingernail.

The stairs were covered with people.

They were heaped on every step, along with armour, shields, broken swords and torn banners. Some wore the Order's crest. Some wore crests I didn't know. Some wore armour so old I had only seen it in wall paintings. Nearly all of them had fallen the same way, heads upward, hands reaching forward. Not one had been going back.

How many there were, I can't tell you. I counted everything on the way down. That was the one thing I didn't count.

They must all have stepped on stone rings too. Some probably died before they reached one. Some died so many times, and then they didn't want to stand up again, so they didn't. How many times that was, I don't know.

There were murals on the walls either side of the entrance. Most of the colour had gone, but you could see how much care had gone into them. This was the oldest part of the temple. The builders had put the most important thing right at the door, for everyone who came in to see. But everyone who got this far had their eyes on that throne.

::lore.f30

I didn't look long at those murals either. All I could think about was the throne. This is it, I thought. This is the bottom of the Well, that's it sitting up there, that's what everyone on the way down died for.

Every person on those stairs had thought the same.

I started up. I had to walk on them. There was no other way. At the three hundred and forty-second step I looked up, and he was looking at me. He sat there as red as something just lifted from a forge, his muscles in blocks like shell, with orange light showing through the cracks. He had a pair of curved black horns and a great pair of ragged wings behind him, chin on one hand, in no hurry at all, as if he had already seen too many like me.

He stood. When his wings opened, they blocked the light of the whole temple.

I had never met anything like him.

One swing of his claws covered everything in front of him, and he swung twice. I caught the first on my shield and my arm went numb to the shoulder. The second sent me flying, shield and all. I went back a dozen steps and landed in a heap of bones and armour. The edge of an old shield cut into my back. I looked round. It was a very, very old round shield with a circle and eight rays on it, the paint nearly gone.

Our crest. At least half of the people in that heap were carrying shields like it.

He came down, and there was another of him beside him, and then another. They weren't illusions. They really did cut you. I'd cut one down and another would turn up. He laughed very loudly. He was the first thing I'd met down there that laughed like a person.

Three of them closed in. I blocked the claws on my left and the ones on my right were already in my side. My armour split at the waist, and the blood jumped out and ran warm down to my foot. I turned and cut the one on the right, very deep, and it scattered into black smoke. I thought I had found something that worked. I turned back, and the real one was right in front of me, wings open, and they closed around me. I couldn't see anything. His sword came down from above.

I never even saw what was in the sky.

The eighth stroke.

The second time, I used the bow. I stood at the foot of the stairs and shot at him, and he came down laughing. I backed up and shot, treading on the bodies, and slipped on a helmet. His copies had already got around behind me. I put one down, and just then I went hot all over and the air above my head lit up.

No sound. Nothing at all.

The ninth stroke. I woke in the stone ring with even my hair smelling burned and all my skin hurting, and I still had no idea how I had died. I sat there looking at the nine strokes on my sleeve and thought of that wall on the twenty-eighth floor. The old peddler held out a bottle to me, and I stared at his robe for a long time.

The third time I walked very slowly.

There were more people on the stairs than I had seen before. There weren't really more. I had started to be able to read them. Some of the skulls were split. Some were burned. Some were a great patch of char, with the armour melted into it. A lot of the charred ones had their hands raised in front of them, toward the top of the stairs and that throne. They had all been looking at him when they died.

Past the two hundredth step I looked down and saw a very small person. It was a child, fifteen at the most, wearing our crest and armour far too big for his body. In his hand was a short wooden-hilted sword, the kind the Ward-hall gives out. He had fallen facing up the stairs, arm reaching out, mouth open. His elbow was against his ribs. After all that time dead, his elbow was still against his ribs.

The instructors had told me about it. Many times in the past the Crown had needed people in a hurry, and the Order had sent down children who weren't grown. I had seen the dates on the plates. I had just never worked out how old they were.

I laid his arm down and set it straight. I know that does no good.

Then the corner of a paper in my coat jabbed me in the ribs.

**Mind the sky when it falleth.**

I looked up. Something flickered in the purple clouds.

The sky was full of fire. It was dropping out of the clouds in lumps, each one bigger than me, right where I stood and right where my next step would land. They fell without a sound. If you didn't look up, you'd never know.

I rolled left.

The spot I'd been standing on blew apart, and the dead on the stairs went flying. A hand landed on my face. I laughed as I rolled, a very ugly laugh. Thank you, lunatic. After that I looked up with every cut. Many people must have died here before me, never knowing where the fire came from. And one of them got hit, didn't quite die, and crawled back to write that line down for someone he didn't know.

I fought him with one eye on the sky. When the fire fell, I led his copies under it and let them take it for me. I ran up ten steps and looked at the sky, then ten steps more. I drank four potions. By the time I reached him, my whole left side was blood, but my feet hadn't stopped. For the first time, his laughing stopped.

He swung hard, twice in a row. I didn't block any more. I went forward under the first one, inside his arm. The second hit nothing and clawed a stone step to pieces. My burning sword went in at the root of his wing. He made a noise, and it wasn't a laugh.

Once he was hurt, everything around us went dark, so black I couldn't even see the lightning. There was only him and me on one small patch, with everything pressing in from all sides until I could hardly breathe. He got faster. He brought his claws down, I raised my shield, and the shield broke and fell in pieces at my feet.

**The shield breaketh first.** Fine, I thought. I know.

With my left hand empty, I actually felt lighter. I looked down at a shard by my foot. It had the mark the Ward-hall instructors burned into their shields, a circle and eight rays, now split in two.

I took the sword in my free left hand as well, set every stone burning, fire, then ice, then thunder, and charged into the dark.

I don't know how long that fight lasted. In the end I was standing in front of the throne, his head had fallen on the seat, and his wings were still twitching. The blackened skin on my hand had spread to my wrist. I knelt and rested my forehead on the cold step.

It was over. I had beaten the master of the abyss, and somehow I was still alive. And then what? I didn't think any further.

His chest split open, and a wisp of smoke came out.

It was black.

I looked up. All the smoke before had been white. Only this wisp was black. It wasn't tired at all. It circled me once, slowly.

::soul.f30#0
