# Book Six: Two Girls

Floors 26 to 30

## Floor Twenty-Six

The moment you came through the door today, you told me your grandmother's name was Aisling too. I know. I carved the plate on her coffin.

You asked if she knew whose name it had been first. She didn't. She told me it was her grandmother's name, and that was all she knew. What called you that day at the Well was using her voice. You can think about that on your own, slowly, when you're a bit older.

Once the webs let go, the paths stopped moving. Only then could I see that those crooked passages had once been a palace.

Some rooms the webs hadn't filled. In the kitchen the pots were still lined up neatly. The musicians' room had a lute with broken strings. Further in was a small room with a very low door, made for children. Inside were two small beds. They were meant to stand apart. Someone had pushed them together, and the legs had left two scratches across the floor.

A very short wooden sword leaned against the wall.

I didn't touch it. I just stood in the doorway for a while. The dust in that room was thinner than anywhere else. Someone had kept on sweeping it, until very, very late.

Past that was a long corridor. The windows on both sides looked out on solid rock, because the whole palace had later sunk into the ground. This used to be the surface. Sunlight used to come in through these windows. At the end of the corridor was the throne room. Half the roof had fallen in, and webs hung down through the hole, gone grey.

Set into the floor in front of the throne was a brass plate, where people knelt to swear. Knees had worn two shallow dips in the middle of it. The words on it could still be read.

::lore.f26

"Her sword for all her days."

I crouched and put my fingers in the two dips. They were small. Whoever it was didn't have big knees.

**This picture is a guess.** I'm no good at drawing faces, so I never actually drew it.

They must have grown up together. You can see that in the tapestry. One in a crown, one with a wooden sword, both still children, the crown still too big. The one with the crown couldn't just wander out of doors, so the other was picked to keep her company, like her shadow, and started on the wooden sword.

Someone must have rapped her hand every morning.

They must have fought, too. There must have been an afternoon when the one with the crown grabbed the wooden sword and said it was her turn to be the knight, and they both got a scolding. They probably had words only they understood, the kind they could still use when they were grown, in front of a full court.

Then one day the one with the crown sat down in this chair. The one with the wooden sword had a real sword now, and she walked out onto this plate and knelt. The whole court was watching. She raised the sword and spoke the oath in a loud voice, every word very solemn.

My guess is they were both trying not to laugh.

I really do guess that. They were young. Nobody yet knew what those words actually meant.

I knelt for a moment beside the plate, not on it. That wasn't my place.

On the way out there was a slanting crack beside the path, and through it I could see a long way down. It was purple down there. Lightning flashed in the clouds, and in the clouds hung blocks of stone with towers still standing on them. Farthest of all stood a very, very tall staircase.

## Floor Twenty-Seven

The Queen's study was on the twenty-seventh floor.

Her desk was much smaller than the Grand Master's and much older. She must have used it for many years. The ink had long since dried, and the pen was still in its stand. Everything was properly put away, the papers sorted by year and tied with ribbon, and even the webs went around this desk. She had turned into what she turned into, and her things were still kept like this.

I untied one of the bundles. One bundle a year, one sheet a day, and on each sheet only a date and one line.

Today, the Well is silent.

Today, the Well is silent.

Today, the Well is silent.

I went through five bundles. Only one day was different: Today, her birthday. The Well is silent. The bundles were stacked up three walls. She never had a clerk do it for her. It was all the same hand, getting smaller and shakier as the years went on.

In the middle of the desk lay a single sheet, with a neat clean square around it. She must have picked it up often and put it back in the same spot.

It was the draft of an edict. The writing was beautiful. Only the last line leaned a little.

::lore.f27

I read it a few times and sat down in her chair.

**I asked who would. She spoke before I had finished.**

I'm old now and I understand more than I did then, so I'll tell you what I saw.

She was the Queen, and she thought everything through. You could tell from her desk. She can't have failed to know who would speak first if she asked that question in open court. In the whole country there was one person certain to speak before she had finished asking. That person had sworn an oath to her. That person had been scolded beside her as a child.

She knew.

But she had to ask. The kingdom was falling, someone had to go down, and she was the Queen. She couldn't give an order. An order would have been pushing Aislinn in with her own hands. So all she could do was ask. Maybe while she was asking, she was begging Aislinn in her heart not to speak. She asked very slowly.

She hadn't finished.

On the twenty-fifth floor she said to me, "It was I who sent her down."

Sitting in that chair, I finally understood what she meant.

I opened another chest that day. It held a few old clothes and a slip of paper, nothing of any use.

::note.p03

I looked up. The ceiling was rock and showed no sign of coming down.

Another lunatic, I thought, and I stuffed the note in my coat and walked on.

Remember this note. Twice in my life I nearly threw away the thing that saved me. One was this note. The other was my master's stick.

## Floor Twenty-Eight

On the twenty-eighth floor there was a small chapel, with room for one person to kneel.

It was the Queen's. The palace had started sinking while she was still alive. She came down every month to look and to wait. Later it was every day. Later still, when she was old, she simply moved down here and never went up again.

On the way to the chapel there was a patch of flowers.

Flowers have no business underground. These grew out of corpses. They were white, each one bigger than my head, with drooping centres that stirred a little. When I came near, one of them turned to face me.

"Hand," it said.

It was Master's voice, talking through his nose.

Another one turned too: "Then don't come back." A third had a southern accent: "Red, you poor thing."

My arms and legs went weak. All I wanted was to walk in among the flowers and get close enough to hear properly, and my legs had already started. The sword tied to my wrist swung as I went, knocking against my thigh, again and again. I looked down and saw it, and only then did I take hold of it.

I spent a whole afternoon burning that patch. They kept talking while they burned, all in the voices of people I knew.

There was a prayer cut into the chapel wall. It wasn't from any scripture. She wrote it herself. She was afraid paper would rot, so she cut it with a graver, very deep, going over every stroke many times.

::lore.f28

When I got to the last line, I went cold all over.

**This picture is a guess.**

She was very old by then, so old that no one in the country remembered her young, and the ministers had been replaced three times over. It had been fifty or sixty years since she sent anyone down the Well. Every day she had herself carried underground to talk to a crack.

One day she realised she was the last person in the world who had ever seen Aislinn.

She asked all through the palace. No one could say what Aislinn had looked like. The young chroniclers even got her name wrong. They all said: Your Majesty means the first knight to go down the Well? Yes, very admirable. What was her name again?

That same night she came here and knelt. She had never in her life cried in front of anyone, and this time she cried. She said: Let me not end. If I end, none will remember her name. She wasn't begging anyone. She just couldn't keep from saying it.

The dark heard.

It didn't lie to her. It never lies. It only told her, very gently, what she wanted to hear: **nothing need end.**

No one had ever talked to her like that. Everyone said: Your Majesty must, Your Majesty ought, Your Majesty, the realm comes first. She had waited alone her whole life, and no one had ever asked if she was tired.

**It was the kindest thing anyone had said to me.**

The captain on the ninth floor thought it was kind, too.

That's how it works. Yesterday you asked me why the voice under the Well sounds so lovely. I thought about it all night, and here's my answer: because it's the one thing in the world that will never tell you no.

Be especially careful of anything that never tells you no. I tell you no all the time, and you get angry with me all the time. So go on being angry.

## Floor Twenty-Nine

This was the last floor of the abyss passage. Below it was the temple.

The webs had started to rot and came down at a puff of wind, and the eggs had stopped moving. With the Queen dead, her children had lost their direction. They were still crawling around, but not in swarms any more. I walked through a hall with hundreds of them on the walls, and they saw me and stayed where they were.

One that had just hatched, no bigger than my palm, crawled down a pillar and up my boot. I raised my sword.

It didn't bite me. It climbed all the way up to the roll of tapestry on my back and stopped there, not moving.

I pulled it off and set it on the ground. It crawled back. I set it down again and walked faster, and it couldn't keep up. I looked back. It was still on the ground, crawling my way, very slowly.

In a clump of old silk at the far end of the hall I found a very old piece of paper. The writing was hard to read, but I had seen that hand in the study. When she wrote this, her hand was no longer a hand.

::lore.f29

"My daughters are many."

I stood holding that paper and thought of the twenty-fifth floor, how she had charged at me straight over her own children and crushed them, and they hadn't even moved out of the way.

She truly did try to do what Aislinn did. She built walls too. She held on too. She couldn't find another way, so she borrowed strength from that thing at the bottom of the Well. But the strength was its strength, so the walls she built wouldn't stay put. She built for nearly a thousand years. What she got was a maze that turned, hundreds of thousands of children who didn't know her, and a self she had nearly forgotten. She kept stroking the same spot on the tapestry, probably for fear of forgetting.

That was who I had struck down. In the moment she stopped to look at me, I ran her through.

I walked back a little way, I can't tell you why. The small one was still crawling. It had come a long way.

I didn't take it with me and I didn't kill it. I just walked around it and left. I can't tell you what that counts as, so don't ask me.

At the top of the stairs going down was the last chest of that stretch, a big one with plenty of good things in it. The round shield on my left arm had come off a rack in the Ward-hall, and by now it had several cracks. The chest happened to have a piece of iron I could patch it with. Under the iron was a sheet of paper.

::note.p05

Just that one line. No name. The hand was frighteningly steady.

Why would he write that? Shields break. Who doesn't know that?

I took it for a tip about fighting, and I patched my shield.

I wouldn't understand what it really meant until the very end.

## Floor Thirty

That staircase was longer than I've drawn it.

I came out of the last archway and was standing at the very foot of it. The stairs ran straight up, with floating stones and broken bridges on either side and purple lightning under the bridges. At the top was a throne, so far off it was the size of a fingernail.

The stairs were covered with people.

They were heaped on every step, along with armour, shields, broken swords and torn banners. Some wore the Order's crest. Some wore crests I didn't know. Some wore armour so old I had only seen it in wall paintings. Nearly all of them had fallen the same way, heads upward, hands reaching forward. Not one had been going back.

How many there were, I can't tell you. I counted everything on the way down. That was the one thing I didn't count.

There were murals on the walls either side of the entrance. Most of the colour had gone, but you could see how much care had gone into them. This was the oldest part of the temple. The builders had put the most important thing right at the door, for everyone who came in to see. But everyone who got this far had their eyes on that throne.

::lore.f30

I didn't look long either. All I could think about was the throne. This is it, I thought. This is the bottom of the Well, that's it sitting up there, that's what everyone on the way down died for.

Every person on those stairs had thought the same.

I started up. I had to walk on them. There was no other way. At the three hundred and forty-second step I looked up, and he was looking at me. He sat there with horns on his head and a great pair of ragged wings behind him, chin on one hand, in no hurry at all, as if he had already seen too many like me.

He stood. When his wings opened, they blocked the light of the whole temple.

I had never met anything like him. One swing of his sword covered everything in front of him. I caught one on my shield, my arm went numb to the shoulder, and I flew back a dozen steps. He came down, and there was another of him beside him, and then another. They weren't illusions. They really did cut you. I'd cut one down and another would turn up. He laughed very loudly. He was the first thing I'd met down there that laughed.

I was reeling, with my whole mind on what was in front of me. Then the corner of a paper in my coat jabbed me in the ribs, and I suddenly remembered that lunatic on the twenty-seventh floor.

**Mind the sky when it falleth.**

I looked up.

The sky was full of fire. It was dropping out of the purple clouds in lumps, each one bigger than me, right where I stood and right where my next step would land. They fell without a sound. If you didn't look up, you'd never know.

I rolled left.

The spot I'd been standing on blew apart, and the dead on the stairs went flying. I laughed as I rolled, a very ugly laugh. Thank you, lunatic. After that I looked up with every cut. Many people must have died here before me, never knowing where the fire came from. And one of them got hit, didn't quite die, and crawled back to write that line down for someone he didn't know.

Once he was hurt, everything around us went dark, so black I couldn't even see the lightning. There was only him and me on one small patch, with everything pressing in from all sides until I could hardly breathe. He got faster. He brought his sword down, I raised my shield, and the shield broke and fell in pieces at my feet.

**The shield breaketh first.** Fine, I thought. I know.

I took the sword in my free left hand as well, set every stone burning, fire, then ice, then thunder, and charged into the dark.

I don't know how long that fight lasted. In the end I was standing in front of the throne, his head had fallen on the seat, and his wings were still twitching. The black had spread to my wrist. I knelt and rested my forehead on the cold step.

It was over. I had beaten the master of the abyss, and somehow I was still alive. And then what? I didn't think any further.

His chest split open, and a wisp of smoke came out.

It was black.

I looked up. All the smoke before had been white. Only this wisp was black. It wasn't tired at all. It circled me once, slowly.

::soul.f30#0

It was his voice. He was laughing.

::soul.f30#1

I looked at the wing. It twitched. The wound was closing, and thin little things were already growing out of the cut.

::soul.f30#2

The black smoke slipped into a crack under the throne.

A sound came up from below. It wasn't a song, it wasn't a bell, and it wasn't a knock.

With all the strength I had left, I pushed the throne aside.

Underneath was a staircase, going on down.

The people on those stairs. Each of them believed him to be the end.

The number on my sleeve was nine thousand two hundred and eighteen.

I stood up and went down.
