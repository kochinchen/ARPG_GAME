# Book Four: The Master

Floors 16 to 20

## Floor Sixteen

After the smoke cleared, it took me a day to stand up. My wounds healed fast, faster than they should have. The hard patch on my palm wouldn't pick off, so I left it alone. A dead miner by the path had a leather glove that was still good. I took it and put it on.

The mine kept going down, and now I started to see the places where business got done: foremen's sheds, tables for weighing stones, and a small stone house with the royal arms over the door. Inside were scales, a magnifying glass and a row of little bottles. Papers lay all over the floor, and one of them was written in a particularly fine hand.

::lore.f16

I like this assayer. He was another honest man.

His job was to assay stones. Hand him a warm stone and the rules said he had to write down where it came from. He had probably burned it, soaked it and cracked it open to look. Then he sat down here, picked up his pen and wrote "The assayer declineth to say," as if he were talking about someone else, and he quit. He knew the answer, so he left.

This mine was dug by another kingdom, older than the crown we have now. I pieced that together slowly from the papers in these sheds. They found warm stones in the mountain, and at first they only used them to warm their hands. Miners kept them in their pockets in winter. Then a forgemaster found that if you set one into a sword, the sword got lighter and faster. On the strength of that the kingdom won three wars and doubled its land. The king paid the miners bonuses, the mine went deeper, the village grew, and the valley filled up with new houses.

Those were probably the happiest few decades they ever had.

Then the miners stopped feeling tired, and stopped wanting to go home. The losses in the ledgers went up every year, and so did the bonuses. Nobody thought the two had anything to do with each other.

How many people in a country know the answer and won't say? And how many just quit and walk away?

That's what I think now, though. At the time I only thought he had been too vague, and that he should have written the answer down properly. I put the paper away and kept walking. In the ash outside the stone house, that line of bare footprints went past the door and didn't come in.

I had seen arms very like the ones over that door, though not down here.

The year I was twenty, a royal clerk came up the mountain. He was very young and very polite, and he brought a cartload of wheat. The cart flew a small flag, and that crest was on it. The Grand Master was so happy he went out to unload it himself. The man said His Majesty took a great interest in the traditions of the Order and wished to collect some old records: ledgers from the mining days, maps, recipes, anything at all. Left up here, they would only feed the rats.

Half our library was already empty. The Grand Master gave him the key. I was sent to help carry, and I carried for three days. The crates were heavy and had centuries of dust on them. The clerk wore gloves and went through them book by book, and anything useful went into the cart. I asked him what the crown wanted with all this.

"For the archive," he said, and gave me a little smile.

When he left, there were more crates on the cart than when he came. We ate that wheat for two winters. Two-Fingers stood in the yard watching the cart go, and the two fingers on his right hand wouldn't stop shaking. That night he got drunk. I had never seen him drink. He said to the wall, "They've remembered."

I thought at the time he meant the Order.

Further down I met my first thing that burrows.

First the little stones on the ground jumped. Then the ground rose under my feet. I sprang back, and a fat white worm as thick as my waist shot out of the earth. Its mouth was a ring of teeth, and it bit down on the spot where I had just been standing. It went back under, and the ground next to it rose. One, then two. I couldn't see them, I could only listen. I shut my eyes, set the point of my sword on the ground and felt for the shaking. If Floorboards knew I was using his lessons this way, he'd probably have been very pleased.

When the shaking reached my feet, I jumped straight up and drove the sword down with both hands. The whole blade went into the dirt, and it hit. The worm rolled up out of the ground, thrashing with my sword in it, and sprayed slime all over my face. It took days to wash that smell off. I hung on to the hilt and got dragged across the ground for a dozen paces or so before I managed to cut its head open.

The miners' quarters were cut into the rock, one cell after another, a lot like the tombs on Floor Five. Each cell had a plank bed and a spare pair of shoes. There were marks scratched on the walls, four strokes and a bar, counting the days until the shift was over. Some men had carved the names of their families. One had carved a woman, badly, with very large breasts. Under one bed was a small wooden horse, only half done, its legs not yet separated.

I picked it up and looked at it. The man was good with a knife. He just ran out of time.

Who was he going to take it up to? Even if that child lived to be old, the child had been dead for centuries. I put the horse back under the bed and straightened the shoes. I did this kind of thing more and more on the way down, putting dead people's belongings back where they had been. It probably did no good at all.

To reach the end of this floor you had to go through the longest of the main tunnels.

Rails ran down the middle of it, and on the rails stood a mine cart, still half full of stones. I was halfway along when a low sound came out of the dark ahead, like someone dragging something very heavy. Then the ground started to shake.

I raised the lamp. A pair of curved horns pushed out of the dark, and behind them came a body like a small hill, plated all over with bone. A horned beast. There was more than one. There was another behind it, and another, one after the next, filling the whole tunnel. They lowered their heads and began to run.

The tunnel was only four paces wide, with rock on both sides and nowhere to hide. I turned and ran, and I couldn't outrun them. I jumped onto the cart and kicked out the wooden wedge that held the wheels. The cart started to roll. That stretch of rail ran downhill.

At first it was slower than walking. The first beast's horns reached the back of the cart and the whole thing jumped. Half a load of stones rolled over me, and then all at once we were going fast.

The rails curved away in the dark and I couldn't see ahead. I nearly lost the lamp, so I bit down on the handle and lay flat in the cart, gripping the sides with both hands. All I could hear was the wheels screaming and the thunder behind me. The cart tipped over on two wheels and slammed back down. Something swept over my head and took a tuft of my hair with it. It was a low wooden beam. I got lower. Behind me came a crash as the lead beast hit the beam, and the whole tunnel began to come down.

I saw a little red light ahead. The rails ended.

Past the break was a stretch of lava about twenty paces wide, with more rails on the far side. I had no time to think. As the cart reached the break I stamped on its rim and jumped, and the cart and I went out into the air together. For an instant I saw the red below me, and the cart turning over under my feet. It fell into the lava without even a sound.

I hit the rock on the far side, and only my top half made it. My legs hung over the edge and my boots began to smoke. I climbed with my chin and my elbows, dug my nails into the cracks and pulled myself up an inch at a time.

When I looked back, the beasts had stopped on the other side. The one in front had a length of beam hanging from its horns. It looked at me for a while, lowered its head and turned around, and one by one they walked back into the dark.

I rolled over, lay on the ground and howled with laughter. I laughed for a long time before I noticed that half a front tooth had snapped off. It grew back the next day.

I thought that was great fun at the time. It was the first fun I'd had since coming down the Well. When I thought about that later, I couldn't laugh at all.

At its end the tunnel opened into a crack halfway up a cliff, with nothing outside. A cold wind blew up and thinned the smell of burning on me.

I went to the edge and looked. I had no idea there was so much room under the ground. Far away on the other side, a mountain stood in the mist, and on the mountain stood a fortress. Its walls rose in tier after tier, one tower after another, and all its flags were in rags. I knew the crest. I was wearing it.

It was our fortress. The Order had built it underground. The books up in the Order gave it one line.

When the Order was at its strongest, two thousand people lived here. That was over five hundred years ago. The seal had come badly loose, and the crown was so frightened that it sent the best children in the country up the mountain and built this fortress under the ground, hoping to shut the Well for good. Our five halls were laid down at that time. Later the loosening slowly stopped. The people sent to the fortress never came back up, one batch after another. Some said they were still on watch. Others said they had long since forgotten how to get up.

Floorboards told me about the fortress once. He was drunk, lying face down in the yard, and he told me that when he was young he had seen a map of somewhere underground, with the five halls on it and a bridge. I asked him where that was. He said it was our real home, and the place on the mountain was only the porter's lodge. When I asked again the next day he said he had never told me any such thing, and sent me off to walk the cellars in the dark.

Then something flashed at the fortress gate. It was too far off to be more than a dot. First orange-red, then blue-white, then gold like lightning.

Then orange-red again.

Only one person in the world practises like that, always fire, then ice, then thunder, and the order never changes. I asked him why when I was little. He said it was easier to remember.

I have drawn this picture many times. In the little circle at the top left I am seven or eight. He is sitting on a rock, correcting the way I hold my wooden sword.

He rarely spoke, as I told you. He taught by doing a thing once and then watching you do it, and when you got it wrong he rapped your hand. When I was eight I asked him why the other instructors gave praise and he didn't. He thought about it for so long that I decided he hadn't heard. Then he took an apple out of his pocket, put it in my hand and walked off.

I kept that apple until it was nearly rotten before I could bring myself to eat it.

The day he taught me to put fire on a blade, I set my own sleeve alight. He slapped the flames out with his hand, which blistered all across the back, and he said nothing. The next day he taught it again: fire, then ice, then thunder. It was many years before it occurred to me that he had probably settled on that order for my sake, because my memory was terrible when I was small.

I stood on that cliff until my hands and feet were cold. For ten years I had practised in my head what I would say when I saw him.

But it had never crossed my mind that when I found him, he would be standing in front of a gate. The captain stood in front of a gate. The priest guarded an altar. That mountain guarded the mine. I had seen all of them.

I knew what would be standing in front of that gate.

I started down, and I walked fast. The numbers I wrote on my sleeve for that stretch, I can't read now myself.

## Floor Seventeen

A strange thing happened on the way.

A skeleton charged me. I had killed thousands of those by then, so without thinking I used my usual move: left foot forward, blade sweeping up from lower right to upper left, then turn with it into a second cut.

It stepped back half a pace and let that go by. Then it put its left foot forward and swept up from lower right to upper left.

It caught me in the shoulder. The wound wasn't bad, but I froze. I stayed frozen too long, and it cut again, turning with the stroke.

That was my second cut.

I smashed it, crouched down and looked. It was a pile of ordinary bones. After that I started paying attention. There was a skeleton mage that held back its usual fireball and hit my feet first with a little ice spike, a habit I'd had since Floor Seven. The archers took to shooting first at the side I usually dodged to.

These things have no brains. They are dead people. All they can do is walk and hack.

But something was teaching them.

I thought of the page torn from the scripture on Floor Thirteen: It only learneth. I thought of Floor Twelve, the day I fought my hardest.

Later I opened a chest.

::note.p04

I laughed, and it wasn't a pretty laugh. The writing was crooked, with long trailing tails. Whoever wrote it was probably hurt, and used what strength was left on this one line. No name, nothing about who it was meant for. It just seemed too ridiculous to leave unsaid.

I thought it was ridiculous too.

I fought badly for the next few fights. I had started to be afraid of my own hands.

One of them was three skeletons in miners' clothes, the kind I could normally finish in a couple of blows. The first used my upward sweep, and I dodged it. The second used the step I always take after that dodge. The third was already standing where that step would land, waiting. Three of myself ran me in circles. I took a cut across the belly and my guts nearly came out. I held the wound shut with one hand, leaned against the back of a mine cart and drank a potion, and watched the three of them come slowly toward me. They still walked like dead men. The moves in their hands were the ones I had practised in the yard for seventeen years.

That feeling is hard to describe. It's like finding someone wearing your clothes, standing at your front door.

In the end I won in a very ugly way. I pushed the mine cart down the slope to knock them over, and stamped on them while they were getting up. If Two-Fingers had seen that, he'd have cut off his last two fingers as well.

That day the line of bare footprints turned up again, right beside the chest. It went half a circle around it before carrying on down, as if he had stopped to look at something. There was blood in the footprints, and it hadn't quite dried.

I followed for a while, into an old tunnel. I didn't find the footprints. What I walked into was a nest of fire-sac crawlers. They are bugs with swollen bellies, as big as dogs, with a sac on the back full of something that burns. They don't crawl fast and they're in no hurry to bite. They just keep edging up against you. I cut the first one open and the whole sac blew, and fire went all over me. The second blew next, and the second set off the third. The tunnel was narrow, and the blast kept slamming back and forth inside it. I went completely deaf. My eyebrows and the front of my hair were gone.

I crawled back on my stomach and saw a dozen more coming slowly out of the dark, the sacs on their backs swelling and sinking. I took out the short bow and burst them one at a time from a distance. Every time one burst, a rock fell from the roof. At the ninth, the whole roof of that stretch came down and cut me off from the tunnel.

The footprints were on the other side of the fall. I stood in front of that heap of rock and got my breath back for a long time, still smoking. Then I turned around and looked for another way.

From that day on I changed my moves. Once I had used a stroke twice, I stopped opening with it. It's exhausting. Unlearning what you have drilled since you were five is harder than learning something new. And I didn't know if it helped, because I didn't know how much it had already seen.

A report was hanging in the foreman's shed on this floor.

::lore.f17

I laid my hand on the rock wall. The seams lay under the stone, line after line, like the veins on the back of a hand. I waited a while.

Before long a thin red line came slowly toward me from deep in the rock. It came to rest right under my palm and stopped.

My palm was warm. So was the stone.

A core the size of a fist pushed out of the wall, freshly grown. I took my hand away and walked off without it.

After a dozen steps or so I went back and picked it up. You wanted the truth, didn't you? That's the truth.

## Floor Eighteen

Today you saw the hard skin on my palm and asked if it hurts. It doesn't. It never has. That's what's so frightening about it.

Floor Eighteen was hard going. One stretch was completely flooded with lava, with only the mine-cart rails left standing over it, two thin lines of iron, burning red. There was no other way. I tore the hem off my cloak, tied it around my boots and stepped on. The cloth started smoking at once. Halfway across, the rails began to sway.

A quake beast stood up on the far bank, built like a great ape, and the mossy boulders on its back lifted one by one. It lifted a front foot and stamped.

The whole line of rail jumped like a plucked string. I went down on my knees and grabbed the iron with both hands. My palms sizzled and the skin stuck to the rail. At the second stamp, the stretch ahead of me broke and sagged toward the lava. So I used the rail as a rope and slid down it, my feet just short of the lava. The cloth on my soles burned away in a moment. I drove my sword into the rock wall and hung from it.

It kept stamping, and each stamp brought red-hot rocks down on my head. I edged sideways along the wall an inch at a time, one hand on the sword, the other digging into the cracks. When I got under its feet, all I could see above me was belly and four legs like pillars. I hit the joint of one foot with fire and ice in turns. Nothing hates sudden heat and cold like stone does. On the third round the joint split. When it stamped, the leg broke, and the whole body toppled sideways, half of it into the lava. It screamed for a long time.

I climbed out onto the bank and took my hand off the hilt, and a layer of my palm stayed behind on it. I drank a potion. By the next day it had grown back.

All the way to the forgemaster's door, I was thinking about that hand. I no longer knew if it still counted as mine.

The forgemaster's workshop was on this floor, built beside a river of lava. I'd guess it was the best spot in the whole mine.

A good old craftsman hangs each tool exactly where his hand will fall on it, and this one had. The handles of the tongs were worn bright. The small hammers were lined up neatly by size. The anvil was cracked. On the wall were charcoal drawings showing how to set a stone into a sword, with the very kind of socket my own sword had.

So he was the one who invented it.

Next to the charcoal drawings a list was nailed to the wall. I only meant to glance at it, and then I stopped.

It was a recipe. Grey powder, how fine to grind it, how much water to add, how to seal it in small bottles, how long until it turned red. Beside it was a note: Works wonders on wounds. A miner can work two shifts in a day.

Grey powder.

I took a potion from my pack, the kind the old peddler sold. Then I dug out the last empty bottle the cook had given me, with a red stain still on its lip. I opened the full one and smelled it. Old keys. I poured a little into my hand. There was a very fine bright powder in the red, glittering, and it was still warm on my palm.

The cook said the recipe had come down through the Order. There was a bag of grey powder, and all she did was add water.

I had drunk more than thirty bottles on the way down.

So it wasn't only that I had those stones on my sword. They had been in my stomach for a long time. Since Floor One, since the first time I smelled that iron. Maybe longer. When I had a fever as a child, the cook fed me half a bottle. Every child in the Order had drunk it.

I remembered helping the cook make the potion when I was sixteen.

After Big Lad and the others were taken away, the kitchen was short of hands, and the cook told me to help. The potion was made once a year, at the very back of the storeroom, behind a locked door. She brought out that bag of grey powder and an old wooden spoon. One spoonful, one bottle. She was very careful about it, far more careful than she was with the porridge.

I asked her where the powder came from. She said the Order had always had it. It was left from hundreds of years ago. There had once been a big barrel, and now there was only this bag.

"What happens when it runs out?"

She looked up at me, and her hands stopped for a moment. She was a very fat woman. For the first time I noticed that her eyes were small and had a lot of fine lines at the corners.

"When it's gone, it's gone," she said. "And good."

I asked why.

She didn't answer. She sealed a bottle, put it on the shelf and picked up the next empty one. After a while she said, "I came up the mountain when I was twenty. My father worked in the royal iron mine. He drank that stuff until his hands turned black, and he still went down." She scraped the wooden spoon clean, very hard, not leaving a speck of grey on it. "Stop asking questions and go split the wood."

She knew. She didn't know all of it, but she knew some. And she went on making it, one spoonful, one bottle, and she still pushed it into my pack. I was going down the Well, and without potions I would certainly die.

When she pushed it in, she added another piece of burnt crust.

I don't know what to think about her. I've been trying to for nearly fifty years.

On the ground by the anvil lay a stone slab, with words cut into it by chisel.

::lore.f18

"They will not."

I pictured him chiselling those words. Men who work iron don't talk much. He knew they had dug too deep, had worn the seal thin, and that this ring was close to giving way. He tried to talk them out of it. Nobody listened, and the crown was very pleased with the results. So he walked into the fire himself and patched the ring with his own body. For hundreds of years he burned and held, and the fire melted him as he held, until he became the mountain I knocked down.

In a corner of the workshop hung a formal coat, so old it fell apart at a touch. It was the kind you wear to go to the palace.

He probably wore it once. He rode for a long time, reached the capital and stood in the great chamber, not knowing what to do with his hands. He was the best forgemaster in the country. The king was very polite to him and told someone to bring him a seat. He stayed on his feet and said: Your Majesty, those stones aren't ore. They are a hand reaching out of the Well, and every one we dig helps it reach further.

The room went quiet for a moment. The king asked, will that mean less this year?

He said people will die.

The king said miners die anyway.

He went back to the mine, hung up the coat and never took it down again. He wiped the anvil clean, hung up his tools and chiselled his last words into the slab. He walked to the edge of the lava river, and when he put his hand in, he was probably still wondering whether he had done up the buttons on that coat.

I sat there for a long time.

Then I laid my sword on the anvil and pried the stones out of the sockets, one by one.

There were six in it by then, none of them small. I lined them up on the anvil, still warm. Then I reached for the sword.

I could barely lift it.

My hand dropped and the point hit the floor. The Order issued me that sword and I had trained with it for more than ten years, and I could not remember it ever being this heavy. One swing and my wrist ached. The second swing was so slow it wouldn't have hit a skeleton. All at once I was so hungry that my sight went black, and my whole body began to hurt. Every wound that had healed too fast hurt at once.

I sat there with both hands shaking and looked at the stones on the anvil.

The forgemaster had already told me. He told me as he died, and he cut his last words here as well. Stop digging, he said.

But I thought about what lay ahead. I thought about that spark of light in front of the fortress gate: fire, then ice, then thunder.

I put the stones back, one by one.

As I put them back, my hands stopped shaking, and I wasn't hungry any more. I hated how good that felt.

Something was sniffing at the door.

It was a lava-jaw beast. I had seen them from a distance a few times in the mine, never this close. It was bigger than a horse, and its lower jaw was one solid piece of red-hot rock. When it opened its mouth, the heat set the tools in the workshop ringing. I suppose the smell of the stones coming out had drawn it. They eat those.

It smashed through the door frame and came in. I jumped onto the anvil and from there onto its back. It swung its head and its jaw bit down on the anvil, and a block of iron weighing hundreds of pounds came away with a bite out of it, glowing red. Sitting on its back, I cut down hard. The scales were harder than iron. It rammed the wall to crush me. I slid down under its belly and grabbed the skin of a front leg, and it ran with me dragging across the floor, my back scraping over the rock.

The belly was soft. No monster has a hard belly. Floorboards taught me that. I let go with one hand and pushed the sword upward, and let its own running do the work, from the chest all the way to the back legs. Hot stuff came pouring down and burned me so badly I screamed. It ran a few more steps before it dropped, with me underneath. It took me a long time to push my way out.

I sat on the forgemaster's anvil and drank a potion. I knew now what that potion was made of. I drank it anyway.

As I drank I looked at the recipe on the wall. At the very bottom he had written one line in charcoal, very small: Don't give it to children.

I'm not telling you this so you'll forgive me. I only want you to know that I was no different from those people. "They will not," he wrote, and he was right. I heard him perfectly clearly, and then I went and didn't listen, just like the rest of them.

## Floor Nineteen

Floor Nineteen is the deepest part of the mine, and you get down to it by cage.

The shaft had a huge winch frame, rusted almost beyond recognition. The stairs ended here. There was only a chain with an iron cage hanging from it. I gave it a good shove and it held. I made a note on my sleeve. This was the first time I couldn't count steps, so I counted links of chain.

Halfway down, the cage swayed.

I looked up. Something was climbing down the wall of the shaft above me, with a lot of legs and a body in segments, its legs spread wider than the cage. A hook-claw worm. The first one jumped straight onto the roof of the cage. Its claw came in between the bars, hooked my shoulder and started hauling me upward. I cut at it, sparks flew, and the cage began to spin. Another one landed, and the chain made a very ugly noise.

I cut through the first one's claw, and the piece stayed hanging from my shoulder, hooked into the flesh. The second put its head in from the side and bit my sword with its ring of teeth. I used fire. The whole worm went up and started crashing around, and it broke a link of the chain. The cage dropped.

I grabbed the bars and my whole body floated up, and my head hit the roof. Red light slowly appeared in the dark below and got brighter. I don't know how long the cage fell. Then a spare chain caught it with a jerk. My shoulder came out of its socket and all I could hear was iron shrieking. The cage hung in the air two storeys above the bottom, and the bottom was bright red lava. The burning worm fell past me and was gone at once.

I knocked my shoulder back in, let myself down from the floor of the cage on a rope and jumped to the wooden scaffolding beside it. Every step I took, a pole collapsed. I ran and jumped all the way, and my last jump landed me on a narrow ledge in the rock, where I lay on my stomach and panted for a long time.

That line of bare footprints was right there on the ledge. I don't know how he got down.

In the last foreman's shed on this floor there was a register, the roll called as the final shift came off work.

::lore.f19

I didn't understand it.

I walked out of the shed and followed the tunnel down to where the last shift had been working.

Then I understood.

It was one whole wall of rock, from the floor up to a ceiling I couldn't see, and it was covered in hands.

They were all human hands, a great many of them. Some held picks and some didn't. Above the wrist they were buried in stone. The fingers were still bent, and there was still dirt under the nails. Every one of them was reaching toward the way the miners had come.

Most of them were very old, the skin and the stone no longer separate. I walked along the wall very slowly, holding the lamp up high.

Near the floor there was a newer one.

I didn't want to look at that hand. I walked past, and then I walked back. It was a very thin hand. The little finger was missing a joint, and there was a copper ring on the ring finger.

It reached out farther than the others, fingers spread, toward the way I had come.

From Floor Eight to here is eleven floors. He walked eleven floors barefoot. I don't know whether he was still asleep when he got here. The fingers of that hand moved, and moved again, curling gently as if to take hold of something.

"Southerner," I said.

It did nothing else.

I took that picture out of my coat, the badly drawn house by the sea. I folded it and tucked it into the hand. The fingers slowly closed.

I went on down. I didn't speak again that day, and I didn't pick up anything else.

The number on my sleeve, by this point, was a little over four thousand six hundred.

## Floor Twenty

The bridge to the fortress is long.

Under it there was only mist and no bottom to be seen. The roadway was wide enough for eight horses abreast. Flagpoles stood along both sides, their flags down to a few strips. I walked down the middle, with no plan to hide, and my footsteps carried a long way.

Halfway across, something in the mist cried out, very shrill, like a child.

They flew up from under the bridge, a dozen or more of them, as high as my waist, red all over, with tattered wings and little forks in their hands, cackling. Imps are nothing, I thought, and knocked two flying with one cut. The third landed on my face, grabbed my hair and stabbed its fork at my eye. I jerked my head aside, and the fork went into my cheek and hit my teeth from the inside. I tore it off, threw it down and stamped it flat. Four more had hold of my cloak by then, and they all flew for the side of the bridge together.

They weren't strong, but there were a lot of them. The cloak choked me and I slid several paces toward the edge. My heels were on the rim, with the mist right beside me. I couldn't undo the clasp, so I swung the sword back over my shoulder and cut through cloak and imps together. They issued me that cloak when I came down the mountain, with the crest on it. It floated off into the mist in pieces.

I got my feet under me, wiped the blood off my face with the back of my hand and walked on. That's when I noticed they had all stopped at one spot and wouldn't come any closer, as if there were an invisible line. On the other side of that line was the gate.

He was standing in front of the gate.

He was shorter than I remembered. When I was twelve he was the tallest person in the world. Now he was only a head or so taller than me. I didn't know that armour. It was so black it was nearly purple, covered in spikes, with purple light showing through the cracks, and a pair of curved horns had grown out of the helmet. I suppose the Well dressed him in it. His cloak was so torn it looked chewed. The helmet had one thin purple slit and I couldn't see his face. A great sword almost as long as I am tall hung from his right hand with its point touching the ground, and the ground there was burned black in one small spot, very deep, as if the sword had been resting there for ten years.

It had been resting there for ten years.

I stopped twenty paces from him. That's the Order's rule: when you spar, you stand twenty paces apart.

"Master."

My voice sounded awful. It had been a long time since I'd spoken to anyone alive.

He raised the sword, and the blade caught fire.

His opening stance was exactly as I remembered it, right foot back, elbow low. Ten years, and his elbow was still that low. When I was little I copied him, and he rapped my elbow with a stick and said: Your arm's not as long as mine. Don't copy it.

He charged.

I have fought a great many battles in my life, and I can't recall most of them clearly. I remember every cut of this one.

The first move he taught me was when I was five, in the yard, the simplest downward cut there is. He said my feet were too far apart and tapped them with the stick. He stood behind me and nudged my toes inward. His hands were very big and smelled of iron and horses.

That was the cut he used on me now. The position of his toes hadn't changed at all.

His fire was hotter than mine and his ice was faster. He had taught me every move I had. He knew everything I was going to do, because he had watched me learn it for seven years. But there was something more in him now. I used the new moves I had only taken up after Floor Seventeen, and he paused for just a moment, and the next round he cut at me with the same stroke. Anything he saw once, he could do.

The first time, I didn't last until he changed to ice.

He thrust, and covered five paces in one step. I knocked the point aside and cut his arm on the backswing, and his shoulder was already slamming into my chest. Then three cuts. The first sliced open my leather armour. The second went into my collarbone. The third wasn't aimed at me at all. It hit the ground, the ground blew up in a burst of fire, and I went off the side of the bridge. I caught the stone rail and hung over the mist, with blood running down my arm and dripping into it. I never heard it land.

He walked over and looked down at me. Inside the helmet it was black and there was nothing to see.

When I was little and fell out of a tree, he looked down at me the same way. He wouldn't help you up. He just stood there and waited for you to get up on your own. That time I got up.

This time he stepped on my fingers.

Fifth stroke. I woke in the stone ring at the end of the bridge, with my fingers still hurting. I stood up and looked along the bridge. He had already walked back to the gate and was standing on that burned black spot, sword point on the ground, as if nothing had happened. The wound I had cut in his arm was gone as well.

He was waiting for me to walk back onto the bridge.

I walked back onto the bridge and stopped at twenty paces.

"Master," I said. My voice was a bit better this time.

It went on a long time. When the fire ran out he switched to ice, and the whole bridge frosted over. When the ice ran out he switched to thunder, and my teeth rattled. Then he stepped back three paces, drew the sword in front of him and stopped.

I first saw that stance when I was seven. It was raining that day, and he told me to come at him in the rain. I ran at him. He stood still and held his stick in front of him. I swung, he gave it a light flick and my sword slid away, and the stick smacked down on the back of my hand. I ran at him again, and got another smack. I ran at him over forty times. The back of my hand swelled up like a bun, and rain ran into my eyes. In the end I just stood there holding the sword and shaking, afraid to move.

He waited a long time, and the rain kept falling.

"Not moving is also a move," he said. "But you have to know why you're not moving."

That was all he said, and he took me back. The cook gave him another scolding.

It was that stance.

I knew it far too well. Every morning for five years ended up here. He would draw the sword in front of him, plant himself and wait for you to move first. The moment you struck he would knock it aside, and then the stick would come down on your hand. He only ever hit the hand, and he hit it until you let go. "Strike too soon," he told me, "and you ruin the hand."

I was panting. Blood dripped off my chin. I had one potion left. My left eye had stopped seeing, and I didn't know if that was blood or something else.

Could I break it? No. In five years I had never broken it once.

I looked at his elbow and thought, all right.

I rushed in and struck with everything I had, which was exactly the stroke he wanted. He blocked it, and his blade slid down along mine, just like when I was little, headed where it always headed, for the hand. I watched it come and didn't dodge. That thundering sword bit into the back of my right hand, and I heard the bones break.

When I was little, this was the point where I let go, every single time.

This time I didn't.

My hand was broken and I was still holding the sword. I can't tell you how. His blade was still stuck in the bones of my hand and he was wide open. The stance was used up. He was waiting for my sword to fall. Ten years on, his body still remembered this, and it was still waiting.

I pushed forward.

The sword went in under his breastplate.

He didn't fall. His own sword slid out of his hand and dropped onto the bridge, and the fire, the ice and the thunder all went out. Slowly he raised his hand and put it on my head.

There was no face inside the helmet, only a wisp of white smoke rising through the slits.

The hand rested on my head for a moment, and then it was empty. The armour collapsed with a great clatter.

The smoke was still there, hanging in front of me. The voice was a little nasal.

The last time I sparred with him was a month before he left. I was twelve. That morning I blocked twelve of his moves in a row, and it took him until the thirteenth to hit my hand. I jumped for joy and asked him if I was close to winning. He put the stick away and looked at me for a long time.

"Winning what?" he asked.
