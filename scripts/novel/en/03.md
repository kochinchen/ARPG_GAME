# Book Four: The Master

Floors 16 to 20

## Floor Sixteen

After the smoke cleared, it took me a day to stand up. My wounds healed fast, faster than they should have. The hard patch on my palm wouldn't pick off, so I left it alone. A dead miner by the path had a leather glove that was still good. I took it and put it on.

The mine kept going down, and now I started to see the places where business got done: foremen's sheds, tables for weighing stones, and a small stone house with the royal arms over the door. Inside were scales, a magnifying glass and a row of little bottles. Papers lay all over the floor, and one of them was written in a particularly fine hand.

::lore.f16

I like this assayer. He was another honest man.

His job was to assay stones. Hand him a warm stone and the rules said he had to write down where it came from. He had probably burned it, soaked it and cracked it open to look. Then he sat down here, picked up his pen and wrote "The assayer declineth to say," as if he were talking about someone else, and he quit. He knew the answer, so he left.

How many people in a country know the answer and won't say? And how many just quit and walk away?

That's what I think now, though. At the time I only thought he had been too vague, and that he should have written the answer down properly. I put the paper away and kept walking.

The miners' quarters were cut into the rock, one cell after another, a lot like the tombs on Floor Five. Each cell had a plank bed and a spare pair of shoes. There were marks scratched on the walls, four strokes and a bar, counting the days until the shift was over. Some men had carved the names of their families. One had carved a woman, badly, with very large breasts. Under one bed was a small wooden horse, only half done, its legs not yet separated.

I picked it up and looked at it. The man was good with a knife. He just ran out of time.

Who was he going to take it up to? Even if that child lived to be old, the child had been dead for centuries. I put the horse back under the bed and straightened the shoes. I did this kind of thing more and more on the way down, putting dead people's belongings back where they had been. It probably did no good at all.

At its end the tunnel opened into a crack halfway up a cliff, with nothing outside. A cold wind blew up and thinned the smell of burning on me.

I went to the edge and looked. I had no idea there was so much room under the ground. Far away on the other side, a mountain stood in the mist, and on the mountain stood a fortress. Its walls rose in tier after tier, one tower after another, and all its flags were in rags. I knew the crest. I was wearing it.

It was our fortress. The Order had built it underground. The books up in the Order gave it one line.

Then something flashed at the fortress gate. It was too far off to be more than a dot. First orange-red, then blue-white, then gold like lightning.

Then orange-red again.

Only one person in the world practises like that, always fire, then ice, then thunder, and the order never changes. I asked him why when I was little. He said it was easier to remember.

::panel.f16-1

I have drawn this picture many times. In the little circle at the top left I am seven or eight. He is sitting on a rock, correcting the way I hold my wooden sword.

He rarely spoke, as I told you. He taught by doing a thing once and then watching you do it, and when you got it wrong he rapped your hand. When I was eight I asked him why the other instructors gave praise and he didn't. He thought about it for so long that I decided he hadn't heard. Then he took an apple out of his pocket, put it in my hand and walked off.

I kept that apple until it was nearly rotten before I could bring myself to eat it.

The day he taught me to put fire on a blade, I set my own sleeve alight. He slapped the flames out with his hand, which blistered all across the back, and he said nothing. The next day he taught it again: fire, then ice, then thunder. It was many years before it occurred to me that he had probably settled on that order for my sake, because my memory was terrible when I was small.

I stood on that cliff until my hands and feet were cold. For ten years I had practised in my head what I would say when I saw him.

But it had never crossed my mind that when I found him, he would be standing in front of a gate. The captain stood in front of a gate. The priest guarded an altar. That mountain guarded the mine. I had seen all of them.

I knew what would be standing in front of that gate.

I started down, and I walked fast. The numbers I wrote on my sleeve for that stretch, I can't read now myself.

## Floor Seventeen

A strange thing happened on the way.

A skeleton charged me. I had killed thousands of those by then, so without thinking I used my usual move: left foot forward, blade sweeping up from lower right to upper left, then turn with it into a second cut.

It stepped back half a pace and let that go by. Then it put its left foot forward and swept up from lower right to upper left.

It caught me in the shoulder. The wound wasn't bad, but I froze. I stayed frozen too long, and it cut again, turning with the stroke.

That was my second cut.

I smashed it, crouched down and looked. It was a pile of ordinary bones. After that I started paying attention. There was a skeleton mage that held back its usual fireball and hit my feet first with a little ice spike, a habit I'd had since Floor Seven. The archers took to shooting first at the side I usually dodged to.

These things have no brains. They are dead people. All they can do is walk and hack.

But something was teaching them.

I thought of the page torn from the scripture on Floor Thirteen: It only learneth. I thought of Floor Twelve, the day I fought my hardest.

Later I opened a chest.

::note.p04

I laughed, and it wasn't a pretty laugh. The writing was crooked, with long trailing tails. Whoever wrote it was probably hurt, and used what strength was left on this one line. No name, nothing about who it was meant for. It just seemed too ridiculous to leave unsaid.

I thought it was ridiculous too.

From that day on I changed my moves. Once I had used a stroke twice, I stopped opening with it. It's exhausting. Unlearning what you have drilled since you were seven is harder than learning something new. And I didn't know if it helped, because I didn't know how much it had already seen.

A report was hanging in the foreman's shed on this floor.

::lore.f17

I laid my hand on the rock wall. The seams lay under the stone, line after line, like the veins on the back of a hand. I waited a while.

Before long a thin red line came slowly toward me from deep in the rock. It came to rest right under my palm and stopped.

My palm was warm. So was the stone.

A core the size of a fist pushed out of the wall, freshly grown. I took my hand away and walked off without it.

After a dozen steps or so I went back and picked it up. You wanted the truth, didn't you? That's the truth.

## Floor Eighteen

The forgemaster's workshop was on this floor, built beside a river of lava. I'd guess it was the best spot in the whole mine.

A good old craftsman hangs each tool exactly where his hand will fall on it, and this one had. The handles of the tongs were worn bright. The small hammers were lined up neatly by size. The anvil was cracked. On the wall were charcoal drawings showing how to set a stone into a sword, with the very kind of socket my own sword had.

So he was the one who invented it.

On the ground by the anvil lay a stone slab, with words cut into it by chisel.

::lore.f18

"They will not."

I pictured him chiselling those words. Men who work iron don't talk much. He knew they had dug too deep, had worn the seal thin, and that this ring was close to giving way. He tried to talk them out of it. Nobody listened, and the crown was very pleased with the results. So he walked into the fire himself and patched the ring with his own body. For hundreds of years he burned and held, and the fire melted him as he held, until he became the mountain I knocked down.

I sat there for a long time.

Then I laid my sword on the anvil and pried the stones out of the sockets, one by one.

There were six in it by then, none of them small. I lined them up on the anvil, still warm. Then I reached for the sword.

I could barely lift it.

My hand dropped and the point hit the floor. The Order issued me that sword and I had trained with it for more than ten years, and I could not remember it ever being this heavy. One swing and my wrist ached. The second swing was so slow it wouldn't have hit a skeleton. All at once I was so hungry that my sight went black, and my whole body began to hurt. Every wound that had healed too fast hurt at once.

I sat there with both hands shaking and looked at the stones on the anvil.

The forgemaster had already told me. He told me as he died, and he cut his last words here as well. Stop digging, he said.

But I thought about what lay ahead. I thought about that spark of light in front of the fortress gate: fire, then ice, then thunder.

I put the stones back, one by one.

I'm not telling you this so you'll forgive me. I only want you to know that I was no different from those people. "They will not," he wrote, and he was right. I heard him perfectly clearly, and then I went and didn't listen, just like the rest of them.

## Floor Nineteen

In the last foreman's shed on this floor there was a register, the roll called as the final shift came off work.

::lore.f19

I didn't understand it.

I walked out of the shed and followed the tunnel down to where the last shift had been working.

Then I understood.

It was one whole wall of rock, from the floor up to a ceiling I couldn't see, and it was covered in hands.

They were all human hands, a great many of them. Some held picks and some didn't. Above the wrist they were buried in stone. The fingers were still bent, and there was still dirt under the nails. Every one of them was reaching toward the way the miners had come.

Most of them were very old, the skin and the stone no longer separate. I walked along the wall very slowly, holding the lamp up high.

Near the floor there was a newer one.

I didn't want to look. I walked past, and then I walked back. It was a very thin hand. The little finger was missing a joint, and there was a copper ring on the ring finger.

It reached out farther than the others, fingers spread, toward the way I had come.

From Floor Eight to here is eleven floors. He walked eleven floors barefoot. I don't know whether he was still asleep when he got here. The fingers of that hand moved, and moved again, curling gently as if to take hold of something.

"Southerner," I said.

It did nothing else.

I took that picture out of my coat, the badly drawn house by the sea. I folded it and tucked it into the hand. The fingers slowly closed.

I went on down. I didn't speak again that day, and I didn't pick up anything else.

The number on my sleeve, by this point, was a little over four thousand six hundred.

## Floor Twenty

The bridge to the fortress is long.

Under it there was only mist and no bottom to be seen. The roadway was wide enough for eight horses abreast. Flagpoles stood along both sides, their flags down to a few strips. I walked down the middle, with no plan to hide, and my footsteps carried a long way.

He was standing in front of the gate.

He was shorter than I remembered. When I was twelve he was the tallest person in the world. Now his shoulders were only a little higher than mine. His cloak was so torn it looked chewed. He wore a helmet and I couldn't see his face. The sword hung from his right hand with its point touching the ground, and the ground there was burned black in one small spot, very deep, as if the sword had been resting there for ten years.

It had been resting there for ten years.

I stopped twenty paces from him. That's the Order's rule: when you spar, you stand twenty paces apart.

"Master."

My voice sounded awful. It had been a long time since I'd spoken to anyone alive.

He raised the sword, and the blade caught fire.

His opening stance was exactly as I remembered it, right foot back, elbow low. Ten years, and his elbow was still that low. When I was little I copied him, and he rapped my elbow with a stick and said: Your arm's not as long as mine. Don't copy it.

He charged.

I have fought a great many battles in my life, and I can't recall most of them clearly. I remember every cut of this one.

His fire was hotter than mine and his ice was faster. He had taught me every move I had. He knew everything I was going to do, because he had watched me learn it for five years. But there was something more in him now. I used the new moves I had only taken up after Floor Seventeen, and he paused for just a moment, and the next round he cut at me with the same stroke. Anything he saw once, he could do.

It went on a long time. When the fire ran out he switched to ice, and the whole bridge frosted over. When the ice ran out he switched to thunder, and my teeth rattled. Then he stepped back three paces, drew the sword in front of him and stopped.

It was that stance.

I knew it far too well. Every morning for five years ended up here. He would draw the sword in front of him, plant himself and wait for you to move first. The moment you struck he would knock it aside, and then the stick would come down on your hand. He only ever hit the hand, and he hit it until you let go. "Strike too soon," he told me, "and you ruin the hand."

I was panting. Blood dripped off my chin.

Could I break it? No. In five years I had never broken it once.

I looked at his elbow and thought, all right.

I rushed in and struck with everything I had, which was exactly the stroke he wanted. He blocked it, and his blade slid down along mine, just like when I was little, headed where it always headed, for the hand. I watched it come and didn't dodge. That thundering sword bit into the back of my right hand, and I heard the bones break.

When I was little, this was the point where I let go, every single time.

This time I didn't.

My hand was broken and I was still holding the sword. I can't tell you how. His blade was still stuck in the bones of my hand and he was wide open. The stance was used up. He was waiting for my sword to fall. Ten years on, his body still remembered this, and it was still waiting.

I pushed forward.

The sword went in under his breastplate.

He didn't fall. His own sword slid out of his hand and dropped onto the bridge, and the fire, the ice and the thunder all went out. Slowly he raised his hand and put it on my head.

There was no face inside the helmet, only a wisp of white smoke rising through the slits.

The hand rested on my head for a moment, and then it was empty. The armour collapsed with a great clatter.

The smoke was still there, hanging in front of me. The voice was a little nasal.

I laughed once, and it sounded a lot like crying. My right hand no longer looked like a hand. With my left I pried the fingers off the hilt, one at a time.

What I said in that courtyard ten years ago, I wanted to take it back. I had practised this many times.

"Master, back then I—"

::soul.f20#1

He wasn't listening. Or maybe he was. I don't know whether smoke can hear.

"Master, I didn't really—"

::soul.f20#2

The smoke was gone.

The bridge was very quiet, and the mist came slowly welling up from below. I stood there and finished what I had to say. I said it three times. The third time I said it very loud, and the echo carried a long way.

Nobody heard. Except maybe that crack on Floor Twelve.

His sword lay on the ground, and now it was only an old sword. I picked it up, walked to the side of the gate and drove it into the gap between two flagstones, good and straight. Then I walked into our fortress.

::lore.f20

This sheet was nailed to the wall inside the gatehouse, and every new arrival had to read it. There's one just like it in the great hall up on the mountain. I started memorising it when I was seven.

I recited it for fifteen years and never once wondered why everyone had to learn all five halls.

It's because only one person goes down.
