# Book Five: She Who Waits

Floors 21 to 25

## Floor Twenty-One

Yesterday, when you finished reading Floor Twenty, you said nothing and drove the sheep home. Today you brought me a boiled egg. I ate it. Thank you.

There was nobody alive in the fortress. There were only some things in armour with our crest, still walking their old patrol routes.

My right hand healed quickly, and three days later it could hold a sword. The broken fingers straightened themselves one at a time, and it didn't hurt at all. The back of the hand grew a dark patch of hard skin that joined up with the one on my palm. I didn't want to look at it, so I pulled the glove up higher.

I first ran into a patrol at a corner of the inner keep. Four things in full armour, marching in step, turning at the same angle. The one in front saw me and raised a hand, and the other three stopped at the same moment. A very dry voice came out of the helmet and asked the question I had answered every night since I was seven.

"Who goes there? Password."

My hand was already on my sword, but when I opened my mouth the password came out by itself: "No one stands down until the Well is quiet." They use the same one up on the mountain. It hasn't changed in a thousand years.

The one in front lowered his hand, stepped aside and saluted me. It was the Ward-hall salute, the elbow set just right. The four of them marched past me and carried on with their patrol, very neat and tidy.

The sweat on me went cold. They didn't know there was no one left here. They didn't know the person in front of them had just killed their guardian. All they knew was that the password was correct.

After that, every time I met them I gave the password and they let me through. I didn't kill a single one. If anyone asks what I have feared most in my life, I won't say the eyes at the bottom of the Well, and I won't say that mountain. I'll say those four saluting me.

The things in the fortress that really did attack me were the ones that had crawled in later: chain wardens dragging their chains, golems stacked up from the stones of the walls, and the little things that hatched out of those egg sacs. They didn't know any password. Fighting them was almost a relief.

That's a lie. It was no relief.

A chain warden stood a little taller than me, a set of bones in an iron mask, wrapped all over in chain, and the chain dragged on the ground when it walked. You could hear it a long way off. It didn't need to walk up to you. It flicked its hand and the chain flew out and wrapped around your ankle, one pull and you were on your face being dragged. The first time, I got dragged more than twenty paces with my whole face scraping along the flagstones, and I couldn't get my blade free until I was at its feet. One cut at its wrist took the hand off, and the chain was still around my foot. Later I learned to listen. When the chain rattled I jumped for the wall and let it wrap around a pillar, then charged while the warden couldn't pull it back.

The golems were more trouble. They were the fortress's own stones, woken by who knows what, and most of the time they were just a stretch of wall. I leaned against a stretch of wall to drink some water, and the wall stood up. My sword got nothing but sparks off it. I ran half the length of a corridor before I remembered that monster on Floor Eighteen: nothing hates sudden heat and cold like stone does. I used fire first until it glowed red, then hit it with ice. A crack opened in its chest, and I drove my sword in and levered. It worked on the third try. By then it had crushed my left hand.

I also went to see the place the chain wardens guarded.

It was a row of cells under the inner keep, forty or fifty of them. I had chased one of the wardens down there. It threw its chain around my waist and yanked me backward, and I smashed through a rotten cell door and landed inside. It stood in the doorway, slowly taking in chain with one hand and pulling me toward it. I grabbed the cell's iron bars and they tore out of the wall. It dragged me to its feet, bar and all. I raised that bar and rammed it upward into its chin. It swayed, and it still didn't let go. I ran fire up the bar, its head lit up from the inside, and only then did the chain go slack.

I lay on the floor getting my breath and looking at the wall of the cell. There was writing on it.

It wasn't the kind guardians leave. This was cut in a mess. Some were just names, some were marks, and someone had carved a horse. I got up and went next door, and next door again. Every cell had them. The bones in the cells were all in our old armour, with shackles at the wrists and ankles.

I had assumed they kept enemies here. But the fortress was under the ground. What enemies?

On the wall of the seventh cell was a neater line: I only wanted to go home and see my mother. Beside it someone else had cut: Over the wall three times. The ninth cell: I said I wouldn't go.

So in those days the ones who ran were caught and brought back. When our Grand Master up on the mountain said "That saves two rations," it was because he had no one left to send after them. Five hundred years ago, there was someone to send.

I ran my hand over the words on the wall of the ninth cell. I said I wouldn't go. They were cut very deep. I stood there a long time. Our instructors never once mentioned these cells. The Order's books have only one line about them, saying that the fortress has "reflection rooms."

The fortress had the five halls too, like the one on the mountain, only bigger. My master and I both came from the Ward-hall.

I walked through all five of them. It took three days.

The range in the Bow-hall was a hundred paces long, and the targets still had arrows in them, with feathers that crumbled at a touch. When Old Bow took us shooting up on the mountain, the range was forty paces. Standing there I remembered the last thing she said to me before I left. She was over seventy by then. Her hands had started to shake, and they only stopped when she raised a bow. After the night at the mill she called me over and showed me her bow, very old, worn shiny at the grip.

I thought she was going to give it to me. She glanced at me and put it away.

"This one's mine," she said. "If you want one, there'll be better down below."

After a while she said, "When you shoot, your right shoulder's too tight. This isn't the first time you've heard that, is it?"

I said it wasn't.

"There you are, then. You Ward-hall lot." She shook her head and told me to shoot another hundred.

The walls of the Art-hall were black as well, ten times the size of the ones on the mountain. Right in the middle was a burned-through hole, and I couldn't see anything at the bottom of it. The Blade-hall had row after row of wooden men, all hacked to pieces. The Way-hall was the most interesting. The whole floor of the room was traps, one slab after another, which tipped when you stepped on them. Under one of the slabs lay a skeleton. It had probably fallen at the seventy-second slab. It still had a charcoal stick in its hand, and on the wall it had written its count of the slabs: "seventy-two."

I walked the same route the way Floorboards taught me, tapping every slab with my sword hilt first. I had gone a hundred and forty slabs before I got out. When I was out, I turned to look at the skeleton and said, "You counted pretty well."

Nobody heard. I had started talking to dead people. That's not a good sign, I know. I walked into the Ward-hall yard. The wooden posts stood there, and the ground had hollows worn by thousands of pairs of feet, exactly like up on the mountain. Without meaning to, I went and stood in front of the third post, which was my place when I was small. There were a few round shields on the rack beside it. I picked the one in the best shape and tied it to my left arm.

I stood at the third post and looked at the second one next to it.

That was Mavis's place. Up on the mountain, of course, not here. But the two yards looked exactly alike, right down to the hollows in the ground.

When I was fifteen, the night before the twins ran away, they came to see me. They were a year older than me, a boy and a girl, and they didn't talk to people much. That night they stood by my bed and said they had saved up a little money and were going over the wall and heading south. They asked if I wanted to come.

I said no. I said we were Ward-hall people, and we had to wait for our assignments.

The girl looked at me for a moment and said, "You know those nails are empty, don't you?"

Of course I knew. I rolled over and told them to get going or I'd shout for someone. They went. I didn't shout.

Mavis was awake as well. She said quietly in the dark, "I was going to go with them."

I asked why she hadn't.

"You still owe me a question," she said. We had been betting on rock-paper-scissors in those days, and the loser had to answer a question, and I had lost. "The day your master left, what did you say to him?"

I didn't answer. She waited a long time, then sighed, rolled over and went to sleep. She never asked again, and she never talked about leaving again.

I have often thought, since then, that she could have gone.

The rule of the Ward-hall hung over its door. I memorised it as a child.

::lore.f21

"The Ward-hall was founded in the name of her who went first." I recited that for fifteen years and no one ever told me who she was. I did ask. The instructor said it was a long time ago, and I didn't ask again.

The second half was different.

The copy in the great hall on the mountain ends at "It hath one lesson: endure." I remember very clearly, because I once traced it letter by letter. This copy was older, and it had one more sentence.

"We do not teach what it costeth."

Somebody left that out when they made the copy. I suppose they felt it wasn't quite right for a place where children would see it every day.

A cupboard in the Ward-hall held a small locked box of old notes that guardians had used. I smashed the lock. Worms had eaten most of what was inside, and only one sheet could still be read. It had four different hands on it, crammed together, filling every inch of blank space. The first three parts had rotted past reading. Only the smallest line, at the very bottom, was clear.

::note.p07

I turned the page over. The back was full too.

The papers in my coat had grown into a thick stack. I put this one with them, and I nearly added a line in the margin: I am the fifth.

But he had said it. There is no more room.

## Floor Twenty-Two

The Grand Master's chamber was at the very top of the fortress, and the door had been sealed from outside with wax. The wax was newer than I expected, and there was that seal on it again.

To reach it you climbed a very long spiral stair with windows all the way up. There was no sky outside, only mist, and now and then something bigger than a person drifted by in it. Halfway up, the thing outside pressed itself against the window.

It was a wrinkled lump of flesh with one eye in the middle, bigger than the window. It just stayed there against the glass and looked at me. I kept climbing. At the next window, there it was. By the fifth window it was gone, and from higher up the stairs came the sound of something sliding.

The stairs were too narrow to swing a sword. It squeezed down from above and filled the whole stairwell, flesh pressed against the walls, eye turned toward me. A beam of light shot out of the eye and hit my chest. The leather scorched on the spot and I fell back and rolled half a turn down the stairs. I got up, and it fired again. I took it on the shield and the shield began to smoke. This wasn't going to work.

I put the sword away, drew my dagger and ran up straight into the light. It hit my shoulder and I ignored it. When I reached the thing I rammed my whole hand, dagger and all, into that eye. Inside it was hot and sticky. I kept stirring, until my arm was in up to the shoulder. Its whole body jerked and pinned me between the flesh and the wall, and I could hardly breathe. It took a long time to go limp.

I crawled out from under the lump, covered in slime, and threw up out of the window for quite a while. Then I wiped my hand clean and went on up.

There wasn't much in the Grand Master's chamber: one big table, two chairs, an iron cabinet. I smashed the cabinet open. It was full of letters between one Grand Master and another, mostly arguing about pay. I read them one after another, since I had nothing better to do.

At the very back was a letter wrapped in black cloth. The wax on it was very old. It had been cracked, and someone had carefully pressed it back together.

::lore.f22

When I finished I put the letter down on the table.

The window of this room looks out over the training ground. In those days, standing here and looking down, you could see the children lined up in rows, holding up their wooden swords. Some Grand Master stood at this window with this letter in hand, looking at those children, and thought: Who then would go?

I picked up a chair and started smashing the table.

It was a very thick table, but I was far too strong, and it split in three blows. After the table I smashed the cabinet. When the cabinet was in pieces I kicked those letters about pay all over the room. Then I stood in the middle of it, breathing hard. Nobody came to shout at me. Nobody came to look. The person who wrote that letter had been dead for centuries.

All those empty nails in the great hall on the mountain, and every one of those nameplates. "Because they are still watching over us."

They had always known.

The old priest on Floor Ten had already told me: "no guardian was ever a hero. We were offerings, every one." That calm handwriting on Floor Eleven had told me too: "The Order lied." Every paper I had picked up on the way said the same thing. And still I kept going down, thinking it was about other people, and that I would be different.

Every guardian thought that.

Did my master read this letter? I'd guess he did. I think he was the one who pressed that wax back together. The Grand Master's iron chest on the mountain must hold one too. The spring I was eleven he spent an afternoon behind that door, and from that day he started taking me down the mountain. Only now did I put those two things together.

"Who then would go?"

You're smarter than I am. You know the answer. The answer is no one.

So think about this. Suppose they had nailed this letter to the door of the great hall back then, where every child could read it, and no one was ever willing to go again. What would have happened?

I don't know either. Maybe the Well would have swallowed everything sooner. Maybe someone would have come up with another way. For a thousand years nobody has known, because nobody has ever tried it.

I folded the letter and put it inside my coat, against my body.

I decided to write this book that day, though I didn't know it yet.

Only then did I understand why, the year the recruiting officer asked "What about this one?", the Grand Master said "This one stays."

I thought he valued me. I was first in the Order, and he was keeping his best to guard the great hall. I was proud of that for six years. Standing in this room I finally worked it out. The Grand Master on the mountain had read letters like this one too. When he called those names, he was already choosing, who went east to fight and who stayed behind to wait for the Well. He looked at me standing straight as a post, elbow against my ribs, first in all five halls, and what he thought was: this is the one. My stance and my first place in the five halls had been beaten into me by my master, one stroke at a time. He wanted me to live, and what he made was the person best fitted to go down the Well.

The day Big Lad left, he told me that at least some people had come back from war.

I laughed at him.

Before I went downstairs I looked once more out of the window at the training ground.

When I was nineteen a new child was sent up the mountain, seven years old. The talk about the cellars below had only just started, and his parents were already dead. We called him Pebble. He was the last new child there ever was. Hardly anyone in the Order had the strength left to teach, so they told Mavis and me to bring him up. I taught him to stand: elbow against the ribs, left foot forward. He didn't last the length of one incense stick before he started crying. I tapped his leg with a stick, not hard, just as a reminder. He looked up at me with snot running into his mouth.

Mavis watched from the side and said nothing. That night she split a stolen piece of bread in two and gave half to Pebble. Then she asked me, "How long are you going to teach him?"

I said until he had learned.

"And when he's learned, what then?"

I didn't know. I really had never thought about it. All I knew was that this was what we did. My instructors had done it to me, and I was doing it to him.

Standing at the Grand Master's window, looking at that empty training ground, I thought about that stick. When I hit him with it, I thought I was teaching him to stay alive.

Later Pebble got a high fever, and it only broke after the cook fed him half a bottle of potion. He should be twelve by now, I thought. If the great hall was still there.

I went down the spiral stair to the next stone ring. The old peddler was sitting there with his cloth spread out. I stood in front of him, with splinters from the table still on me.

"Twelve a bottle," he said.

I drew my sword and cut straight through his neck.

I don't know why. I didn't really want to kill him, or else I really did. I just needed to cut something. That face, that tattered robe, those words, twenty-two floors of them, on every floor, and he had never answered a single question of mine. The sword went through and I felt nothing, as if I had cut a puff of smoke. The robe stirred a little. The blade came out the other side.

He was still sitting there. There wasn't so much as a line on his neck.

I cut his chest. I cut his head. I kicked his cloth over and the bottles rolled all over the floor. I kept cutting until I was gasping for breath, and his robe didn't even have a hole in it.

He looked at me with very cloudy eyes, and I couldn't tell where they were looking. Then he slowly reached out and picked up the rolling bottles one at a time, put them back on the cloth and lined them up neatly.

"Twelve a bottle," he said.

I stood there and let the sword hang down. Then I took out twelve gold coins, put them on the cloth and took a bottle.

I've done plenty of shameful things in my life. That one is near the top of the list.

## Floor Twenty-Three

Floor Twenty-Three was living quarters, room after room, with nameplates on the doors. A lot of the plates were blank. I wasn't looking for anything.

That's a lie. I was looking.

The patrols in the quarters were different.

In the second corridor a man stood ahead of me in our armour, in much better shape than the ones outside. His cloak was still whole. He held a longsword with its point down, and he didn't move at all. I stopped and gave the password first: "No one stands down until the Well is quiet."

He raised his head. There were two patches of red light inside the helmet. He didn't step aside.

That was a fallen templar. I saw several more on this floor later. They weren't the kind who were dead and still on patrol. These had been alive the whole time, stayed too long, and been slowly changed by the place. He still knew the password. He just didn't care any more.

In one step he was on me.

It was the first time I had fought a stranger from my own Order. His opening stance was my opening stance, and he already knew my guard. I stopped all three cuts of the first round, and my arm went numb. He was stronger than me. The fourth cut pressed my sword down and his helmet came straight into my nose. The bridge broke and blood ran into my mouth. I backed away and he followed, Ward-hall footwork, left foot forward, not one step wasted. The crest on his chest was covered in scratches, just like mine.

I backed down the corridor with him driving me all the way. I used fire. He raised his hand and the fire hit his iron glove, and he caught my wrist and slammed it against the wall. My sword fell. He was in no hurry to kill me. He slowly raised the longsword in his other hand and aimed it at my chest, in very correct form, just the way we practised in the yard.

I laughed, with my mouth full of blood. I don't know why. For fifteen years this was what the instructors had taught me, this very move. It was so correct that I knew where his next step would be.

I hooked my foot behind his left foot. You got hit for doing that. Mavis invented it, for cheating in matches. His weight tipped, and the cut slid past my ribs and stuck in the wall. I pulled the little knife out of my boot, the one from Floor Three, and stabbed it through the eye slit of his helmet.

When he went down, his hand was still on that sword. I pried his fingers open, pulled the sword out of the wall and laid it on his chest. Then I picked up my own. There was something cut into the inside of his breastplate, too faint to read.

The names on the door plates were too faint to read as well. One after another, all people I didn't know.

I walked for a whole day, and in the last room at the end of the corridor, my master's name was cut into the plate on the door. Ten years ago he passed through here, and he must have spent a night in this room.

I won't tell you his name. It's his.

The room was very small and the bed was neatly made. There was a nail in the wall with a wooden sword on it.

It wasn't mine. Mine is still up on the mountain. This one was shorter, made for a child smaller than I had been. He never said he had taught anyone else. Or it could have been his own when he was small. I took it down and tried the grip. It was too short for me, and the handle was worn shiny. The shine wasn't in the middle. It was forward, with the knuckles against the guard, the grip of someone who has clamped on and won't let go. I had thought I was the only one on the mountain who held a sword that way.

When I was little I was never allowed in his room. I did sneak into the one on the mountain once, when I was eleven and he was out on patrol, because I wanted to know what he did at night. That room was just like this one, with only a bed in it. I expected there would be something: a portrait, letters, things from when he was small. There was nothing. Under the pillow was a small cloth bag with some apple seeds in it, dried and wrinkled.

I didn't know what that meant. I put it back and never went there again.

That autumn he took me around behind the mountain. There was a very old apple tree there, with small sour fruit. He knocked one down with a stick and threw it to me, and took one for himself. We sat on a rock and ate. He didn't talk and neither did I. I was already very good by then. I could get through three moves against him without him touching my hand. That day he suddenly asked me, "If you didn't have to train, what would you want to do?"

That stopped me. I thought for a bit and said I wanted to be Grand Master.

He nodded and went on eating his apple. When he finished he spat the seeds into his palm and put them in the little cloth bag.

That was the only time he ever asked me that. I took it for a test.

There was a letter on the table, with no name on the envelope. It had been sealed and opened, opened and sealed, so many times that the edges had frayed.

I knew I should wait a bit before reading it. I couldn't wait.

::lore.f23

I read it standing. Then I sat down and read it again.

His handwriting was always ugly. He pressed too hard, and every stroke looked like a sword cut. This letter was uglier than usual. In a few places he had blacked out words so heavily that even under the lamp I couldn't tell what they had been. The blackest place was at the very top, written and crossed out and written again until the paper had nearly gone through. I guess that was where you put the name. To the end he didn't know what to call me.

"If this reach thee, I have failed."

He wrote it, sealed it, opened it, read it, sealed it again.

He never sent it.

There was nowhere to send it from, either. He wrote it after he came down, when the stairs back up were long gone, and of course he knew that. At a little table on Floor Twenty-Three, he wrote to a twelve-year-old who had told him "Then don't come back," telling her to be a smith, a farmer, anything. Then he sealed the letter, left it on the table, went to the gate and stood there.

**The Order will call it cowardice. Let them.**

I thought of that smithy, that half-mended fence, that sugar melting in my hand. He took me there and sat a whole afternoon, and I kept asking him when we were going back to practise.

I put my face in my hands.

He had known all along. He had read that secret letter. He knew that everyone failed, and he knew that sooner or later they would send me. He hit my hand with that stick until it swelled, until I let go. Let go. Only then did it occur to me that this was what he had been teaching me for those seven years. To let go.

I did not become a smith. I did not become a farmer. I walked down twenty-three floors on my own, to his table, and only then did I read this letter.

I have thought about this for decades. I'll tell you one thing now, and don't tell anyone else: that letter did reach me in the end.

A little late, that's all.

That night I slept in his bed, and I didn't tie my hand to my sword. The singing from below went on all night, and it called me in his voice. I lay still with that short wooden sword in my hand, and I didn't take one step.

The next morning I hung the wooden sword back on its nail and put the letter inside my coat.

## Floor Twenty-Four

The way down was inside the gatehouse, so I had to walk back to the gate.

I climbed up into the gatehouse. The place where my master stood for ten years was right below. Looking out from here you saw what he had been looking at all that time: the bridge, the mist, and nothing in the mist. The innermost wall of the gatehouse was covered in carving.

I had taken it for a pattern.

Up close it was words, big and small, new and old, layer on layer, centuries of them. I raised the lamp and found the newest lines. The writing was ugly. Every stroke looked like a sword cut.

::lore.f24

My hand was shaking, so I had to put the lamp down.

Higher up was an older line: "None standeth here; I take it up. I can bear it." Above that: "The gate is empty. I come." Above that, so old it was almost worn smooth, you could still make out two words: "Only I." Every one of them thought they were the only one. Every one of them came to this wall and found that the people before them had written the same thing. And then they cut their own words into it anyway. My master finished with "I find, upon this very wall," and went downstairs to stand his post.

I honestly don't know what to do with that man.

Standing at that wall, I thought about my last night before I went down the Well.

The Grand Master called me in that evening. That was the time he recited the old words and handed me that paper. When he had done it he opened the register, pointed at my name and said, "Tomorrow." I said yes. When I walked out of the room my hands were very steady, and I felt quite proud. Seventeen years, and this was what I had been waiting for.

I went back to the long dormitory. The beds were nearly all empty and Pebble was already asleep. Only Mavis was up, sitting on the next bed mending a sock, and making a terrible job of it.

"I heard," she said.

I said yes.

She stuck the needle in the sock and looked at me for a long time. Then she said, "Let's go."

I thought I had heard wrong.

"Over the wall," she said. "Right now. The twins got away, so can we. You know how to fight, I know how to steal. We go south. They say there's a sea."

I said you're crazy. The dead have walked as far as the mill. Somebody has to go down.

"Why you?"

Because I'm the strongest. Because I'm first in all five halls. Because of my master. I said a lot all at once, very loudly, and when I stopped the room was very quiet.

Mavis didn't get angry. She just looked at me. I had seen that look only once before, when she had finished crying over Stammer and was sitting alone behind the mountain.

"You know those nails are empty, don't you?" she said.

I had heard that before, from the twins. I said I knew. I said they were all still watching over us. I said I would bring my master back and mend the seal, and then I'd come back and make her pay me the twelve gold coins she owed me.

She laughed, a very ugly laugh. She lowered her head and went back to mending the sock.

"Fine," she said. "Then wear your socks."

Neither of us slept that night. She didn't bring up leaving again. Just before dawn we climbed the watchtower and looked toward that little hill in the valley, without a word, and she leaned her head on my shoulder. I thought at the time that she was tired.

The words on that wall were the same, every one, as what I said that night. I can hold this. I alone can hold this.

The next part is what I least want to tell you.

I looked out the window. The gate was empty. It was empty because I had killed him. The smell in the mist was stronger than before. With nobody standing here, what would happen to this ring? Would the thing below come pouring up through here? There were still villages up there. Every ring on the way down had someone holding it, the captain, the priest, the forgemaster, my master, and I had knocked them down one by one. I had got this far by tearing down one wall after another. Who was going to patch them?

My hand had gone very steady. I wasn't tired at all. I had the strength of eight or nine stones in me. I thought, I can do this. There's a place for me here. I can hold this. I alone can hold this.

When I came to, I was holding the little knife from the pack on Floor Three, with its point already against the wall. I had no memory of picking it up.

There was still a blank patch under his line, exactly the right size.

It was very quiet in my ears. No singing and no voice of his. The Well didn't have to say anything.

Ten years ago he must have stood here too, with a knife too, having just sealed that letter. He wondered too whether the gate could simply be left empty. How long did he think about it? Not long, I'd guess.

It took me a long time to get the knife down. My hand kept wanting to go back. I put the knife in my boot, walked down from the gatehouse, went to the stairs at the very bottom of the fortress and looked back once at that gate.

I didn't put it down because I was brave. I put it down because that blank patch was just too exactly the right size, as if it had been saved for me. I thought, if I cut my words there, I'm the thousandth fool on this wall, and I didn't want to be the thousandth. That's all it was.

In front of the stairs at the very bottom of the fortress was a big hall. The floor was covered in broken stone, stacked very neatly, every heap about the same height. I was halfway across when the first heap moved.

They stood up from the floor one after another, all pieced together from wall stones. Some had only a top half, and some had only two arms. I had just put that knife down and my hands were still shaking. I didn't want to fight. Nobody asked me what I wanted.

The first punch came and I blocked it with my shield, and the shield made a noise like it was about to split. I ran sideways, got behind a pillar and hit one with fire. It glowed red. I hit it with ice and it cracked. I drove my sword into the crack and levered, and half its body fell off, and the other half kept walking. I ran, burned, froze, levered. Ran, burned, froze, levered. By the sixth one the heat in me was used up, and all my hand could produce was a few sparks. The seventh caught my foot, hung me upside down and swung me at the floor.

My face hit the stone. Second swing, my shoulder. As it lifted me for the third, I saw from up in the air that its wrist had an old crack in it, left by someone else a long time ago. I drove my sword in and hung my whole weight from it. The hand snapped off, and I fell to the floor along with it.

I crawled out of that stone hand and crawled toward the stairs. They couldn't follow. They couldn't leave that hall. The moment my foot touched the first step they all stopped and collapsed back into heaps of broken stone, every heap about the same height.

I looked at them. Another lot who couldn't leave their door.

Beside the stairs was a chest under a thick layer of dust. It held potions and some gold, and a sheet of paper. The handwriting was calm and very small.

In there? Where was this person standing to hear that? Had they reached the bottom? If they had, how did the paper get back up to a chest on Floor Twenty-Four?

A woman. I looked up at the dark stairs and kept going down. That day the number on my sleeve was five thousand nine hundred and thirty.

## Floor Twenty-Five

You left early today. You said you had to go and see a ewe that was about to lamb. I'll write this floor first and leave it here. Come and read it tomorrow. This one is longer.

When you come back, don't stand at the edge of the Well looking down. I tell you that every day, and every day you nod.

The stairs here move.

There's no earthquake. A whole flight turns slowly to the left, and when you get to the bottom you find the exit isn't in the direction you saw it a moment ago. The walls and bridges were all crooked, and a corridor would turn into a ceiling while you were walking along it. There was white silk everywhere, some strands thicker than my arm, hung with translucent eggs that had something moving inside. Some of the eggs had broken, and what crawled out was what I had been killing all the way from Floor Fourteen.

Now at last I knew whose children they were.

There was a room here that must once have been very fine, with heavy curtains. Now it was full of cobwebs, hanging layer on layer from the ceiling to the floor, and the fire on my sword lit only one step ahead of me.

There was a sound behind me.

She was huge, bigger than I drew her. Above the waist she was a woman, of no age I could tell, with a ring of spikes growing from her head that might once have been a crown. Her skin was grey-white and her eyes were green. Below the waist she was an enormous spider, purple-black and covered in spikes, standing on the web on eight legs, each leg tipped with green light, and dragging a belly bigger than a carriage. The web moved, and all the corridors in the place moved with it. I came to understand that she had woven these corridors herself, and that she had never stopped weaving.

She didn't look at my face. She looked at the fire on my sword.

Then she screamed.

I don't much want to talk about this fight, but I promised not to hide things from you.

She wasn't like the captain or my master. She fought with no method at all, and in great pain, like someone who hadn't been this angry in a very long time.

She opened her mouth and a stream of green venom shot at me. I dodged most of it, and a little splashed my left arm. The leather smoked on the spot, the skin under it began to blister, and the whole arm felt like a hundred needles were going into it. I raised my shield again, and its face had already gone soft. Silk shot at me from every side. One strand caught my foot and one caught my sword. I pulled hard and it wouldn't break. The harder I pulled, the tighter it got.

The eggs burst one after another.

The things that came out were as big as my palm, dozens at a time. They swarmed up my legs and into my clothes, worked into the seams of the leather and bit, one mouthful after another. My sword was stuck in the silk, so I used my hands, pulling them off and crushing them until my hands ran with green pulp. One got into my ear. I dug at it like a madwoman and tore it out along with a piece of my earlobe.

That was when she came down on me.

A body that size, and it could jump. Her shadow covered me all at once. Her eight legs landed in a ring around me, and one went through my thigh and pinned me to the floor. She lowered her head to look at me, her face an arm's length from mine. It must have been a very beautiful face once. She opened her mouth, and it was full of venom.

I woke screaming in the stone ring, clawing at myself, and I had torn my skin open before I realised there were no bugs on me. The old peddler's face showed nothing. Sixth stroke.

The second time, I tied my leather on tight and bound my neck and cuffs with rope. I left her alone at first and went for the eggs. I used fire. The webs caught at once, the fire ran along the strands, and the whole sheet went up with a whoosh. Hundreds of eggs burst in the flames. I still remember that sound and that smell.

She screamed. This scream was different. Those were her children, even if they didn't know her.

She swept one leg at me and kicked me flying, shield and all. I smashed through a door and fell into the corridor below. Now the maze began to move. The corridor I had fallen into slowly turned over, the floor became a wall, and I slid down it. By the time I had climbed back to her room, the way had changed three times. Her leg came down through the ceiling, went through my stomach and lifted me into the air.

Seventh stroke. I hadn't even had time to drink a potion.

The third time I stood in the stone ring and first drank everything I had. Then I sat down and thought about my master.

When I was eleven there was a stretch when I lost to Big Lad every time. I watched his sword, and wherever it went I blocked, and every time I was half a step late. My master watched for a few days, then called me over. "You keep watching his sword," he said. "The sword is the last thing that moves. Watch him. What he's afraid of, what he's protecting, which foot moves first when he's tired, what his hands do when he's in a hurry. Watch with your heart." I said a heart doesn't have eyes. He rapped me on the head with his knuckles and told me to go and grow some.

After that I started beating Big Lad. Every time, before he swung, he took a breath first, because he was afraid of getting hurt and had to work up his nerve. I waited for the breath.

This time I went into the room without a light. The second time, her children had all come swarming toward the fire. They crawled toward anything that shone or moved. I damped the fire on my sword as low as it would go, until it was only a little warmth in my palm, felt my way to the middle of the room in the dark, and stood still.

I couldn't see anything. All I could do was listen, and feel with the soles of my feet. The web was moving. The strands under me tightened and slackened one after another. She was walking on the web, and every step came through the silk into my feet. She was circling me. Her children were crawling too, very lightly, a dry rustling, and they couldn't find me.

I didn't listen to her feet. I listened to her. When she was angry, the silk pulled fast and hard, jerk after jerk, like someone yanking on it. She went round the room again and again, and each time she reached one particular place, the silk went slow and light, as if she were afraid of waking something. Past that place, the yanking began again. She never spat venom that way and never let her children crawl that way. I heard her stamp the ones that did, one after another.

She was protecting something.

I walked toward it. She noticed and screamed. The whole web went taut at once, and a leg came stabbing out of the dark and went through my shoulder. I didn't stop. I dragged that arm along, got to the wall, and reached out.

It was cloth. I thought it was a curtain, but curtains are coarse, and this was tightly woven. I could feel patterns in it, line after line. In one place it was especially thin, so thin I could feel the warp and weft beneath, worn down by a hand touching it for years and years.

I turned round and stood with my back against it.

I heard her spring at me. The web jolted in midair as she wrenched herself aside. Her venom came halfway out and she swallowed it back, choking so hard the whole web shook. She didn't dare spit at this wall. She circled me, stabbing with her legs, very carefully, holding back on every one. I listened to the silk, and whenever it pulled tight I cut. When her children crawled up to my feet, she stamped them dead.

That's how I fought her, and I'm not proud of it. With my back against a piece of cloth, I cut off three of her legs. I didn't yet know what was woven on it.

I've done many ugly things in my life. That's the one I have thought about longest.

Near the end she stopped caring. She came at me like a mad thing and one leg swept me away. I flew and smashed through half a wall, and lay on the floor with all my ribs broken and my sword still in my hand. The fire on it flared up by itself, and I had no strength left to damp it. I watched her come, towering over me. First she glanced at that wall. Then she saw my face and stopped.

It was only for an instant.

I don't know what she saw. Maybe it was the red hair. Maybe it was only that I wasn't dressed like someone from the court. She just stopped for a moment, like a person who gets to a door and suddenly forgets what she came for.

I drove my sword upward into her chest.

The webs began to slacken. The silk in the room snapped strand by strand, the stairs stopped and the corridors stopped moving. She opened her mouth and cried out. Not to me. To that wall. "If I hadn't asked that day—" She didn't finish. She went down on her knees. Her eight legs slowly folded in until she had curled herself into a very small ball, with her face buried inside.

I propped myself up on my sword and sat, made the fire on it burn bright, and for the first time saw clearly what had been at my back.

All right. This is Floor Twenty-Five, and I made you a promise.

My name is Aisling.

It's nothing special as names go. Where we live, every third girl is called that. There were four in my village, and my mother's sister was one of them. The Order had two, and one of them was the cook. Go ask the old people over the mountain, and every one of them had an aunt called Aisling. Nobody can tell you why everyone uses it. It sounds nice, and it's an old name from around here.

That name is a thousand years old. It went up from this place and was passed along for a thousand years, and on the way the spelling changed and so did the sound. Aislinn became Aisling. Nobody knew whose it had been in the first place.

My aunt had that name, the one I kept the flies off in the barn. The cook had it too. She made the Order's porridge for forty years, and whenever someone called "Aisling," both of us looked round. To tell us apart, Mavis called her "the big one" and me "the red one."

I once asked my master what my name meant. He was sharpening his sword. He thought for a moment and said it didn't mean anything.

He probably didn't know. Nobody did. The Order passed down the way she stood for a thousand years, and the mothers in the villages passed down her name for a thousand years, and none of them knew what they were passing along.

That's why I told you that until I got here, I didn't know what my name meant.

Later, on Floor Twenty-Eight, I read what she had cut into the wall: **Let me not end. If I end, none will remember her name.** I hadn't read it yet that day in her room, but I already understood. This woman turned herself into that thing and lived for nearly a thousand years because she was afraid no one would remember this name.
