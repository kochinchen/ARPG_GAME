# Book Five: She Who Waits

Floors 21 to 25

## Floor Twenty-One

Yesterday, when you finished reading Floor Twenty, you said nothing and drove the sheep home. Today you brought me a boiled egg. I ate it. Thank you.

There was nobody alive in the fortress. There were only some things in armour with our crest, still walking their old patrol routes.

My right hand healed quickly, and three days later it could hold a sword. The broken fingers straightened themselves one at a time, and it didn't hurt at all. The back of the hand grew a dark patch of hard skin that joined up with the one on my palm. I didn't want to look at it, so I pulled the glove up higher.

I first ran into a patrol at a corner of the inner keep. Four things in full armour, marching in step, turning at the same angle. The one in front saw me and raised a hand, and the other three stopped at the same moment. A very dry voice came out of the helmet and asked the question I had answered every night since I was seven.

"Who goes there? Password."

My hand was already on my sword, but when I opened my mouth the password came out by itself: "No one stands down until the Well is quiet." They use the same one up on the mountain. It hasn't changed in a thousand years.

The one in front lowered his hand, stepped aside and saluted me. It was the Ward-hall salute, the elbow set just right. The four of them marched past me and carried on with their patrol, very neat and tidy.

The sweat on me went cold. They didn't know there was no one left here. They didn't know the person in front of them had just killed their guardian. All they knew was that the password was correct.

After that, every time I met them I gave the password and they let me through. I didn't kill a single one. If anyone asks what I have feared most in my life, I won't say those eyes, and I won't say that mountain. I'll say those four saluting me.

The things in the fortress that really did attack me were the ones that had crawled in later: jailers dragging chains, golems stacked up from the stones of the walls, and the little things that hatched out of those egg sacs. They didn't know any password. Fighting them was almost a relief.

The fortress had the five halls too, like the one on the mountain, only bigger. My master and I both came from the Ward-hall. I walked into the Ward-hall yard. The wooden posts stood there, and the ground had hollows worn by thousands of pairs of feet, exactly like up on the mountain. Without meaning to, I went and stood in front of the third post, which was my place when I was small.

The rule of the hall hung over the door. I memorised it as a child.

::lore.f21

"The Ward-hall was founded in the name of her who went first." I recited that for fifteen years and no one ever told me who she was. I did ask. The instructor said it was a long time ago, and I didn't ask again.

The second half was different.

The copy in the great hall on the mountain ends at "It hath one lesson: endure." I remember very clearly, because I once traced it letter by letter. This copy was older, and it had one more sentence.

"We do not teach what it costeth."

Somebody left that out when they made the copy. I suppose they felt it wasn't quite right for a place where children would see it every day.

A cupboard in the Ward-hall held a small locked box of old notes that guardians had used. I smashed the lock. Worms had eaten most of what was inside, and only one sheet could still be read. It had four different hands on it, crammed together, filling every inch of blank space. The first three parts had rotted past reading. Only the smallest line, at the very bottom, was clear.

::note.p07

I turned the page over. The back was full too.

The papers in my coat had grown into a thick stack. I put this one with them, and I nearly added a line in the margin: I am the fifth.

But he had said it. There is no more room.

## Floor Twenty-Two

The Grand Master's chamber was at the very top of the fortress, and the door had been sealed from outside with wax. The wax was newer than I expected, and there was that seal on it again.

There wasn't much inside: one big table, two chairs, an iron cabinet. I smashed the cabinet open. It was full of letters between one Grand Master and another, mostly arguing about pay. I read them one after another, since I had nothing better to do.

At the very back was a letter wrapped in black cloth. The wax on it was very old. It had been cracked, and someone had carefully pressed it back together.

::lore.f22

When I finished I put the letter down on the table.

The window of this room looks out over the training ground. Standing here and looking down, you can see the children lined up in rows, holding up their wooden swords. Some Grand Master stood at this window with this letter in hand, looking at those children, and thought: Who then would go?

I picked up a chair and started smashing the table.

It was a very thick table, but I was far too strong, and it split in three blows. After the table I smashed the cabinet. When the cabinet was in pieces I kicked those letters about pay all over the room. Then I stood in the middle of it, breathing hard. Nobody came to shout at me. Nobody came to look. The person who wrote that letter had been dead for centuries.

All those empty nails in the great hall on the mountain, and every one of those nameplates. "Because they are still on watch."

They had always known.

The old priest on Floor Ten had already told me: "no guardian was ever a hero. We were offerings, every one." That calm handwriting on Floor Eleven had told me too: "The Order lied." Every paper I had picked up on the way said the same thing. And still I kept going down, thinking it was about other people, and that I would be different.

Every guardian thought that.

Did my master read this letter? I'd guess he did. I think he was the one who pressed that wax back together.

"Who then would go?"

You're smarter than I am. You know the answer. The answer is no one.

So think about this. Suppose they had nailed this letter to the door of the great hall back then, where every child could read it, and no one was ever willing to go again. What would have happened?

I don't know either. Maybe the Well would have swallowed everything sooner. Maybe someone would have come up with another way. For a thousand years nobody has known, because nobody has ever tried it.

I folded the letter and put it inside my coat, against my body.

I decided to write this book that day, though I didn't know it yet.

## Floor Twenty-Three

Floor Twenty-Three was living quarters, room after room, with nameplates on the doors. A lot of the plates were blank. I wasn't looking for anything.

That's a lie. I was looking.

I walked for a whole day, and in the last room at the end of the corridor, his name was cut into the plate on the door. Ten years ago he passed through here, and he must have spent a night in this room.

I won't tell you his name. It's his.

The room was very small and the bed was neatly made. There was a nail in the wall with a wooden sword on it.

It wasn't mine. Mine is still up on the mountain. This one was shorter, made for a child smaller than I had been. He never said he had taught anyone else. Or it could have been his own when he was small. I took it down and tried the grip. It was too short for me, and the handle was worn shiny.

There was a letter on the table, with no name on the envelope. It had been sealed and opened, opened and sealed, so many times that the edges had frayed.

I knew I should wait a bit before reading it. I couldn't wait.

::lore.f23

I read it standing. Then I sat down and read it again.

His handwriting was always ugly. He pressed too hard, and every stroke looked like a sword cut. This letter was uglier than usual. In a few places he had blacked out words so heavily that even under the lamp I couldn't tell what they had been.

"If this reach thee, I have failed."

He wrote it, sealed it, opened it, read it, sealed it again.

He never sent it.

There was nowhere to send it from, either. He wrote it after he came down, when the stairs back up were long gone, and of course he knew that. At a little table on Floor Twenty-Three, he wrote to a twelve-year-old who had told him "Then don't come back," telling her to be a smith, a farmer, anything. Then he sealed the letter, left it on the table, went to the gate and stood there.

**The Order will call it cowardice. Let them.**

I put my face in my hands.

He had known all along. He had read that secret letter. He knew that everyone failed, and he knew that sooner or later they would send me. He knew it when he was teaching me to hold a wooden sword. He hit my hand with that stick until it swelled, until I let go. Let go. Only then did it occur to me that this was what he had been teaching me for those five years. To let go.

I did not become a smith. I did not become a farmer. I walked down twenty-three floors on my own, to his table, and only then did I read this letter.

I have thought about this for decades. I'll tell you one thing now, and don't tell anyone else: that letter did reach me in the end.

A little late, that's all.

That night I slept in his bed, and I didn't tie my hand to my sword. The singing from below went on all night, and it called me in his voice. I lay still with that short wooden sword in my hand, and I didn't take one step.

The next morning I hung the wooden sword back on its nail and put the letter inside my coat.

## Floor Twenty-Four

The way down was inside the gatehouse, so I had to walk back to the gate.

I climbed up into the gatehouse. The place where he stood for ten years was right below. Looking out from here you saw what he had been looking at all that time: the bridge, the mist, and nothing in the mist. The innermost wall of the gatehouse was covered in carving.

I had taken it for a pattern.

Up close it was words, big and small, new and old, layer on layer, centuries of them. I raised the lamp and found the newest lines. The writing was ugly. Every stroke looked like a sword cut.

::lore.f24

My hand was shaking, so I had to put the lamp down.

Higher up was an older line: "None standeth here; I take it up. I can bear it." Above that: "The gate is empty. I come." Above that, so old it was almost worn smooth, you could still make out two words: "Only I." Every one of them thought they were the only one. Every one of them came to this wall and found that the people before them had written the same thing. And then they cut their own words into it anyway. My master finished with "I find, upon this very wall," and went downstairs to stand his post.

I honestly don't know what to do with that man.

The next part is what I least want to tell you.

I looked out the window. The gate was empty. It was empty because I had killed him. The smell in the mist was stronger than before. With nobody standing here, what would happen to this ring? Would the thing below come pouring up through here? There were still villages up there. Every ring on the way down had someone holding it, the captain, the priest, the forgemaster, my master, and I had knocked them down one by one. I had got this far by tearing down one wall after another. Who was going to patch them?

My hand had gone very steady. I wasn't tired at all. I had the strength of eight or nine stones in me. I thought, I can do this. There's a place for me here. I can hold it. I alone can hold it.

When I came to, I was holding the little knife from the pack on Floor Three, with its point already against the wall. I had no memory of picking it up.

There was still a blank patch under his line, exactly the right size.

It was very quiet in my ears. No singing and no voice of his. It didn't have to say anything.

**This picture is a guess.** Ten years ago he stood here too, with a knife too, having just sealed that letter. He wondered too whether the gate could simply be left empty. How long did he think about it? Not long, I'd guess.

It took me a long time to get the knife down. My hand kept wanting to go back. I put the knife in my boot, walked down from the gatehouse, went to the stairs at the very bottom of the fortress and looked back once at that gate.

I didn't put it down because I was brave. I put it down because that blank patch was just too exactly the right size, as if it had been saved for me. I thought, if I cut my words there, I'm the thousandth fool on this wall, and I didn't want to be the thousandth. That's all it was.

Beside the stairs was a chest under a thick layer of dust. It held potions and some gold, and a sheet of paper. The handwriting was calm and very small.

::note.p06

In there? Where was this person standing to hear that? Had they reached the bottom? If they had, how did the paper get back up to a chest on Floor Twenty-Four?

Later I worked it out. This paper had been carried up like everything else, like that meat between your teeth I told you about.

A woman. I looked up at the dark stairs and kept going down. That day the number on my sleeve was five thousand nine hundred and thirty.

## Floor Twenty-Five

The stairs here move.

There's no earthquake. A whole flight turns slowly to the left, and when you get to the bottom you find the exit isn't in the direction you saw it a moment ago. The walls and bridges were all crooked, and a corridor would turn into a ceiling while you were walking along it. There was white silk everywhere, some strands thicker than my arm, hung with translucent eggs that had something moving inside. Some of the eggs had broken, and what crawled out was what I had been killing all the way from Floor Fourteen.

Now at last I knew whose children they were.

There was a room here that must once have been very fine, with heavy curtains. Now it was full of cobwebs, except for one tapestry on the wall that nothing had covered. The webs went around it.

::lore.f25

I reached out and gently touched the worn place. It was the wooden sword in the little girl's hand, and only there had the thread worn down far enough to show the backing.

There was a sound behind me.

She was huge, bigger than I drew her. Above the waist she was a woman, of no age I could tell, with a ring of spikes growing from her head that might once have been a crown. Below the waist she was an enormous spider, standing on the web on eight legs. The web moved, and all the corridors in the place moved with it. I came to understand that she had woven these corridors herself, and that she had never stopped weaving.

She didn't look at my face. She looked at my hand, the one that had just touched the tapestry.

Then she screamed.

I don't much want to talk about this fight. She wasn't like the captain or my master. She fought with no method at all, and in great pain, like someone who hadn't been this angry in a very long time. The venom she spat could burn holes in stone. Webs shot in from every side, and once they wrapped you, you couldn't move. The eggs burst one after another and her children poured out and swarmed up my legs. I cut them down and they didn't try to dodge. She didn't look at them either. When she charged, she trampled them.

Her legs were very long, each one like a long spear. I used every move I had, fire and ice and even the one I'd used on my master, and still one of those legs kicked me flying, and I smashed through half a wall. I lay on the floor and watched her come. First she looked at my hand. Then she saw my face and stopped.

It was only for an instant.

I don't know what she saw. Maybe it was the red hair. Maybe it was only that I wasn't dressed like someone from the court. She just stopped for a moment, like a person who gets to a door and suddenly forgets what she came for.

I drove my sword upward into her chest.

The webs began to slacken. The silk in the room snapped strand by strand, the stairs stopped and the corridors stopped moving. She went down on her knees. Her eight legs slowly folded in until she had curled herself into a very small ball, with her face buried inside.

The white smoke drifted very slowly, very thin. It didn't come toward me. It drifted over to the tapestry and stopped at the worn place.

::soul.f25#0

It was a very ordinary voice, a tired old woman with good manners.

::soul.f25#1

I was sitting on the floor with one hand pressed to my ribs, and I couldn't say a word.

The smoke turned toward me.

::soul.f25#2

**Her name is Aislinn.**

I opened my mouth and nothing came out.

All right. This is Floor Twenty-Five, and I made you a promise.

My name is Aisling.

It's nothing special as names go. Where we live, every third girl is called that. There were four in my village, and my mother's sister was one of them. The Order had two, and one of them was the cook. Go ask the old people over the mountain, and every one of them had an aunt called Aisling. Nobody can tell you why everyone uses it. It sounds nice, and it's an old name from around here.

That name is a thousand years old. It went up from this place and was passed along for a thousand years, and on the way the spelling changed and so did the sound. Aislinn became Aisling. Nobody knew whose it had been in the first place.

That's why I told you that until I got here, I didn't know what my name meant.

Later, on Floor Twenty-Eight, I read what she had cut into the wall: **Let me not end. If I end, none will remember her name.** I hadn't read it yet that day in her room, but I already understood. This woman turned herself into that thing and lived for nearly a thousand years because she was afraid no one would remember this name.

I jumped up. My broken ribs hurt so much I nearly went down again. I shouted at that wisp of smoke:

"People remember! It's everywhere! There's one in every village up there! I'm—"

The smoke was already gone.

The two girls in the tapestry looked at me. The one with the wooden sword had hair so faded you couldn't tell what colour it used to be.

She hadn't heard.

She waited a thousand years, and she missed it by one sentence.

I took the tapestry down from the wall, rolled it up and tied it on my back. It was very light. I also made a note of what she said: "tell her I never left." I was going to deliver that for her, whatever it took.
